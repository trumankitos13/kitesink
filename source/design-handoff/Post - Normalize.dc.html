Why I normalize every set I've ever logged

Raw tonnage lies. The only honest way to compare a brutal week to an easy one is to put everything on the same scale.

T
Truman Kitos
Building Vantix · @tkitos
normalized volume · arbitrary units · 24-week view

For years I tracked tonnage — sets times reps times load — and quietly trusted it. It felt like data. It looked like progress when the number went up. But tonnage is a liar, and it took me an embarrassingly long time to catch it in the act.

The problem is that a set of twenty light reps and a near-maximal triple can produce identical tonnage while asking completely different things of your body. One is a conversation; the other is a confrontation. Adding them together and calling the sum "volume" throws away the only information that mattered.

The unit problem

What I actually wanted was a single number that meant the same thing on a deload week as it did during a brutal peak. That means normalization: expressing every set relative to a stable reference rather than in raw kilograms. Once each set is scored against an estimate of your current capability, a hard week and an easy week finally live on the same axis.

It sounds academic until you see your own history redrawn. Weeks I'd remembered as productive turned out to be junk volume. A stretch I'd written off as "just maintenance" was quietly the most effective block of the year. The data hadn't changed — only the scale had — and the scale was the whole story.

You can't manage what you measure wrong. Most of lifting is measuring the right thing and then having the patience to believe it.

What normalization changed

Three things, immediately. I stopped chasing the tonnage high. I started trusting deloads, because the normalized line made it obvious how much they preserved. And I began to see fatigue as a number I was spending, not a vague feeling I was enduring.

This is the idea Vantix is built around. Not more charts — fewer, truer ones. A single normalized view that tells you whether this week actually moved you forward, or just made you tired. Everything else in the app is downstream of that one decision.

Note

If you only change one thing about how you track training this year, make it this: stop adding kilograms together. Score the effort, not the iron.

The honest scale is rarely the flattering one. But it's the only one worth keeping for forty years — which, if longevity is the real game, is exactly how long you're playing.
