# Neighbouring rights: KiteSink music field guide

What the recording earns on its own: SoundExchange, PPL and performer shares. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, who it is for, where it applies, how well sourced it is, and its sources.
Grades: Sourced (law or official) and Directional (reported or industry practice).

## Guidance

### Non-interactive services license by statute; interactive services license from the owner

**Section 114 gives non-interactive digital services a statutory licence at rates the Judges set. An interactive service needs a licence from the recording owner.**

A service is interactive when a listener can receive a programme made for them, or a chosen recording on request (section 114(j)(7)). The statutory licence in section 114(d)(2) is for non-interactive services that meet its conditions. For plays licensed outside it, section 114(g)(1) says featured and non-featured artists are paid by the owner under their own contracts, and section 114(d)(3) limits how long an exclusive licence to an interactive service may run.

- Check whether listeners can pick tracks before assuming statutory rates apply.

### The Judges name the collective for the statutory licence, and for now it is SoundExchange

**The Judges designate the collective, and the 2026 commercial broadcaster rule names SoundExchange for the current rate period. Services pay it and send play reports.**

Section 114(g)(2) gives the dividing of statutory licence receipts to a nonprofit collective chosen by the Judges. The 2026 commercial broadcaster rule defines that collective as SoundExchange for the present rate period. SoundExchange says services pay it together with playlists of what they played, and that it passes the money on to artists and owners. The rule leaves out plays the service licensed directly from the owner.

- Expect statutory licence money from SoundExchange, not from the service.

### A "featuring" credit splits the featured share equally, and each artist signs a letter

**SoundExchange treats "Artist A featuring Artist B" as a collaboration and divides the featured-artist share 50/50, unless both artists tell it another split.**

SoundExchange gives "Artist A featuring Artist B" as its example. The 45 percent featured-artist share in section 114(g)(2)(D) is divided between the artists first. SoundExchange pays no producer off the top, so a producer to be paid from both parts needs a letter of direction from each artist, and one letter cannot cover recordings by different featured artists.

- Tell SoundExchange the agreed split if it is not equal.
- Producers: get a letter from each featured artist you are to be paid by.

### Non-featured musicians and vocalists are paid through funds, not directly by SoundExchange

**Session musicians and backing vocalists get their statutory share through two escrow funds, not from SoundExchange, whether or not they belong to a union.**

Section 114(g)(2) puts 2.5 percent for non-featured musicians and 2.5 percent for non-featured vocalists into escrow accounts, each run by an independent administrator. Recording owners appoint the musicians' one jointly with the American Federation of Musicians, and the vocalists' one jointly with AFTRA; a successor body can take either union's place. The statute pays those who performed, members or not. SoundExchange says it does not pay non-featured artists directly.

- Ask the fund administrator, not SoundExchange, about a session or backing-vocal share.

### Pre-1972 recordings are partly inside federal law, including the statutory licences

**The Copyright Office says Title II of the Music Modernization Act brought recordings fixed before 15 February 1972 partly into federal law, with remedies and a licensing regime.**

The Copyright Office says the Classics Protection and Access Act added a chapter 14 to Title 17. For non-interactive digital streaming such as web radio, satellite services and cable TV music channels, it brings in a statutory licensing scheme modelled on the one for later recordings. Existing limits such as fair use also apply. The pages used here do not describe how SoundExchange registers or pays pre-1972 owners.

- Read the Office's Title II page before relying on any pre-1972 remedy.

### Pre-1972 owners usually file a schedule first, and remedies start 90 days after indexing

**To recover statutory damages or attorneys' fees for a pre-1972 recording, owners must typically file a schedule with the Copyright Office. Only later uses count.**

The Office says the schedule stands in place of the usual registration requirement, and that remedies apply only to uses more than 90 days after the schedule is indexed. Where a transmitting service filed a notice of contact information, the owner must also send it a notice that it is not authorised to use the recording, identified in a conforming schedule. The Office keeps a database of indexed schedules. A music lawyer should review any claim before a notice goes out.

- Check the Office schedule database before assuming a recording is listed.

### Web VI: no final rule for section 380.10 webcasters found as of 20 November 2026

**Four Web VI final rules for 2026 to 2030 were published on 10 March 2026. None for the webcasters in 37 CFR 380.10 was found as of 20 November 2026.**

Web V set rates for 2021 to 2025. The four Web VI rules cover noncommercial educational webcasters, Educational Media Foundation, commercial broadcasters and certain public radio stations; the CRB announced a rule for new subscription services at the same time. On 20 November 2026 the Federal Register, the CRB announcements page and the eCFR showed no final Web VI rule for commercial or noncommercial webcasters, and section 380.10 still printed 2025 rates. The last related CRB notice found set supplemental closing arguments for 13 February 2026.

- Before quoting a webcaster rate, check docket 23-CRB-0012-WR at the CRB and the current eCFR.

### Rome and the WPPT provide a payment right for broadcast use that countries may limit

**Both WIPO treaties provide one equitable payment when commercial recordings are broadcast or communicated to the public, and both let a country limit it or opt out.**

WIPO says that under the Rome Convention (1961) the user pays one equitable remuneration to performers, producers or both, and states are free not to apply it or to limit it. For the WPPT (1996), a party may restrict the right or, with a reservation, deny it, and other parties may then deny that party national treatment. The WPPT also gives an on-demand making-available right. Payment to a foreign performer therefore depends on the country and the society.

- Check each society's own rules instead of assuming a treaty guarantees payment.

### PPL pays a performer only for a 'qualifying performance'

**A UK performer claim has to pass a test based on where the performance took place or where the performer lives or is a citizen.**

PPL says a performance qualifies in one of two ways, by the country of performance or by the performer's country of residence or citizenship at the time, and in each case the country must be one that UK law treats as qualifying. PPL updates the list now and then; it covers all EU states plus many others. The PPL entry on the basics page says what happens to a share that does not qualify.

- Read PPL's Performer Qualification Rules to see whether your country and recording qualify.

### SoundExchange's view: some countries pay US creators less because of nationality

**SoundExchange's advocacy page says countries such as the UK, France and Japan treat US creators worse on nationality grounds, citing unpaid AM/FM play abroad as an example.**

The page is undated advocacy and frames the issue as a breach of national treatment. It also cites a 2020 European Court of Justice ruling and its own estimate of lost royalties; neither was checked for this guide. PPL's published test turns on where a performance took place or where the performer lived or was a citizen. Treat the page as SoundExchange's position, and check each society's current rules.

- Ask the society directly whether a US recording or performer qualifies.

### SoundExchange can collect abroad for members who sign an international mandate

**SoundExchange says members who complete an international mandate can have it collect neighbouring rights money abroad through its agreements with foreign societies.**

It says it holds over 100 agreements with at least 75 societies, covering 93 percent of the global neighbouring rights market; these are its own figures. A creator registers, becomes a member, picks territories and submits recordings through SoundExchange Direct. Its partner list includes PPL (UK), GVL (Germany), SCPP and ADAMI (France), PPCA (Australia) and Re:Sound (Canada). Whether a society pays a given performer is still that society's rule.

- Ask which territories and roles the mandate covers, and have its terms reviewed before signing.

### GVL handles German neighbouring rights; its foreign service needs an assignment

**GVL is the German society for performers and sound recording producers. For performers, its international service needs an agreement that transfers international rights to GVL.**

GVL says more than 180,000 rights holders place neighbouring rights with it, and it reports upwards of 70 international agreements with foreign societies. It says performers report their contributions to productions in its portal, with three years from each distribution year, and that it passes on foreign money without deductions for international payments. Foreign societies run their own distributions, and GVL says it cannot influence their calculations.

- Have a music lawyer read the assignment terms before signing.

### Content ID is for owners with exclusive rights to a large body of material

**YouTube approves Content ID only for owners holding exclusive rights to a large catalogue of original material that is uploaded often, and who show a need.**

YouTube's help pages say eligibility depends on whether the material suits claiming through Content ID and on a need the owner can show, with evidence of the content the owner controls exclusively. An approved owner signs an agreement that limits references to exclusive content and states the territories if not worldwide. YouTube points others to tools such as the copyright complaint webform and the Enterprise Copyright Match Tool.

- Check the criteria on YouTube Help before applying for direct access.

## In order

### A letter of direction needs a signed form, a repertoire chart and a percentage

**SoundExchange asks for a signed form, a chart of the tracks covered with a percentage for each, and proof of any electronic signature. Allow at least two weeks.**

The percentage is a share of that featured artist's royalties on each track. SoundExchange says a letter can pay only creative participants such as producers, mixers and engineers, not labels, lenders or advance companies, and pays a company only if the participant solely owns it. Payment then follows SoundExchange's usual payment thresholds.

- Download the current form and chart from SoundExchange before drafting.
- Have a music lawyer review the percentage and any producer agreement.

## Common mistakes

### Mistake: treating a radio station's online stream like its broadcast

**Over-the-air play on a US AM or FM station is exempt from the recording's digital performance right. A broadcaster's stream under the statutory licence pays per play.**

Section 106(6) gives recordings a performance right only for digital audio transmissions, and section 114(d)(1)(A) exempts a nonsubscription broadcast transmission, which section 114(j)(3) ties to an FCC-licensed terrestrial station. The 2026 Web VI rule sets a per-performance rate for commercial broadcasters' nonsubscription streams under section 114, paid to SoundExchange. The claim on AM/FM radio covers the broadcast side.

- Count a station's stream as a digital service when you trace recording income.

### Mistake: reading a per-performance rate as what an artist is paid

**A rate such as $0.0028 is what a service owes for one performance. The collective then divides it between owners, featured artists and the non-featured funds.**

The rule counts each time any part of a recording is played to a listener, leaving out incidental uses and plays licensed directly from the owner. It credits 5 percent of payments to ephemeral copies and 95 percent to section 114. After reasonable costs under section 114(g)(3), section 114(g)(2) gives 50 percent to the owner, 45 percent to featured artists and 2.5 percent to each non-featured fund.

- Work out an artist share from the statutory split, not from the headline rate.

### Mistake: sending a full album, remaster or non-exclusive track as a Content ID reference

**References must be exclusive, distinct and given one per piece. YouTube lists full albums, remasters and non-exclusive licences as ineligible.**

YouTube says exclusive rights must cover the territories claimed. It names karaoke, remasters, sound-alikes, some dubbed content, Creative Commons and public domain recordings, and content licensed non-exclusively as ineligible. Full album recordings must be split into single songs. It says abuse or error can lead to disabled references, released claims or an ended partnership.

- Split releases into single-track references and confirm exclusivity by territory.

## How long federal remedies run for pre-1972 recordings

The Office says remedies last 95 years from the year of first publication, plus an additional period that depends on when the recording was first published.

| First published | Additional period beyond the 95-year term |
|---|---|
| Before 1923 | Ended 31 December 2021 |
| 1923 to 1946 | 5 years after the 95-year term |
| 1947 to 1956 | 15 years after the 95-year term |
| All other recordings fixed before 15 February 1972 | Ends 15 February 2067 |

## Recording income checklist

Tick what applies to you. Each register is separate, and a lawyer should read any agreement that assigns rights.

**In the US**

- SoundExchange registration done for every role you hold (see the registrations checklist)

- International mandate terms read, with the territories listed

## Terms

Defined on the glossary: https://kitesink.com/music/glossary.md

- **Artist, Performer and Sound Recording Owner (SoundExchange's terms)**: SoundExchange uses Artist for the name on the release, Performer for each person who makes up that Artist, and Sound Recording Owner for whoever owns the master, which is separate from owning the song.
- **Featured and non-featured performers**: A featured artist is the person or group most prominent on a track. Non-featured performers, such as session musicians and backing singers, are paid through a separate route.
