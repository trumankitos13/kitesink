# Music business claims, checked: KiteSink

Common things people say about royalties, rights and deals. Each has a verdict, what the answer rests on, and the source.
Verdicts: Holds up, Mostly holds, Depends, Overstated, Not supported.

## Royalty basics

### Does owning the master mean I own the song?

**Verdict: Not supported.** No. The song and the recording are two separate works with separate rights, and they can have different owners.

The Copyright Office treats a song (the music and lyrics) and a recording of someone performing it as two distinct works. Its circular on sound recordings says the copyright in a recording does not cover the underlying music or lyrics. The songwriter is the usual author of a song, and the featured performer and the producer are the usual authors of a recording, unless the work was made for hire. Read your agreements too, and have a music lawyer check any deal that moves ownership.

What this rests on: Copyright Office Circulars 50 and 56, as retrieved 20 November 2026 (US).

- Write down who owns the song and who owns the recording for each release

### Are featured artists paid 45% of US digital performance royalties?

**Verdict: Depends.** It depends on the money. For receipts under the US statutory licence, the law gives featured artists 45 percent, paid per recording. Other income follows contracts.

17 USC 114(g)(2) divides receipts from transmissions licensed under the statutory licence: 50 percent to the owner of the digital performance right, 45 percent to featured recording artists, and 2.5 percent each to escrow funds for non-featured musicians and vocalists. The collective may first deduct its reasonable costs, and an artist can send part of the share to a producer, mixer or engineer by letter of direction. For transmissions outside that licence, section 114(g)(1) points the artist to the recording owner and the artist's contract.

What this rests on: 17 USC 114(g)(1) to (g)(5), as retrieved 20 November 2026 (US).

- Read your recording contract for income outside the statutory licence

### Do I have to register my recording with the Copyright Office to have a copyright?

**Verdict: Not supported.** No. For a US sound recording made since 1978, protection starts when it is fixed. Registration is a separate, optional step with its own form, fee and deposit.

The Copyright Office's sound recordings circular says recordings created after 1 January 1978 are protected automatically once first fixed, and that neither registration nor publication is required. This answer rests on that circular and covers recordings only, not songs. Registering has other effects, and the Office changes its fees, so check its current pages before you apply.

What this rests on: Copyright Office Circular 56 for recordings; application steps in Circulars 50 and 56.

- Read the Office's circular for your type of work before applying

### Do recording owners get paid when a song plays on US AM/FM radio?

**Verdict: Not supported.** Not under US copyright law. Recording owners hold a performance right only for digital audio transmissions, and over-the-air radio broadcasts fall outside it.

17 USC 114(a) gives a sound recording owner only some of the section 106 rights and leaves out the general public performance right in clause (4). Clause (6) covers digital audio transmissions, and section 114(d)(1)(A) exempts nonsubscription broadcast transmissions, which (j)(3) defines as those of FCC-licensed terrestrial stations. Song owners do hold a performing right, usually licensed through a society. This is US law only: in the UK, for example, PPL pays recording owners and qualifying performers for broadcasts.

What this rests on: 17 USC 106(4), 106(6), 114(a), (d)(1)(A) and (j)(3), as retrieved 20 November 2026 (US); PPL for the UK example.

- Check each country's rules before expecting recording income from radio play

### Do non-US songwriters need to fill in Form W-8 BEN?

**Verdict: Depends.** It depends on the payer and your situation. The IRS says a foreign individual gives the form to the withholding agent or payer when asked for it.

The IRS page says a foreign individual who beneficially owns an amount that is subject to US withholding gives the form to whoever pays or withholds, when that party asks, whether or not a lower rate or an exemption is claimed. The form is for individuals; the page links a separate form for entities. It does not say which rate applies to royalties. This is not tax advice: ask a tax adviser how US withholding applies to you.

What this rests on: IRS page About Form W-8 BEN, as retrieved 20 November 2026 (US).

- Ask each payer which form it needs from you
- Ask a tax adviser how US withholding applies to you

## Mechanicals and the MLC

### Do I have to claim my unclaimed MLC royalties before April 2027?

**Verdict: Not supported.** No. April 2027 is when the MLC plans to start paying out remaining unclaimed accrued royalties by market share. It is a start date, not a cut-off for claims.

The MLC says it intends to begin in April 2027 with the earliest blanket usage, from January 2021, then to work through the remaining eligible 2021 royalties one month at a time. The pool is only money the MLC has not matched or paid out by then, so a song matched before its usage month comes up is paid in the normal way instead. These are the MLC's plans, so watch its dashboard, and register and match early.

What this rests on: MLC unclaimed-royalties page and its 30 September 2026 release; 17 USC 115(d)(3), as of November 2026.

- Register and match your songs well before your usage months come up
- Watch the MLC's unclaimed dashboard for updated schedules

### Does the MLC take a fee from my mechanical royalties?

**Verdict: Not supported.** No. The MLC says it deducts no fees from the mechanical royalties it pays out. Digital services fund its budget separately. Your own publisher or administrator may still keep a share.

The MLC's audits page says digital services fully fund its operating budget through an administrative assessment set by the Copyright Royalty Board, separate from the royalties they owe, and that it deducts no fees from royalties. It adds that rightsholders do not pay for its audits of services and that audit recoveries are passed on without costs taken out. The Copyright Office FAQ also names voluntary contributions from the services. This covers US digital mechanicals as the MLC describes them. A publisher or administrator may charge under its own contract.

What this rests on: MLC DSP audits page and Copyright Office MMA FAQ, US digital mechanicals, as retrieved 19 November 2026.

- Ask your publisher or administrator what share it keeps

### Does registering my song with the Copyright Office also register it with the MLC?

**Verdict: Not supported.** No. The Copyright Office says a copyright registration does not replace an MLC registration. You, or your publisher or administrator, still have to register the song with the MLC.

The Copyright Office FAQ says owners who already hold a copyright registration must still sign up with the MLC to receive blanket-license digital mechanicals. It also says a song need not be registered with the Office to be eligible for those royalties. For statutory royalties on some physical uses, such as CDs and vinyl, the song must be in the Office's records. Registration has other effects in copyright law that this answer does not cover.

What this rests on: Copyright Office Music Modernization Act FAQ and overview page, as retrieved 19 November 2026.

- Search the MLC's public database, then claim or register the song
- Ask your publisher or administrator whether it handles the MLC

### Does a streaming service pay a fixed mechanical rate per stream?

**Verdict: Not supported.** No. US law sets no per-stream amount for mechanicals. It sets a formula that produces a pool for each service offering, which is then divided across plays.

37 CFR 385.21 takes, for most offerings, the greater of a share of service revenue and a content-cost amount, subtracts performance royalties the service pays, applies any per-subscriber floor, then divides the pool by plays. What one stream earns therefore depends on the service, the type of offering and its results. This covers interactive streaming and limited downloads in the US under the MLC's blanket license, for the rate period the regulation states.

What this rests on: 37 CFR 385.21(b), Subpart C, as retrieved 19 November 2026.

- Read the offering and period on your statement before comparing per-stream figures

Topics: Mechanicals, Royalties · Intermediate · Sourced (law) · For: songwriters, artists, publishers and labels, administrators · Where: US · As of 20 November 2026 · Sources: [37 CFR 385.21](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/385.21)

### Is 13.1 cents the streaming mechanical rate?

**Verdict: Not supported.** No. 13.1 cents is the 2026 US rate per work for physical copies and permanent downloads. Streaming mechanicals come from a formula, not that figure.

37 CFR 385.11 sets the 2026 rate for physical copies and permanent downloads as 13.1 cents per work, or, if larger, 2.52 cents for each minute or part of one. The Judges' 2026 cost-of-living notice applies those adjusted figures from 1 January to 31 December 2026. Streaming falls under 385.21 and has no fixed per-play amount. A 2027 figure is not given here.

What this rests on: 37 CFR 385.11(a)(1) and 385.21; Federal Register notice of 1 December 2025, as of November 2026.

- Use the 13.1 cent rate only for copies and downloads in 2026

### Must publishers pay songwriters a share of unclaimed MLC money they receive?

**Verdict: Holds up.** Yes. A copyright owner that receives an MLC unclaimed distribution must pay or credit its songwriters by reported usage, never less than half of what it receives for their works.

17 USC 115(d)(3)(J)(iv) covers songwriters whose works the owner licenses or administers for covered activities, and applies despite any agreement to the contrary. The payment is allocated by each song's usage as reported by digital services in the periods the distribution covers, and a songwriter's amount cannot be under 50 percent of what the owner received for that songwriter's works. Contract terms govern the rest. The rule covers distributions of unclaimed accrued royalties and interest, not all publishing income. A music lawyer should read any agreement that matters.

What this rests on: 17 USC 115(d)(3)(J)(iv), as retrieved 19 November 2026.

- Ask your publisher how it will credit these payments to you

## Unclaimed royalties

### Is a "black box" the same as unclaimed royalties?

**Verdict: Depends.** Not exactly. Black box is an informal nickname for money no one has allocated. The MLC uses its own terms, and each collector defines its own.

Industry use of black box varies, and collectives use names such as suspense account for money held while works are unidentified. The MLC counts both unmatched and unclaimed royalties inside what it calls unclaimed accrued royalties. In US law, unmatched means the owner is not yet known or found. Read the collector's own definition before comparing figures.

What this rests on: Industry nickname with no official definition; MLC unclaimed page, 17 USC 115 and the 2020 Copyright Office survey, as of November 2026.

- Use each collector's own term when you search for money held for you

### Does registering with my PRO also get me SoundExchange or MLC money?

**Verdict: Not supported.** No. A performing rights society, the MLC and SoundExchange each keep their own accounts and pay only what they collect. Each needs its own registration.

SoundExchange says artists and recording owners must register with it to receive US digital performance royalties. The MLC's Missing Member Lookup lists rightsholders who have not joined but may be owed US digital audio mechanicals. A PRO registration covers neither. Each kind of income has its own register, so a missing one can leave money unpaid.

What this rests on: SoundExchange artists page and MLC Missing Member Lookup, as retrieved 19 to 20 November 2026.

- List every collector for each kind of income you earn
- Search the MLC and SoundExchange unregistered lists for your names

### Do collecting societies worldwide share out unclaimed money by market share?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Not as a rule. US law tells the MLC to use market share. A 2020 survey of collectives found most of those taking part did not use owner market share for such money.

The survey was written for the Copyright Office from the answers of collectives whose names were kept confidential, gathered between January and April 2020. It says that, for works nobody has identified or claimed, the participants usually share the money out without weighing each owner's market share. Its list of methods includes catalogue market share and several pro rata splits among owners already paid. The survey is not a count of every society, and practice varies by society and type of income, so ask your own.

What this rests on: Butler survey of collective rights management, April 2020 (participating collectives only); 17 USC 115(d)(3)(J).

- Ask each society abroad how it shares out income it cannot allocate
