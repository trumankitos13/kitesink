# Producer royalties: KiteSink music field guide

Points, letters of direction and SoundExchange. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, who it is for, where it applies, how well sourced it is, and its sources.
Grades: Sourced (law or official) and Directional (reported or industry practice).

## Guidance

### Points are a contract term, and this guide states no standard figure

**How many points a producer gets, what they are measured on and when they are paid are all set by the producer's agreement. No law or collective sets them.**

A producer may be paid by a label, an artist or a company, and each agreement says what income counts and which costs come off first. Whether the royalty is paid only after the label has recovered its costs (recoupment) is also a contract matter. Because the terms vary, this guide gives no typical number. A music lawyer should review the agreement.

- Get the points, what they are measured on and the payment timing into a signed written agreement.
- Have a music lawyer review the agreement before you agree to it.

### Title III of the Music Modernization Act made letters of direction a statutory policy

**Section 114(g)(5) requires the designated collective to adopt a policy for accepting letters of direction. While one is carried out, the person it names is treated as owning that payment.**

Title III of the Music Modernization Act, the Allocation for Music Producers (AMP) Act, added this rule. The statute lets a payee of the recording owner's 50 percent or of the featured artists' 45 percent give a letter, in circumstances the collective decides are appropriate. SoundExchange's page is narrower: it says only featured artists can use its letters. While a letter is in effect, the law treats the payee who gave it as having no claim to that payment.

- Read section 114(g)(5) and SoundExchange's current page before drafting a letter.

### One letter follows a recording onto every release it appears on

**SoundExchange applies a letter to a recording wherever it appears, so a track on a single, an album and a compilation is covered on all of them.**

SoundExchange says one letter can list several recordings, or several albums, by one featured artist, and that it follows each recording onto every release. A recording with more than one featured artist needs a letter from each artist; the recorded-music area explains how SoundExchange splits a 'featuring' credit.

- List every recording by that artist in one repertoire chart.
- Keep each artist's signed letter with the release file.

### Letter payments wait for thresholds and stop if the artist's money is held

**Payment starts in the first pay period after the letter is active and the producer's balance passes SoundExchange's threshold. It stops on a recording whose featured-artist money is held.**

SoundExchange's page gives these thresholds: for electronic payment, monthly above $100 and quarterly above $10; for cheques, quarterly above $100. It names a dispute over the featured artist's royalties, or the artist withdrawing registration, as cases where nothing is paid on a recording. Amounts can change.

- Check your payment method and account details before the first payment is due.

### SoundExchange handles withholding and reporting on letter payments

**SoundExchange says that since 1 January 2020 artists and producers face backup withholding unless it holds a taxpayer ID, and that it handles the reporting itself. This is not tax advice.**

Before that date, SoundExchange says, artists reported letter payments themselves and gave producers a Form 1099 or 1042 each year as needed. It says the artist no longer needs to do that. SoundExchange gives no tax advice, and neither does this guide. Form W-8 BEN for non-US payees is covered in the basics area.

- Give SoundExchange your taxpayer details in your account.
- Ask a tax adviser how these payments are taxed for you.

### For recordings fixed before 1 November 1995, producers may share 2 percent with no letter

**Section 114(g)(6) lets the collective move 2 percent of an older recording's receipts from the featured artists' share to eligible producers, mixers or engineers, on strict conditions.**

The 2 percent is taken from all receipts for the recording, out of the share otherwise paid to the featured artists (45 percent of receipts), after the collective's costs. The collective adopts a policy and applies it in circumstances it decides are appropriate. The checklist below sets out each condition. This entry rests on the statute alone; no SoundExchange procedure for this route was read for this guide.

- Ask SoundExchange how it applies this route today.
- Have a music lawyer read the statute and your contract.

### A producer who helps write the song may be a joint author, and then registers as a writer

**17 USC 101 defines a joint work as one by two or more authors who intend their contributions to merge into "inseparable or interdependent parts of a unitary whole."**

The statute does not say how much a producer's input to a song has to be for the producer to be a joint author. Where that is unclear or disputed, a music lawyer should advise. A producer who is a writer of the song registers it like any other writer; the PRO and MLC areas give the steps.

- Put every writer's agreed share on a signed split sheet.
- Register the song where a writer registers; see the PRO and MLC areas.

### Whether a recording is made for hire turns on facts and signed wording

**Calling a recording 'for hire' in an agreement does not settle it. The Copyright Office describes tests that have to be met, and a producer agreement should get legal review.**

Circular 56 says a recording an employee makes in the course of the job is made for hire, with the employer as author. Sound recordings are not among the nine categories Circular 30 lists for commissioned works. Circular 56 describes a commissioned recording as for hire when it is made as a compilation or a contribution to a collective work and the parties agreed in writing. Circular 30 says a commissioned work that fails any of its four conditions is not for hire. How this applies to a given recording is a question for a music lawyer.

- Have a music lawyer review any work-for-hire or ownership wording before you accept it.

### A letter of direction cannot pay session musicians or backing singers

**SoundExchange says it will not honour a letter for studio musicians or background singers, so a producer's letter is no way to pay them.**

Their statutory share comes through the two escrow funds covered in the recorded-music area. For a transmission licensed outside the statutory licence, section 114(g)(1) says a non-featured artist is paid by the recording owner under the applicable contract or agreement. In the UK, a session player can claim as a performer at PPL.

- Put session payment terms in the session agreement.

### Keep legal names, roles and ISRCs from the session, because the paperwork asks for them

**A SoundExchange letter needs legal names and track details, with ISRCs where available, and PPL asks each performer to pick a role. Missing details slow things down.**

SoundExchange's letter form asks for each performer's legal name and the payee's name and contact details, and its chart asks for track titles, effective dates and ISRCs where available. It says missing items delay activation. PPL's claim route asks each performer to pick a role, and some roles are not payable. Collecting these details at the session is this guide's suggestion, not a rule.

- Write down each contributor's legal name, role and contact when the session ends.
- Match each letter's track title and ISRC to the released credit.

### In the UK, PPL treats a studio producer as a performer only in specific cases

**PPL says a studio producer may count as a performer when they conduct, or musically direct in a similar way, another performer's live take while it is captured.**

PPL says later edits and remixes do not qualify, though new versions with new audible performances may. A qualifying producer is always listed as non-featured, and PPL's Eligible Studio Producer form has to be completed by the producer and the featured performers. A producer whose own instruments, electronics or vocals are audible can claim for that as any performer can. Some myPPL roles are not payable.

- Read PPL's Eligible Studio Producer form and its performer page before claiming.

### Producer claims in Canada, Australia, Germany and France are not covered yet

**This area has read producer rules only for SoundExchange in the US and PPL in the UK. It has not checked how producers claim in Canada, Australia, Germany or France.**

The letter of direction is a US statutory route and the studio-producer test is PPL's own rule, so neither says what happens in another country. Until this guide covers those countries, ask the recording-rights society there directly, and a music lawyer there about any producer agreement.

- Ask the recording-rights society in your country how producers claim.

## Common mistakes

### Mistake: expecting SoundExchange to pay a producer who has no letter of direction

**SoundExchange's page describes one way to pay a producer, mixer or engineer: a letter of direction from a featured artist. A credit or points in a deal do not do that job.**

Section 114(g)(5) requires the collective to have a policy for accepting letters, and SoundExchange's program takes them from featured artists. Its page names no payment to a producer based on a credit or on a deal with the label. For recordings fixed before 1 November 1995, section 114(g)(6) sets a separate, conditional route, covered below.

- Ask each featured artist for a letter of direction around release.
- Set up your own SoundExchange account, since letter payments are paid into it.

Letters of direction · Beginner · Sourced (official) · For: producers, artists · Where: US · As of 20 November 2026 · Sources: [SoundExchange n.d.-d](https://www.soundexchange.com/what-we-do/for-artists-labels-and-producers/letters-of-direction/), [USCCLIIC n.d.-c](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/114)

### Mistake: copying contract points onto a letter of direction

**A letter states a percentage of one featured artist's share at SoundExchange. Points are measured on whatever base the producer agreement sets, so the two numbers need not match.**

SoundExchange's chart asks for a payment percentage for each recording, measured against the featured artist's share, and tells filers not to enter points or fractions of a percent. The producer agreement may measure points on a different base, so a number cannot simply be carried across. Agreeing what the letter will state belongs in the producer's agreement.

- Agree the letter percentage in writing with the artist.
- Have a music lawyer help turn the deal into that percentage.

Letters of direction · Advanced · Sourced (official) · For: producers, artists · Where: US · As of 20 November 2026 · Sources: [SoundExchange n.d.-d](https://www.soundexchange.com/what-we-do/for-artists-labels-and-producers/letters-of-direction/)

### Mistake: assuming a producer credit is a songwriting credit

**The Copyright Office names the producer as an author of the recording, unless it was made for hire. Writing the song is separate, so a production credit does not make anyone a songwriter.**

The song and the recording are separate works, as the basics area explains. Circular 56 names the featured performer and the producer, the person who recorded and processed what you hear, as authors of the recording. A producer who also wrote part of the music or lyrics may be a writer of the song as well, but that rests on what they contributed and what the writers agree, not on the production credit.

- Agree in writing who wrote what, and each writer's share, before release.
- In the split-sheet tool, pick the role for what you wrote and tick 'Also credited as the producer'.

## Four ways a producer can be paid beyond a fee, and what triggers each

Contract points are separate from these. Each route below has its own trigger and its own register, and one does not stand in for another.

## Conditions for the pre-1995 two percent route, as the statute states them

Every condition has to be met. A written objection from an artist payee stops later payments from that payee's share, though earlier ones stand.

**Who can be paid**

- The person produced, mixed or engineered the recording
- A written deal with the featured artist, or with a label that helped make or lawfully exploit the recording, giving a share of royalties paid from the artist's
- A creative contribution to the recording
- A certification of these points under penalty of perjury, and an accurate copy of that contract, sent to the collective

**Before any payment**

- At least 120 days of reasonable efforts to get a letter from the artist payee, with no written yes or no
- A certification of those efforts, under penalty of perjury, sent to the collective
- At least 120 days, before the first payment, of the collective trying to tell the artist payee about it
- No written objection from the artist payee as of 10 business days before the first distribution

**Afterwards**

- Several eligible people divide the 2 percent equally
- An objection stops later payment within 10 business days; payments already made stand
- Where a recording has several artist payees, one payee's objection affects only that payee's share

## A producer's checklist before release

Tick what applies. The agreements come first, because the letters and claims that follow rest on them.

**Agreements**

- Points, what they are measured on and payment timing written down
- Ownership and any work-for-hire wording reviewed by a music lawyer
- Any songwriting share agreed in writing on a split sheet

**US recording money**

- A letter from each featured artist you are to be paid by
- Your own SoundExchange account and tax details
- Repertoire chart with ISRCs and the agreed percentage
- Older recording: conditions of the pre-1995 route checked

**Credits**

- Legal names and roles recorded for everyone who worked on the track
- Released credits match the paperwork

**UK**

- Studio-producer claim only if PPL's conditions fit, with the form completed by you and the featured performers

## Terms

Defined on the glossary: https://kitesink.com/music/glossary.md

- **Letter of direction (LOD)**: Under 17 USC 114(g)(5), an instruction from a payee of the recording owner's or featured artists' share to the designated collective (SoundExchange) to pay part of it to the recording's producer, mixer or sound engineer.
- **Points (a producer's royalty)**: The usual name for a producer's royalty on a record. Each point is one percentage point of whatever base the producer's agreement sets.
- **Work made for hire**: A work whose author, in law, is the employer or commissioning party. Either an employee made it within the scope of the job, or it was commissioned for one of nine listed uses under a signed written agreement calling it for hire.

## Split-sheet generator

A split sheet records what the writers of a song agreed: who wrote it, in what role, and each writer's share. It is not a publishing contract, and this page is not legal advice. For anything beyond recording the split, get a music lawyer.

Fill it in together, check that the shares make exactly 100 percent, then print it and have every writer sign and date it. Give every writer a copy.

A sheet holds the song title, any alternate titles and the date agreed; the ISWC, and the ISRC and artist of the recording, if there are any; and whether the song uses samples or interpolations, and which. Samples and interpolations need clearance from the owners of the work they use. This sheet does not clear them.

For each writer it holds their legal name, role, PRO or society, IPI name number, publisher and the publisher's role, and share, with a line to sign and date. Shares are percentages of the song, to two decimal places, and must add up to exactly 100. Split evenly gives three writers 33.34, 33.33 and 33.33. Pick each writer's role for what they wrote. A producer who co-wrote the song gets the role for their part, with "Also credited as the producer" ticked.

Writer roles: These are the writer roles societies use when a song is registered. Arranger, adaptor and translator are for a new version of a song that already exists. If that song is still in copyright, its owners must agree to the new version.

- Composer: Wrote music for the song, but none of the words.
- Author (lyricist): Wrote words for the song, but none of the music.
- Composer and author: Wrote both music and words for the song.
- Arranger: Made a new arrangement of the music of a song that already existed.
- Adaptor: Reworked the words of a song that already existed, other than by translating them.
- Translator: Translated the words of a song that already existed into another language.

Publisher roles:

- Original publisher: The publisher the writer signed their share of this song to. A writer who publishes themselves names their own company.
- Administrator: A company that registers the song and collects for the writer or their publisher under an admin deal, usually without owning it.
