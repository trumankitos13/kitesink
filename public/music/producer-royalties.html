Producer pay, point by point.

How producers are paid: the fee and the points, recoupment, a letter of direction for SoundExchange, and when a producer also earns as a writer.

General information on how music rights and royalties work, not legal advice. For a contract, a termination notice or a dispute, talk to a music lawyer.

Split sheet

Agree the split, then write it down.

For the co-writers of one song: who wrote it, in what role, and each writer's share, ready to print and sign.

Not a contract

A split sheet records what the writers of a song agreed: who wrote it, in what role, and each writer's share. It is not a publishing contract, and this page is not legal advice. For anything beyond recording the split, get a music lawyer.

Fill it in together, check that the shares make exactly 100 percent, then print it and have every writer sign and date it. Give every writer a copy.

The generator needs JavaScript. A split sheet can also be written by hand: the song title, any other titles and the date agreed; the ISWC, and the ISRC and artist of the recording, if there are any; whether the song uses samples or interpolations, and which; then for each writer their legal name, role, PRO or society, IPI name number, publisher and the publisher's role, and share, with the shares adding up to 100 percent; and a line for each writer to sign and date.

The song

Only if the song already has one. A song gets its ISWC when it is first registered, so a new song will not have one yet.

The code of the recording this split is for, if it has one.

Does the song use a sample or an interpolation?

A sample uses part of an existing recording. An interpolation re-records part of an existing song.

Samples and interpolations need clearance from the owners of the work they use. This sheet does not clear them.

The writers

Shares are percentages of the song, to two decimal places, and must add up to exactly 100. Split evenly gives three writers 33.34, 33.33 and 33.33. Pick each writer's role for what they wrote. A producer who co-wrote the song gets the role for their part, with "Also credited as the producer" ticked.

What the roles mean

These are the writer roles societies use when a song is registered. Arranger, adaptor and translator are for a new version of a song that already exists. If that song is still in copyright, its owners must agree to the new version.

Composer
Wrote music for the song, but none of the words.
Author (lyricist)
Wrote words for the song, but none of the music.
Composer and author
Wrote both music and words for the song.
Arranger
Made a new arrangement of the music of a song that already existed.
Adaptor
Reworked the words of a song that already existed, other than by translating them.
Translator
Translated the words of a song that already existed into another language.

The publisher's role, when the writer has a publisher for this song:

Original publisher
The publisher the writer signed their share of this song to. A writer who publishes themselves names their own company.
Administrator
A company that registers the song and collects for the writer or their publisher under an admin deal, usually without owning it.

      Points and other routesPoints are a contract term, and this guide states no standard figureHow many points a producer gets, what they are measured on and when they are paid are all set by the producer's agreement. No law or collective sets them.BeginnerDirectional

      A producer may be paid by a label, an artist or a company, and each agreement says what income counts and which costs come off first.       Letters of directionTitle III of the Music Modernization Act made letters of direction a statutory policySection 114(g)(5) requires the designated collective to adopt a policy for accepting letters of direction. While one is carried out, the person it names is treated as owning that payment.IntermediateSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

      Title III of the Music Modernization Act, the Allocation for Music Producers (AMP) Act, added this rule. The statute lets a payee of the recording owner's 50 percent or of the featured artists' 45 percent give a letter, in circumstances the collective decides are appropriate. SoundExchange's page is narrower: it says only featured artists can use its letters. While a letter is in effect, the law treats the payee who gave it as having no claim to that payment.

      • Read section 114(g)(5) and SoundExchange's current page before drafting a letter.

      Letters of directionMistake: copying contract points onto a letter of directionA letter states a percentage of one featured artist's share at SoundExchange.       Producer or writerA producer who helps write the song may be a joint author, and then registers as a writer17 USC 101 defines a joint work as one by two or more authors who intend their contributions to merge into "inseparable or interdependent parts of a unitary whole."IntermediateSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

      USCCLIIC n.d.-a

      Producer or writerMistake: assuming a producer credit is a songwriting creditThe Copyright Office names the producer as an author of the recording, unless it was made for hire. Writing the song is separate, so a production credit does not make anyone a songwriter.MistakeBeginnerSourced

      The song and the recording are separate works, as the basics area explains. Circular 56 names the featured performer and the producer, the person who recorded and processed what you hear, as authors of the recording. A producer who also wrote part of the music or lyrics may be a writer of the song as well, but that rests on what they contributed and what the writers agree, not on the production credit.

      • Agree in writing who wrote what, and each writer's share, before release.
      • In the split-sheet tool, pick the role for what you wrote and tick 'Also credited as the producer'.

      Ownership and work for hireWhether a recording is made for hire turns on facts and signed wordingCalling a recording 'for hire' in an agreement does not settle it. The Copyright Office describes tests that have to be met, and a producer agreement should get legal review.AdvancedSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

      Circular 56 says a recording an employee makes in the course of the job is made for hire, with the employer as author. Sound recordings are not among the nine categories Circular 30 lists for commissioned works. Circular 56 describes a commissioned recording as for hire when it is made as a compilation or a contribution to a collective work and the parties agreed in writing. Circular 30 says a commissioned work that fails any of its four conditions is not for hire. How this applies to a given recording is a question for a music lawyer.

      • Have a music lawyer review any work-for-hire or ownership wording before you accept it.

      Credits and session playersKeep legal names, roles and ISRCs from the session, because the paperwork asks for themA SoundExchange letter needs legal names and track details, with ISRCs where available, and PPL asks each performer to pick a role. Missing details slow things down.BeginnerSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

      SoundExchange's letter form asks for each performer's legal name and the payee's name and contact details, and its chart asks for track titles, effective dates and ISRCs where available. It says missing items delay activation. PPL's claim route asks each performer to pick a role, and some roles are not payable. Collecting these details at the session is this guide's suggestion, not a rule.

      • Write down each contributor's legal name, role and contact when the session ends.
      • Match each letter's track title and ISRC to the released credit.

      Producers abroadIn the UK, PPL treats a studio producer as a performer only in specific casesPPL says a studio producer may count as a performer when they conduct, or musically direct in a similar way, another performer's live take while it is captured.IntermediateSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

      PPL says later edits and remixes do not qualify, though new versions with new audible performances may. A qualifying producer is always listed as non-featured, and PPL's Eligible Studio Producer form has to be completed by the producer and the featured performers. A producer whose own instruments, electronics or vocals are audible can claim for that as any performer can. Some myPPL roles are not payable.

      • Read PPL's Eligible Studio Producer form and its performer page before claiming.
