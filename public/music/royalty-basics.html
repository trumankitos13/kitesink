Two copyrightsEvery recorded song holds two separate copyrightsThe song (music and lyrics) and the recording of it are two works, each with its own rights and its own income.BeginnerSourced

A song is a musical work. A recording of someone performing it is a sound recording. US law treats them as distinct works, and their owners can be different people. The rest of this page follows that split: each work earns through its own rights and its own collectors.

  • Write down who owns the song and who owns the recording for every release.

Two copyrightsA recording's authors: the performer and producer, unless made for hireThe Copyright Office names the featured performer and the producer as authors of a sound recording. For a work made for hire, the employer or hiring party is the author.IntermediateSourced

That is the starting point for US registration. Ownership can be transferred by contract, so the owner on paper may differ from the author. Read the agreement for each release, and have a music lawyer review anything that transfers ownership.

  • Check each contract for who owns the master and for how long.

Two copyrightsCopyright protection starts on creation; registration is a separate stepA US sound recording made since 1978 is protected from the moment it is fixed. Registering with the Copyright Office is a separate step with its own form, fee and deposit.IntermediateSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

Circular 56 says protection under current law needs neither registration nor publication. Circulars 50 and 56 explain how to apply: a completed application, a filing fee and a copy of the work. The Office warns that its fees change, so check the current list before you file.

  • Read the circular for your type of work before applying.

Two copyrightsMistake: registering the song and assuming the recording is coveredA registration for a song covers its music and lyrics, not the recorded performance. Recordings are normally registered on their own.MistakeBeginnerSourced

Circular 50 says a song and a recording usually have to be registered with the Copyright Office separately. Only in limited cases can a single application cover both, and the Office points to Circular 56A for those.

  • Check that the song and the recording each have their own registration.
  • Read Circular 50 and Circular 56 before filing.

Income streamsMechanical and performance royalties pay for different usesA mechanical royalty pays for making copies of a song, and a performance royalty pays when it is performed or communicated to the public.BeginnerSourced

Both terms come from industry custom more than from statute. WIPO describes the mechanical right as the right to record a work and distribute copies, and the performing right as covering live or broadcast use and other ways of reaching the public. A single stream of a song can involve both rights, so more than one body may collect on it.

  • Register each song with every body that collects on it.

Income streamsUS recordings have no general public performance rightA US sound recording owner has a performance right only for digital audio transmissions, not the broader right that song owners hold.IntermediateSourced

Section 114(a) gives a sound recording owner only four of the section 106 rights, (1), (2), (3) and (6), leaving out the general performance right in (4). Clause (6) covers digital audio transmissions, so the US recording side has one dedicated route for performance money.

  • Do not assume a UK or Canadian recording royalty has a US equivalent.

Income streamsSync licences need permission for the song and for the recordingPutting music in a film, ad or game is licensed by deal. In practice, using an existing recording means clearing both the song and the recording.BeginnerDirectional

Sync is agreed case by case between the user and the owners of each work, and this guide gives no fee levels. Clearing one side does not clear the other. A music lawyer should review a sync licence before it is signed.

  • Ask the owner of each side for a written licence before the music is used.

Income streamsPrinted music and lyrics are another reproduction usePrinted sheet music counts as a reproduction of the song. Lyrics are part of the song, so using them is also a use of the song.IntermediateSourced

WIPO lists printed sheet music as one form of reproducing a musical work, and Circular 50 treats the words that go with the music as part of the musical work. Who licenses print and lyrics varies, so do not assume it is covered.

  • Ask your publisher or administrator whether print and lyric licensing is included.

Who collectsThe MLC collects US digital mechanicals for songsThe MLC collects mechanical royalties from eligible US streaming and download services under the blanket licence, then pays the song's owners.BeginnerSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

The Copyright Office designated the MLC under Title I of the Music Modernization Act. Services send it usage data and royalties each month, and it pays members monthly for registered songs it can match. Registering, claiming and matching are covered on the mechanicals page.

  • Register your songs with the MLC, or confirm your publisher has.

Who collectsPerforming rights societies collect for public performance of songsIn the US, a performing rights society licenses public performance of songs for their owners. The statute names ASCAP, BMI and SESAC; Songview also lists GMR.BeginnerSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

Section 101 describes a performing rights society as a body that licenses public performance of nondramatic musical works for their copyright owners. Songview, a joint project of ASCAP, BMI, GMR and SESAC, reconciles their song ownership data and feeds the ASCAP and BMI public databases.

  • Join a performing rights society as a writer and register each song with it.

Who collectsSoundExchange collects digital performance money for recordingsSoundExchange pays recording owners and artists for digital play on non-interactive services, but only those who have registered with it.BeginnerSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

SoundExchange names services such as SiriusXM and Pandora, and says recording artists and owners must register with it to be paid. The U.S. Copyright Royalty Board decides what these services pay. Its lists of creators owed money are covered on the unclaimed-money page.

  • Register as artist and, if you own the master, as a recording owner.

Splits and sharesA song's income divides into a writer share and a publisher shareWhere a song has a publisher, a society divides its income between the writer and the publisher, by their contract, the society's rules, or both.BeginnerSourced

WIPO notes that most musical works have at least a composer and a publisher as rights holders, and that a society's rules may set a minimum part for the composer. This guide gives no standard percentage; check your publishing agreement and your society's distribution rules.

  • Read your publishing contract and your society's rules for the split.

Splits and sharesA publisher licenses songs and pays advancesA music publisher gets the right to license a writer's songs, collects the income, and often pays an advance that is later recovered from royalties.BeginnerSourced

WIPO describes publishers as the link that brings songs to record and broadcast companies and as a source of advances to writers. The writer is paid a share of what the publisher receives. Terms vary, and a music lawyer should read any publishing deal before it is signed.

  • Ask what the publisher does for the share it keeps.

RegistrationsPre-1972 recordings and producer letters of direction have their own rulesTitle II of the Music Modernization Act gives federal remedies to recordings fixed before 15 February 1972. Title III lets producers, mixers and engineers be paid by letter of direction.AdvancedSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

To recover statutory damages or fees for a pre-1972 recording, the owner usually has to file a schedule with the Copyright Office first. Under section 114(g)(5), SoundExchange can accept a letter from the artist or recording owner sending part of their digital performance money to a producer, mixer or engineer. Neither is covered in detail here.

  • Read the Copyright Office pages on Titles II and III before relying on either.

At release · RegistrationsRegister the recording when it is releasedRegister with SoundExchange, make sure the recording has an ISRC, and check it is linked to the song so the song's owners are paid.StepBeginnerSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

SoundExchange says recording artists and owners must be registered to receive its digital performance royalties. The ISRC identifies the recording, and the MLC's Matching Tool uses it to connect recordings to songs, so give the code to your co-writers or publisher.

  • Register with SoundExchange for each role you hold.
  • Check the match to the song in the MLC Matching Tool.

InternationalSocieties work abroad through reciprocal agreementsA society licenses mainly at home. To be paid abroad, societies appoint each other, so foreign income reaches you through your own society.IntermediateSourced

WIPO describes reciprocal representation agreements, under which each society licenses the other's repertoire in its own country and then passes on the money. Because of this, a writer's registration at home matters for income earned in other countries.

  • Confirm that your society collects abroad on your behalf.

InternationalOutside the US, recordings can earn neighbouring rights moneyIn the UK and Canada, PPL and Re:Sound deal with money for recordings on behalf of performers and recording owners. It is a separate account from songwriter income.IntermediateSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

PPL says rightsholder members can ask it to collect royalties from abroad, and Re:Sound tells anyone who performed on or made released recordings that they may be owed. Each body has its own rules on who qualifies, so a US artist should check country by country.

  • Ask PPL or Re:Sound whether you qualify for recordings played in those countries.

InternationalPPL divides UK recording money between rightsholders and performersFor UK public play and broadcasts, PPL matches reported use against its database of over 28 million recordings and, after costs, pays half to rightsholders and half to qualifying performers.BeginnerSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

PPL checks that a performer qualifies for a track before paying the performer share; if not, that part goes to the rightsholder. Several performers on one recording share by agreement or under PPL's allocation policy. PPL says the performer share is sometimes called equitable remuneration.

  • Check in myPPL that you are credited on each recording.

How money flowsHow money moves from a stream to a songwriterA service reports plays and pays a collector, which matches them to registered songs and pays the owners on file. A writer may be paid by the collector directly or through a publisher.BeginnerSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

For US digital mechanicals the collector is the MLC; for public performance it is a performing rights society. WIPO notes that societies usually take both writers and publishers as members. Where a publisher receives the writer's money, the writer is paid a share under the publishing contract. A song that is not registered or matched stays unpaid.

  • Check each step: registered, matched, and paid to the right account.

How money flowsHow money moves from a stream to an artistStatutory digital performance money goes through SoundExchange and is split by law. Other recording income reaches the artist through the recording owner, as the contract says.BeginnerSourcedAs of 20 November 2026

Section 114(g)(2) sets the split for money collected under the statutory licence. For a transmission licensed outside it, section 114(g)(1) entitles a featured artist to payment from the recording owner under the artist's contract. WIPO notes that recording contracts often include a recoupable advance. Have a music lawyer review the deal.

  • Ask for the royalty statement that shows each source of income.
