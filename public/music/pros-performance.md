# Performance royalties: KiteSink music field guide

PROs, public performance, and how plays become pay. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, who it is for, where it applies, how well sourced it is, and its sources.
Grades: Sourced (law or official) and Directional (reported or industry practice).

## Guidance

### US law gives song owners the exclusive right to perform in public

**Section 106(4) gives the owner of a musical work the exclusive right to perform it publicly, live or by transmission. A PRO licenses that right for its members.**

Section 101 treats a performance as public if it happens where the public can attend or a sizeable group beyond family and friends gathers, or if it is transmitted to the public, whether people receive it together or apart. Section 106 makes the right subject to the exceptions in sections 107 to 122, so not every public play needs a licence. The basics page sets this right beside the mechanical right.

- Ask a music lawyer before relying on an exception for a specific use.

### Collective licensing exists because song-by-song deals do not scale

**A PRO pools many owners' rights so a user can cover many songs with one licence, and a writer does not chase every bar, station and service alone.**

The US Department of Justice describes ASCAP and BMI as pooling their members' copyrights and licensing them collectively, so users avoid separate deals with every writer and publisher. WIPO's training material makes the same point: where works are performed in public or broadcast, collective management is the only practical route for both sides.

### GMR says the venue, not the performer, must hold the licence

**GMR states that the operator of a venue, not the musicians or DJs playing there, is responsible for licensing the music performed on its premises.**

In its FAQ for businesses, GMR says paying performers does not remove the operator's duty to obtain a licence, and that live and recorded music both need permission. This is GMR's own statement, aimed at licensees. It explains why PROs license venues, which is where live-performance royalties start.

What PROs do · Intermediate · Sourced (official) · For: songwriters, artists, administrators · Where: US · As of 20 November 2026 · Sources: [Global Music Rights n.d.-b](https://globalmusicrights.com/FAQ)

### A licence from one US PRO does not cover another PRO's songs

**GMR says a venue licensed by ASCAP, BMI or SESAC still needs a GMR licence to play GMR writers' music, and the reverse also holds.**

GMR's FAQ says its licence is separate from those of ASCAP, BMI and SESAC, and that theirs do not cover GMR clients' songs. A user playing songs from several PROs needs a licence from each. Where co-writers belong to different PROs, the DOJ says ASCAP and BMI favour licensing only the share they control; see fractional licensing below.

### Many societies take your performing right, not just a mandate to collect

**In many English-speaking countries a society takes over ownership of the performing right outright, so it holds that right and can enforce it.**

WIPO's 2012 training material describes a range. Some bodies act only as agent for the rights holder. In many English-speaking countries the society takes a full assignment and becomes owner of the performing right. In Germany it holds rights in trust, and the US practice it describes is a non-exclusive licence. Terms differ by society, so read the membership terms before you sign, and ask a music lawyer what an assignment means for your catalogue.

### The ASCAP and BMI consent decrees shape what each can do

**The decrees bar exclusive licensing, let members license their works directly, and require ASCAP and BMI to accept qualifying members.**

The DOJ says the decrees date from 1941 in their earliest form, with the latest changes in 1994 (BMI) and 2001 (ASCAP). They also require the same terms for similarly placed users, cap membership agreements at five years, and shield users from infringement suits over works missing from or wrongly shown in the public repertories. Stated as of the January 2021 closing statement.

Consent decrees · Intermediate · Sourced (official) · For: songwriters, publishers and labels, administrators · Where: US · As of 15 January 2021 · Sources: [USDJAD 2021b](https://www.justice.gov/atr/page/file/1355391/dl)

### SESAC and GMR are not bound by the consent decrees

**The DOJ notes that the two decrees bind only ASCAP and BMI, so other PROs work without those limits. Terms can differ.**

In its 2021 statement the DOJ listed this among critics' concerns and also noted that more PROs had risen in the market. The decrees set rules on membership terms and licence access for ASCAP and BMI; the other two PROs run under their own terms. Read each PRO's agreement, and use a music lawyer for any decision about assigning rights.

### In January 2021 the DOJ closed its review of the decrees and proposed no changes

**The DOJ closed its review of the ASCAP and BMI decrees on 15 January 2021, proposed no changes, and called for a fresh review every five years.**

The statement, by the Assistant Attorney General for Antitrust, followed over 800 public comments in 2019 and a public workshop in 2020. It noted views that the competition concerns behind the decrees still exist. On 20 November 2026 the DOJ review page and its ASCAP and BMI case pages showed nothing later than this statement. DOJ site search was blocked, so this is not a full check.

- Check the DOJ Antitrust Division's ASCAP and BMI pages for any later review.

### Under fractional licensing, each PRO licenses only its own share of a song

**A user then needs a licence from every PRO with a share of the song, or from the owners directly. The Second Circuit held that BMI's decree permits this.**

ASCAP and BMI favour fractional licensing; licensees favour full-work licensing, where one licence covers the whole song. The DOJ once took the view that the decrees required full-work licensing, and the Second Circuit disagreed on BMI's decree. The 2021 statement says BMI is not obliged to license full works and that the debate continues. For writers, co-writers' PRO choices affect how a work is licensed.

### As of 2021, publishers could not pull only streaming rights from ASCAP or BMI

**Partial withdrawal would let an owner keep digital services out of a PRO licence. The DOJ says a 2013 attempt to read the decrees that way failed.**

The 2021 statement says some writers and publishers favour partial withdrawal because they expect direct deals with streaming services to pay more, while many licensees oppose it. Because the 2013 effort failed in the rate courts, the DOJ says change would probably take amended decrees or legislation. Status as of January 2021.

### ASCAP's decree limits it to performance rights

**The DOJ says ASCAP's decree bars licensing anything but performance rights, while BMI's decree is silent on other rights.**

The DOJ says the publishing community handles most mechanical and sync licensing, as of its 2021 statement. For mechanical money, see the mechanicals page; it covers the MLC. The DOJ added that ASCAP and BMI had shown interest in other rights and that some publishers were concerned.

### The Music Modernization Act changed how ASCAP and BMI rate disputes are heard

**Per the DOJ, since the Act, ASCAP and BMI rate disputes go to one of several judges in the Southern District of New York, not the judge who oversees the decree.**

The DOJ's 2021 statement says the Act created this rotation, partly repealed a limit on using digital sound recording rates as evidence in setting musical work rates, and means DOJ must tell Congress before it moves to end a decree. Described as of January 2021.

### The Copyright Office ran a 2025 inquiry into PROs and sent Congress a summary

**Congress asked the Office to look at the rise in PRO numbers and how PROs distribute. The Office's 20 November 2025 letter made no new policy recommendations.**

Three members of Congress asked in 2024. The Office opened its inquiry with a notice on 10 February 2025, with initial comments due 11 April and replies due 27 May 2025, and received over 5,000, of which more than 4,865 were near-identical. Its letter summarising them points to earlier Office reports on music licensing. Check the Office's page for later steps.

### The Copyright Office says not every PRO discloses what it licenses or how it pays

**The Office notes that more PROs may weaken licensing efficiency and that some do not publish full repertoire or distribution policies.**

This is the Office's own background statement on its inquiry page, not a finding about any named PRO. For a writer, it is a reason to ask any PRO for its payment rules and repertoire data in writing before joining, and to compare them. Statement as read in November 2026.

- Ask a PRO for its distribution rules before you join.

### Songview was built so licensees can see each song's owners and shares

**The DOJ says ASCAP and BMI launched Songview in December 2020 as an authoritative listing of who owns each song in their repertories and in what shares.**

The DOJ hoped it would help licensees see which licences each song needs, and encouraged the PROs to keep improving it. Songview now says ASCAP, BMI, GMR and SESAC take part and that it covers over 40 million works. How its data flows is on the basics page. Check that your shares match across PROs.

- Search your songs in the ASCAP and BMI repertory tools and compare shares.

### SOCAN membership has no fee for writers and a five-work test for publishers

**A composer, songwriter, producer or lyricist can join SOCAN free if a work is published, recorded or performed where SOCAN licenses it. Publishers need five qualifying works.**

Per the sign-up page, publishers need signed contracts showing they own, or are entitled to the publisher's share of, at least five works by a SOCAN member or a Canadian, alone or with others. Reproduction rights can be assigned separately. Terms as read in November 2026.

- Read the SOCAN sign-up page and agreement before applying.

### GEMA charges an admission fee and a yearly fee, and waives the first for younger creators

**A creator pays EUR 90 plus VAT to join and EUR 50 a year. Creators who turn 30 or younger in the year they apply pay no admission fee.**

A publisher pays EUR 180 net to join and EUR 100 a year; one edition name costs nothing and each further one is EUR 100 net. An application received by 31 October starts membership that year; a later one starts on 1 January. Fees as read in November 2026.

- Check GEMA's current fee page and application date before applying.

### SOCAN pays performing-right royalties on set dates, with cut-offs before each

**SOCAN's 2026 schedule lists performing-right payments on 15 May, 14 August and 13 November, each with cut-offs for cue sheets and agreements.**

A late cue sheet or agreement registration can miss a distribution, per the cut-off columns. The page warns some dates may change. The 13 November 2026 date has passed. Check the live schedule for the next one, and for the reproduction-rights dates, which differ.

### Societies may pay from samples or proxy data when counting every play is impractical

**WIPO says ideal pay-per-play is not always practical, so societies may pay from a sample of uses or from a proxy source such as radio data.**

Its examples: one day a week of broadcasts standing in for the week, or radio data used to pay for music in a shop or on a jukebox. Each society has its own distribution rules, agreed with its members. Your statement may reflect a method, not a count of every play. Ask your society which applies to each income line.

- Ask your society how each income line is calculated.

### GEMA states it is non-profit and pays by its members' distribution plan

**GEMA says its only deductions are administration costs and money for members' social and cultural support, and distribution follows its plan.**

Members can change the plan at the annual General Assembly. GEMA also says it pays creators abroad and receives royalties from foreign sister societies for its members' works. This is GEMA's own description, read in November 2026. Read the plan itself for the rules on a given income type.

- Read the GEMA distribution plan for your type of income.

### Live shows abroad are licensed by the local society, so tell your own society

**A venue abroad deals with its local society, which licenses foreign repertoire by reciprocal agreement. Your own society needs your dates to follow up.**

WIPO lists concerts and festivals among uses a society can license, and says each society licenses partner societies' repertoire in its own country. Whether a given venue is licensed, and whether your society accepts late reports, varies. Check your society's foreign live reporting rules and deadlines before the tour.

- Ask your society how to report foreign shows and how old they can be.

## Common mistakes

### Registering the writer side and never checking who holds the publisher share

**Many songs have at least a writer and a publisher. If the publisher side of a registration is empty or wrong, the song may not pay as you expect.**

WIPO notes most works have a composer and a publisher, and that societies need every rights holder documented. Societies differ on how they treat the publisher share of a song with no publisher, and this guide states no rule for any US PRO. Ask your society how it treats an unpublished song.

- Ask your society in writing how it handles the publisher share of a self-published song.

Registering · Intermediate · Directional (industry practice) · For: songwriters, artists, producers · Where: US, International · Sources: [WIPO 2012](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/pubdocs/en/wipo_pub_emat_2014_2.pdf)

### Not reporting live sets can leave live performances unpaid

**A society cannot pay for a use it does not know about. For many live shows the writer reports the set, so keep dates and set lists.**

WIPO says a society cannot distribute properly without knowing what works were used, and that a club reporting what a visiting DJ played is hard. SOCAN's member portal lets members submit live performances. Other societies set their own routes, so check yours.

- Keep date, venue and set list for each show.
- Find where your society takes live reports.

## Setting up for performance royalties

A short order of work: pick a society, register each work with its shares, then keep records of live and screen uses.

**Before you join**

- Ask the society for its payment rules and repertoire data
- Check its fees and membership term
- Ask a music lawyer about any rights assignment

**After you join**

- Register each work with every writer and publisher
- Check your shares in the ASCAP and BMI repertory searches (US)
- Keep date, venue and set list for each show
- Send cue sheets for screen uses

## Terms

Defined on the glossary: https://kitesink.com/music/glossary.md

- **Blanket licence (performing right)**: One licence that lets a user perform any work in a society's repertoire, including foreign works it represents under reciprocal deals, with no song-by-song clearance.
- **Cue sheet**: A list of the music in a TV or film programme, with each work and use, sent to a society so it can pay the writers and publishers.
- **Performing rights organisation (PRO)**: A body that licenses public performance of non-dramatic musical works for their owners. US law calls it a performing rights society.
