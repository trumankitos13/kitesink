# AI and music rights: KiteSink music field guide

Training, outputs, likeness, and the cases and rules so far. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, who it is for, where it applies, how well sourced it is, and its sources.
Grades: Sourced (law or official) and Directional (reported or industry practice).

## Guidance

### US copyright needs a human author, and the courts have said so

**In the US, a work with no human author cannot be registered, and the Supreme Court declined in March 2026 to revisit that.**

The D.C. Circuit decided Thaler v. Perlmutter (No. 23-5233) on 18 March 2025. It held that the Copyright Act requires eligible work to be authored first by a human being, and it upheld the refusal to register an image credited to a software system. The Supreme Court's docket for No. 25-449 shows the petition denied on 2 March 2026. The case concerned an image, not a song, and it did not say how much human input is enough.

- Treat a track made by a tool with no real human input from you as outside US copyright protection.
- Ask a music lawyer how the rule applies if your human input is partial.

### A text prompt alone does not make you the author of the output

**The Copyright Office says that, with today's tools, prompts alone do not give a user enough control to be the author of what comes back.**

Part 2 of the Office's AI report (29 January 2025) says human authors keep rights in what they wrote or played that shows in an output, in a creative selection or arrangement of outputs, and in creative changes they make. Whether the human input is enough is decided case by case. The Office saw no need for a new right for AI-generated content. This is the Office's view, not a court ruling.

- Keep dated project files that show what you wrote, played and edited yourself.
- Treat a song generated from a prompt alone as unprotected in the US.

### UK law still protects computer-generated works, but the government proposes ending that

**Section 9(3) of the UK Act names the person who arranged a computer-generated work as its author; the March 2026 report proposes removing that rule.**

The report of 18 March 2026 says section 9(3) CDPA sits uneasily with the originality test, and proposes removing the protection for works generated wholly by a computer unless evidence shows ongoing value. Works made with AI help that still show human creativity would stay protected. The report adds that AI-generated music may still be covered as a sound recording. This is a proposal, not a change in the law.

### The Copyright Office's training report exists only as a pre-publication version

**Part 3 calls training an audio model to make new recordings for the same market at best modestly transformative, and leaves fair use to case-by-case decisions.**

The Office released Part 3 in pre-publication form on 9 May 2025, and its AI page, checked 20 November 2026, still says a final version will follow with no substantive change expected. The report says training an audio model on recordings to generate new recordings aims at the same market, while using a model to remove unwanted distortion would be more transformative. It says existing or likely licence markets weigh against fair use, and that government intervention in licensing would be premature. It is the Office's view and does not bind courts.

### In the EU, rightsholders can reserve their music from commercial text and data mining

**In the EU, commercial text and data mining of lawfully accessible works is allowed unless the rightsholder reserves the use; German law requires online reservations to be machine-readable.**

GEMA's notice explains the rule as Germany applies it in section 44b UrhG, which implements Article 4 of the EU copyright directive of 2019. GEMA says it declared the reservation for the works it represents, including for uses outside Germany, and that licensees who put its repertoire online must signal the reservation in machine-readable form. GEMA's own notice adds that it is not settled whether commercial generative AI falls under the exception. German court rulings on this are in the litigation entries.

- Check whether your society has filed a reservation covering your works.
- Ask a music lawyer how a reservation applies to music you publish yourself.

### EU rules make AI model providers keep a copyright policy and publish a training summary

**In the EU, general-purpose AI model providers must keep a copyright policy and publish a training summary; Commission enforcement, with fines, began on 2 August 2026.**

The Commission says the duties applied from 2 August 2025, its enforcement powers apply from 2 August 2026, and models placed on the market earlier must comply by 2 August 2027. ### Tennessee's ELVIS Act protects a person's voice, including a simulated one

**Since 1 July 2024, Tennessee law covers a voice whether real or simulated, and reaches tools whose main purpose is copying one person's voice.**

Public Chapter 588 (HB 2091) amends Tennessee's personal rights law. It defines voice as a sound readily identifiable with an individual, real or simulated. It creates liability for publishing or distributing a voice or likeness with knowledge it was not authorised, and for offering a tool whose primary purpose is producing a specific person's voice or likeness. A company holding an exclusive recording or distribution contract can also sue. Uses such as news, criticism and parody are treated as fair where the First Amendment protects them. It is state law; a lawyer should say whether it reaches a case.

### California and New York have signed performer digital replica laws

**Governors signed California bills on 17 September 2024 and New York bills on 11 December 2025; read the statute text before relying on a summary.**

### What the reported NO FAKES text would do for a voice or likeness

**The reported bill would create a federal property right in voice and visual likeness, with limits on licence length and a takedown process for platforms.**

The reported text makes the right licensable but not assignable during life, limits a lifetime licence to 10 years, and lets the right run after death for up to 70 years through renewals. Platforms would act on notices, with a counter-notice step of at least 14 days before restoring material. It would take effect 180 days after enactment. It also contains a preemption section with carve-outs, including state laws in place on 2 January 2025. Details can change before passage. A music lawyer should read the text for any real decision.

### In Germany, a court ruled for GEMA against OpenAI over song lyrics

**Munich Regional Court I ruled for GEMA on 11 November 2025 (42 O 14139/24); LTO reported it was not yet final.**

Per GEMA, the court held that OpenAI should have licensed lyrics in GEMA's repertoire for training and running ChatGPT, that the systems hold copies reproduced in response to simple prompts, and that the text and data mining exception does not justify storing or outputting them. LTO gives the docket and says nine songs were at issue. GEMA is a party, so its account is one side's. This is one first-instance German court on lyrics, not music audio. ### In Germany, a court largely upheld GEMA's claims against Suno

**Per the court's own release, Munich Regional Court I largely upheld GEMA's claims against Suno on 31 July 2026 (42 O 763/25); the judgment is not final.**

The 42nd civil chamber ruled on six musical works, not lyrics. It found the works reproducibly contained in Suno models v3.5 and v4 stored on servers in Germany, held that this copy was not covered by the text and data mining exception, and found the outputs infringed. It held Suno, not its users, responsible for outputs from simple prompts. It also upheld claims for copying during training in the US and rejected fair use. Suno disputed the claims at the hearing of 9 March 2026. This is a first-instance ruling in one court, and no appeal decision was found.

### GEMA's AI licence model asks for 30 percent of a provider's net income

**GEMA's two-part licence model, set out in September 2024, asks AI providers for a 30 percent share of net income, with a minimum royalty tied to output.**

GEMA says the first component covers training, including fine-tuning, with 30 percent of the net income of the model or system and a minimum royalty linked to how much output is produced. It targets providers operating in the German market whatever the place of training. A second component would cover later use of AI-generated music, such as background music. This is GEMA's own proposed model. The sources opened do not name a provider that has signed it.

### ASCAP, BMI and SOCAN accept partly AI works and reject wholly AI ones

**On 28 October 2025 the three societies aligned: works that combine AI material with human authorship can be registered, entirely AI-made works cannot.**

This rests on SOCAN's pages; check ASCAP's and BMI's own pages too. SOCAN separates AI-generated outputs, which it will not register and may remove, from AI-assisted works, which it will. It says members need not explain their AI use at registration for an AI-assisted work, and that the member carries responsibility for registrations. If you registered a wholly AI output by mistake, SOCAN asks you to contact it to have it removed.

- Read your society's AI policy before you register an AI-assisted song.
- Ask your society to remove any wholly AI work you registered by mistake.

**The MLC follows Copyright Office guidance, cannot register entirely AI-generated works and does not decide whether a given song has enough human authorship.**

The MLC's help article says works with meaningful human authorship may be registered and entirely AI-generated works may not. ## In order

### Registering a song that contains AI material with the US Copyright Office

**Use the Standard Application, claim only your own authorship, and exclude AI material that is more than a minor part of the work.**

The Office's guidance (88 FR 16190, effective 16 March 2023) says to describe the human authorship in the Author Created field and not to name an AI tool or its maker as an author. AI content beyond a minor amount should be excluded, which can be done under Limitation of the Claim, Other, Material Excluded. If you are unsure, you can give a general statement that the work contains AI material, and the Office will follow up. An earlier registration can be corrected by a supplementary registration. The guidance covers all kinds of works and does not name music.

- Describe your own contribution in the Author Created field.
- List the AI-generated material as excluded in Limitation of the Claim.
- Ask a music lawyer before filing if ownership of the AI output is unclear.

## Common mistakes

### Mistake: signing a long or total voice and likeness licence without a music lawyer

**A broad licence of your voice or likeness can leave you without control for years; both the Copyright Office and the NO FAKES text point to that risk.**

Part 1 of the Copyright Office's report (31 July 2024) recommends that people may license their voice and image but not fully assign them, and warns that unequal bargaining power, especially early in a career, can lock people into broad terms. The reported NO FAKES text would cap a lifetime licence at 10 years. Neither is current law for your contract. What a deal covers, for how long and for which uses is a contract question.

- Have a music lawyer read any AI voice or likeness licence before you agree.
- Ask what is licensed, for how long, and whether you can end it.

### Mistake: releasing a voice clone of a real artist without their approval

**Spotify allows vocal impersonation only with the impersonated artist's authorisation, and Tennessee law can reach unauthorised voice use.**

Spotify's September 2025 policy says vocal impersonation is allowed in music on its service only when the artist whose voice is imitated has authorised it. Tennessee's ELVIS Act, in force since 1 July 2024, covers real or simulated voices. Other places have other rules. A track that imitates a named artist's voice without approval breaks Spotify's policy, and the artist may also have a legal claim. A music lawyer can advise on a specific case.

- Get written approval from an artist before releasing a model of their voice.
- Use your own voice or a licensed voice model.

## If you use AI in your music

A working list for a songwriter, artist, producer or admin who uses AI tools. It is not legal advice; ask a music lawyer about contracts.

**Before you start**

- Read the tool's terms on who owns outputs and whether your inputs train its models.
- Decide which parts you will write, play or edit yourself.
- Do not feed in someone else's recording, lyrics or voice without permission.

**While you work**

- Keep dated project files, stems and notes that show your own contribution.
- Save the prompts and settings behind each AI-made element.
- Note which tool and version produced each AI element.

**Before you release**

- Answer your distributor's AI question as it is asked.
- Add AI credits where your distributor and the service support them.
- Avoid a voice that imitates a named artist unless they approved.

**Before you register**

- Register only works that carry your own authorship.
- In the US, use the Standard Application and describe the AI material.
- Answer your society's AI question truthfully, for example GEMA's box.
- Know that ASCAP, BMI, SOCAN and the MLC do not register wholly AI works.

**If a voice or likeness is involved**

- Get written permission from the person before using a model of their voice.
- Have a music lawyer read any voice or likeness licence.

## Terms

Defined on the glossary: https://kitesink.com/music/glossary.md

- **AI-assisted work and AI-generated output**: SOCAN's split: an AI-assisted work is made by a person using AI as a tool; an AI-generated output is made with minimal human involvement and cannot be registered.
- **Digital replica**: In the NO FAKES bill (S. 4591, not law as of November 2026), a newly made, computer-generated, highly realistic copy of a person's voice or likeness. Authorised remixes, samples and remasters are excluded.
- **Text and data mining (TDM)**: Automated analysis of one or more digital works to extract information such as patterns, trends and correlations. In the EU, a commercial use is allowed only where the rightsholder has not reserved it.
- **Training content summary**: The overview of what content trained a general-purpose AI model, which EU rules require its provider to publish in the Commission's template format.
