# Diet: KiteSink fitness field guide

Losing or gaining weight on purpose. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, how well supported it is, and its sources.

## Guidance

### The 3,500 kcal per pound rule is a rough guide that varies with body fat

**About 3,500 kcal (7,700 per kg) per pound of loss is only an average, and leaner people need less per unit lost.**

The rule says a cumulative deficit of 3,500 kcal equals one pound (32.2 MJ per kg) of weight loss. A model found it approximately matches obese people with more than 30 kg of body fat, and it overestimates the deficit needed for people with less body fat. Weight also responds slowly: the half time was about one year in a separate model. Use it to size a plan, then correct against the scale.

- Use the rule to pick a starting deficit, then check the trend over three to four weeks.

Deficit and rate of loss · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Hall 2008](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0803720), [Hall 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(11)60812-X)

### Aim to lose about 0.5 to 1 percent of body weight a week

**A pace of 0.5 to 1 percent of body weight a week is the usual advice for keeping muscle while getting leaner.**

A contest-prep review recommends 0.5 to 1 percent a week, and a later review prefers 0.5 percent or less for attenuating fat-free mass loss. In 24 athletes, both groups lost about 5.5 percent of body weight, but the 0.7 percent weekly group gained 2.1 percent lean mass and lost 31 percent of fat mass, while the group set 1.4 percent (1.0 percent achieved) kept lean mass unchanged and lost 21 percent of fat mass. McDonald's view is that faster loss mainly costs muscle when protein is low and lifting is light. At 80 kg (176 lb), 0.5 to 1 percent is 0.4 to 0.8 kg (0.9 to 1.8 lb).

- Pick a weekly percentage, convert it to kg or lb, and check against your four-week trend.

Deficit and rate of loss · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Helms 2014b](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20), [Garthe 2011](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.21.2.97), [Roberts 2020a](https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2019-0096), [McDonald 2010i](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/muscle-loss-single-digit-bodyfat)

### Deficits much beyond 500 kcal a day cost lean mass in lifters

**In a meta-regression, a deficit of about 500 kcal a day stopped lean-mass gains from training, while strength held up.**

A systematic review and meta-analysis of resistance training in an energy deficit found lean-mass gains were impaired against training without a deficit (effect size -0.57), but strength gains were comparable. The meta-regression put the point where gains were prevented at roughly 500 kcal a day. For people trying to preserve lean mass rather than add it, the authors advise avoiding deficits above that size. Short trials can differ: 40 young men on a 40 percent deficit with intense training gained 1.2 kg of lean mass on 2.4 g/kg of protein in 4 weeks.

- Keep deficits at or below about 500 kcal a day if gaining or keeping muscle is the priority.

Deficit and rate of loss · Intermediate · Well supported · Sources: [Murphy 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14075), [Longland 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.119339)

### Eat more protein when you diet: about 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of lean mass

**Lean lifters in a deficit kept more muscle with higher protein, around 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of fat-free mass.**

A review of six studies in lean, resistance-trained athletes in a deficit put protein needs at 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of fat-free mass, scaled up with the severity of the deficit and the leanness. The ISSN position gives 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of body weight. In a 4-week trial of 40 young men on a large deficit, 2.4 g/kg gained 1.2 kg of lean mass against 0.1 kg on 1.2 g/kg. A meta-analysis of 24 trials found higher protein held on to 0.43 kg more fat-free mass.

- Set protein by your lean mass, and move up as the diet gets harder and you get leaner.

### Keep lifting while dieting, because training tells the body to keep muscle

**Resistance training reduced the lean mass lost in a diet, and strength was largely kept in deficit trials.**

An overview of 12 systematic reviews (149 studies) found resistance training limited lean mass loss during weight loss by about 0.8 kg. In six trials of older adults with obesity, training offset about 93 percent of the lean mass lost to calorie restriction. Strength gains in the deficit trials were comparable with and without a deficit.

- Keep the main lifts heavy, and drop sets before you drop load.

Keeping muscle · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Bellicha 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13256), [Sardeli 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10040423), [Murphy 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14075)

### Short sleep shifts diet loss toward muscle and away from fat

**In ten adults, five and a half hours of sleep cut the fat share of weight lost by 55 percent.**

Ten overweight adults ate a reduced-calorie diet for 14 days in two conditions, with 8.5 or 5.5 hours in bed. Short sleep cut the fraction of weight lost as fat by 55 percent (1.4 vs 0.6 kg) and increased loss of fat-free mass by 60 percent (1.5 vs 2.4 kg). Hunger rose too. It is a very small and short crossover study, so treat it as a strong hint and not a finding.

- Protect seven to nine hours in bed while dieting.

Keeping muscle · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Nedeltcheva 2010](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-153-7-201010050-00006)

### Protein and unprocessed food help control hunger, at least in the short run

**Protein reduced hunger in meal studies, and a ward trial found unprocessed meals led to about 500 kcal less intake.**

A meta-analysis of 49 acute studies found protein lowered hunger and raised fullness, but long-term trials were inconclusive. In a ward study of 20 adults, ultra-processed meals led to 508 kcal a day more intake than unprocessed meals matched on presented calories, sugar, fibre and sodium, with weight up 0.9 kg against down 0.9 kg over two weeks. That is one small, short crossover trial.

- Build meals around protein and whole foods before cutting portion size.

Hunger and fullness · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Kohanmoo 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2020.113123), [Hall 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2019.05.008)

### Appetite rises as you lose weight, so hunger is part of the process

**In one 52-week trial, appetite rose enough to add about 100 kcal a day per kg of weight lost.**

In 153 people losing weight without being aware of the deficit, a model found eating drifted above baseline by about 100 kcal a day for each kilogram lost, more than three times the matching fall in energy expenditure. This is one analysis of one drug trial. It points to appetite, not metabolism, as the main pressure against keeping weight off.

- Plan for hunger to rise late in a diet, and use protein, fibre and a fixed end date.

Hunger and fullness · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Polidori 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.21653)

### Water-rich fruit and vegetables lower hunger for the same calories

**In one year-long trial, women who ate more water-rich foods lost more weight and reported less hunger.**

In 97 women with obesity told to cut fat, one group was also told to eat more water-rich foods, mainly fruit and vegetables, with no calorie goal. Among completers after a year, that group lost 7.9 kg against 6.4 kg, ate a greater weight of food at a lower energy density, and reported less hunger. It is one trial in women with obesity, not lifters.

- Fill half the plate with vegetables or fruit before adding the rest.

Hunger and fullness · Beginner · Thin evidence · Sources: [Ello-Martin 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.6.1465), [Hall 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2019.05.008)

### Metabolic slowdown with dieting is real but smaller than most people fear

**Adaptation is real but small, tens to low hundreds of kcal a day in most studies, so check your log first.**

A systematic review of 33 studies (2,528 participants) found adaptive thermogenesis in 27, with lower or non-significant values in the better-designed studies. In 171 women, the drop after loss was 54 kcal a day, and in a subset of 46 it was no longer significant at one and two years. In 32 men on a 50 percent deficit for three weeks, true adaptation was 72 kcal a day. After 6 years, 14 Biggest Loser contestants were 499 kcal a day below prediction, an extreme case.

- Check the log and weekends before blaming metabolism.

### Day-to-day weight changes are mostly water, so read the trend

**Two-week changes of 1 to 3 kg were largely fat-free mass and water, not fat, in free-living adults.**

In 46 adults, a unit of two-week weight change was 84 percent fat-free mass, and the energy density of 1 to 3 kg changes was about 2,380 kcal per kg, well below that of fat. Glycogen is stored with three to four parts water. Weight runs higher on Sunday and Monday in 80 adults, and fluid retention peaks at the start of menstrual flow in 62 women. None of these is fat gain.

- Weigh at the same time under the same conditions, and judge the weekly average.
- If you menstruate, compare the same week of one cycle with the same week of the next.

### A two-week stall is not proof your diet stopped working

**Check logging and weekend intake before cutting calories, since stalls are usually noise or under-logging.**

Ten people who reported not losing weight on under 1,200 kcal actually under-reported intake by 47 percent and over-reported activity by 51 percent. In a one-year trial of 48 adults, calorie-restricted participants stopped losing weight on weekends. Add water noise from the table above and a two-week stall tells you little. A real stall holds over three to four weeks of honest logging.

- Wait three to four weeks, then cut by about 100 to 200 kcal or add steps.

### Weighing yourself regularly goes with more loss inside a programme

**Programmes that included self-weighing beat minimal care by 3.4 kg, and daily weighing did no better than weekly.**

A meta-analysis found 15 trials of multi-component programmes that included self-weighing lost 3.4 kg more than minimal or no intervention, with no significant difference between daily and weekly weighing. Only one study tested self-weighing alone, and it found no evidence of an effect. In two studies of female first-year students, daily weighing with feedback on the trend of the last 7 days kept weight steady while controls gained 3.1 and 2.0 kg. McDonald favours daily weigh-ins read as a running average. The scale is feedback inside a plan.

- Weigh daily or on a fixed schedule, and act on the 7-day average, not on single days.

### Diet breaks and refeeds give mixed fat-loss results and may ease hunger

**Planned weeks at maintenance did not improve fat loss in lifters, and they lowered hunger.**

In 61 resistance-trained adults over 12 weeks, alternating three weeks of restriction with one week at maintenance gave the same fat and lean mass as continuous dieting, with lower hunger. A meta-analysis of 12 trials (881 participants) found the same body composition and a smaller drop in resting rate. A small 7-week trial found a weekly two-day carb refeed kept more lean mass (-0.4 vs -1.3 kg). Results in men with obesity favoured breaks.

- Try a one- to two-week break at maintenance if hunger or training quality is slipping.

### The best diet is the one you can keep to, since types differ little

**Low-carb and low-fat diets lost about the same weight, so pick the one you can keep.**

A network meta-analysis of 48 randomized trials (7,286 people) found weight loss with any low-carbohydrate or low-fat diet, with small differences among named diets. A larger analysis of 121 trials (21,942 patients) found about 4.6 kg at 6 months with low carb and 4.4 kg with low fat. In a 12-month trial of 609 adults, the difference was 0.7 kg, not significant. Behavioural support added about 3.2 kg at six months in one analysis.

- Choose the pattern that fits your food preferences and schedule.

### Intermittent fasting works by eating less, with no clear extra effect

**Time-restricted eating did not beat plain calorie control in long trials, though one pooled analysis disagrees.**

In 139 adults over 12 months, eating 8 am to 4 pm with calorie restriction lost 8.0 kg against 6.3 kg for calorie restriction alone, not a significant difference. A 12-week trial of 116 people found no advantage, and a 41-person feeding trial matched calories and found no effect on weight. A pooled analysis of 30 studies did report 1.46 kg more loss in matched comparisons. With lifting, an analysis of eight studies (221 participants) kept fat-free mass.

- Use a fasting window only if it makes eating less easier for you.

### Meal frequency and late eating matter little for fat loss at equal calories

**Differences between meal patterns are small at equal calories; fewer meals showed about 1 kg more loss in one analysis.**

A network meta-analysis of 22 trials (647 people) found two meals a day slightly lowered weight against three meals or six, by about 1 kg, under equal calories. In 16 obese adults on the same deficit, three meals and three snacks did no better than three meals. A meta-analysis of 9 trials found earlier eating gave 1.2 kg more loss, and a controlled crossover trial found late eating raised hunger. Pick the pattern you can follow.

- Choose a meal pattern around your day, and keep protein in each meal.

### Weight lost tracked sticking to the diet, not which diet it was

**In one year-long trial, weight lost followed how closely people kept to their diet, with no link to the diet they chose.**

In 160 overweight or obese adults with heart-risk factors, assigned to Atkins, Zone, Weight Watchers or Ornish, mean one-year loss was 2.1 to 3.3 kg and only 50 to 65 percent finished. Loss correlated with self-reported adherence (r = 0.60) but not with diet type (r = 0.07). Adherence was self-reported and chosen by the participants, so this shows who did well, not that pushing adherence causes loss.

- Pick the eating pattern you can see yourself keeping for a year.

Diet type and meal pattern · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Dansinger 2005](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.293.1.43), [Johnston 2014](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2014.10397)

### Exercise adds to fat loss, and daily movement drops on diets if you let it

**Exercise adds 1.5 to 3.5 kg of loss in reviews, and people on diets may move less outside the gym.**

An overview of 12 meta-analyses found exercise led to 1.5 to 3.5 kg of weight loss and similar results from aerobic and interval training when energy expenditure matched. The ACSM suggests 150 minutes a week as a floor and 200 to 300 for long-term loss. A systematic review of 36 articles found lower non-exercise activity in 63 percent of diet-only arms. In 16 overfed adults, NEAT explained the large differences in fat gain.

- Set a daily step or activity floor and keep it during the diet.

Cardio and daily movement · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Bellicha 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13256), [DONNELLY 2009](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181949333), [Silva 2018](https://doi.org/10.1017/S000711451800096X), [Levine 1999](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.283.5399.212)

### Getting very lean costs hormones, mood and training quality, so go carefully

**Long spells under about 25 kcal per kg of lean mass led to muscle loss and hormone and mood problems in men. In women, 30 marks rising risk, not a cut-off.**

Reviews of lean athletes link a long period at low energy availability to muscle loss, hormone disruption and psychological problems. In one natural bodybuilder, body fat fell from 14.8 to 4.5 percent and testosterone from 9.22 to 2.27 ng/mL, recovering after the contest. In 27 women dieting for a fitness show, sex hormones fell and most recovered within three to four months. The 30 figure comes from a five-day lab study; in 35 women over three cycles, risk rose steadily with no cut-off, and the 2025 Triad update moves away from a threshold. See the women area. Involve a coach and clinician.

- Treat contest-level leanness as a short, supervised project, not a lifestyle.
- See a clinician if periods stop or mood drops.

### Weight regain is common, and maintenance is possible with sustained habits

**Most people regain some weight. A minority keep most of it off, and they report steady activity and regular weighing.**

In 171 women, 52 percent of the weight lost was regained at one year and 89 percent at two. A meta-analysis of 29 US studies found the average person kept off more than 3 kg at five years. Among 2,886 National Weight Control Registry members, mean loss was 31.3 kg at entry and 23.1 kg at ten years; they report about an hour of activity a day and regular weighing. That link to activity is observational and self-selected: an overview of controlled trials found no significant effect of exercise training on maintenance. McDonald's rule is to return to maintenance promptly, not to climb in small steps for months.

- After the diet, bring calories back to maintenance within about two weeks, then hold there.
- Keep weighing, activity and protein up.

### A small surplus adds muscle, and a large surplus mostly adds fat

**A surplus of about 10 to 20 percent over maintenance is a common starting range for lifters, and nobody has proven the best size.**

A narrative review for physique athletes suggests 10 to 20 percent above maintenance, with advanced lifters more conservative. In 17 lifters over 8 weeks, a 15 percent surplus mainly increased fat gain rather than strength or muscle thickness. Another review finds the ideal surplus is unknown. If maintenance is 2,500 kcal, 10 to 20 percent is 250 to 500 kcal. Evidence is thin and short.

- Start near the low end of the surplus and adjust to the scale.

### Recomposition works best for beginners, returners and people carrying more fat

**Gaining muscle while losing fat is realistic with high protein and hard training, and likely harder for lean, advanced lifters.**

In 40 young men on a roughly 40 percent deficit with heavy training and 2.4 g/kg of protein, lean mass rose 1.2 kg while fat mass fell 4.8 kg over four weeks. A review of six studies found that the groups that held on to or gained lean mass were among those with the highest body fat, the smallest deficit or a new training stimulus. Advanced lifters with low body fat have less room.

- Use a small deficit or maintenance with high protein and progressive training.

### Size the deficit as a share of maintenance, not a fixed calorie number

**A percentage cut scales with the person: 20 percent is 360 kcal a day at 1,800 kcal maintenance and 600 kcal at 3,000.**

A fixed 500 kcal cut is more than a quarter of a small woman's intake and a sixth of a big man's. McDonald sets deficits as a share of maintenance and calls 10 to 15 percent small, 20 to 25 percent moderate and anything beyond 25 percent large. The same percentage gives a larger person a bigger daily gap, so they lose faster on paper. In resistance-training trials, lean-mass gains stopped at a deficit of roughly 500 kcal a day, and a 25 percent cut at high maintenance can pass that point.

- Estimate maintenance and take 20 to 25 percent off; if you lift, check the result against the 500 kcal point.

### Losing weight quickly did not mean regaining it faster in a long trial

**Of 200 adults with obesity, those who reached the target had regained about 71 percent by week 144, whether the loss was rapid or gradual.**

A 12-week rapid and a 36-week gradual programme, both run through a hospital, aimed for 15 percent loss. Of the rapid group 81 percent reached 12.5 percent, against 50 percent of the gradual group; only they were followed, and completers had regained 70.5 and 71.2 percent by week 144. One person in the rapid group needed gallbladder surgery. In 262 obese, middle-aged women in a lifestyle programme, the fastest losers in month one had lost most at 18 months, an association only. McDonald argued in 2009 that fast loss can last if it builds habits; the trial came later and found no regain penalty, not an advantage.

- Only consider a rapid diet inside a supervised programme with a clinician; it carries risks such as gallstones.
- Lean lifters should still use the slower pace in the rate-of-loss entry.

### A fixed calorie cut slows to a plateau because the gap closes as you shrink

**On a fixed cut a shrinking body burns less, so loss slows and stops. Supervised dieters fell about a quarter short of the 3,500 rule in about 65 days.**

Each kg lost early holds less energy than fat, because water and lean tissue go too (see the week-four energy number). Even so, 103 adults with supervised or measured intake, on deficits near 1,440 kcal a day, lost 20.1 lb (9.1 kg) in about 65 days, against 27.6 lb (12.5 kg) predicted by the rule. For an average overweight adult, a validated model puts each lasting change of 100 kJ (24 kcal) a day at about 1 kg of eventual weight, half within a year. Eating less also trims the energy spent digesting food. To keep the pace, reopen the gap with less food or more movement.

- Use a dynamic planner, such as the NIH Body Weight Planner, for forecasts beyond a few weeks.

### Set up a fat-loss diet in order: calories, protein, fat, then carbohydrate

**Fix the calorie target first, then protein, then a moderate fat intake, and let carbohydrate fill whatever is left.**

A review for natural bodybuilders in contest prep sets calories for a loss of 0.5 to 1 percent of body weight a week, protein at 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of lean mass, fat at 15 to 30 percent of calories and carbohydrate as the remainder. McDonald uses the same order, holds that fatter dieters need less protein per kg, and starts fat at 20 to 25 percent of calories because very low-fat diets are hard to stick to. Example: 75 kg (165 lb) on 2,200 kcal, with 165 g protein (660 kcal) and 61 g fat (550 kcal), leaves about 248 g of carbohydrate. When calories must drop, carbohydrate usually gives way first.

- Write down the four numbers before you choose foods.

### Athletes in a deficit should keep food around hard sessions and cut elsewhere

**When an athlete trims carbohydrate to diet, the cut should come from meals away from hard training, not from the meals that fuel it.**

A review of weight management in athletes lists eating around exercise among its core strategies, next to avoiding severe restriction and choosing foods of low energy density. It warns that severe restriction during hard training lowers strength, glycogen stores, concentration and the response to training. McDonald's view is that skipping food before or after hard sessions to save calories only hurts training, because the deficit sets fat loss either way. Easy, low-intensity sessions are the exception.

- Keep some carbohydrate and protein before and after hard sessions, and take cuts from other meals.

### Athletes should diet in a low-intensity training block, not close to competition

**Plan fat loss for the off-season or a general preparation block, and aim for a weight you can hold for most of the year.**

A review on athletes' weight advises finding a healthy weight that can be kept for most of the year, so little has to come off before competition, and allowing enough time to get there safely. McDonald places fat loss in the general preparation phase, when volume is high and intensity lower, using only a small calorie cut. In his view most sports have an ideal leanness, and going below it costs muscle and performance. The IOC statement on REDs links low energy availability to health and performance harm.

- Map the season and pick the diet weeks first.
- Choose a target weight you can hold without constant dieting.
- If periods, mood or performance change while dieting, see a sports doctor or dietitian.

### Flexible control of eating goes with lower weight and fewer binges than rigid rules

**In large surveys, people who allowed limited amounts of any food weighed less and binged less than people who ran on strict food rules.**

Among 54,517 people in a weight-loss programme and a population sample of 1,838 aged 14 and over, rigid control was linked with higher BMI and more frequent, more severe binges. Flexible control was linked with lower BMI, fewer binges and a better chance of losing weight over a year. In 188 non-obese women, rigid dieting went with eating disorder symptoms and low mood, and flexible dieting did not. These are associations, not trials, so they show who does well rather than proving the cause.

- Plan some favourite foods into the week instead of banning them.
- If food rules or binges feel out of your control, see a doctor or an eating disorder specialist.

### When the trend says change, move calories about 10 percent and wait again

**Adjust intake or activity in steps of about a tenth, then reassess after two to three weeks, or four if water swings hide the trend.**

Lifts falling with sound training, or loss well beyond the plan, means ease off: McDonald adds about 10 percent to calories or drops some cardio, as falling strength can signal muscle loss. Loss short of the plan after about three weeks of honest logging means cut intake or add activity by about a tenth, then look again in two to three weeks. A rate on target needs nothing. Take a break at maintenance if intake is already low or sleep, mood or training are slipping (see the very-lean entry). - Change one thing at a time.

### Holiday weight gain is small on average, but it tends to stay

**In 195 adults, weight rose about 0.4 kg over the winter holidays, far below the 2.3 kg (5 lb) often claimed, and the gain was not lost in the months after.**

Adults were weighed from late September to early March and again the following autumn. The mean gain from mid-November to January was 0.37 kg, with no significant change before or after, and the season's net 0.48 kg had not reversed by the next autumn in the 165 who returned. The spread was wide, about 1.5 kg either way. McDonald's view is that the jump after one large meal is mostly carbohydrate, salt and water, not fat. His advice is to plan a few treats, start parties with protein and vegetables, and not write off the season after one rich meal.

- Weigh yourself a week after a holiday meal, not the next morning.
- Choose the treats you most want rather than grazing on all of them.

### Skipping breakfast did not hurt weight control in short trials

**In a meta-analysis of short trials, people assigned to breakfast ate about 260 kcal a day more and weighed slightly more than people assigned to skip it.**

In randomised trials in adults, breakfast eaters weighed 0.44 kg more (7 trials) and took in about 260 kcal a day more (10 trials), though trials were short and mostly of low quality. The energy spent digesting food depends on how much you eat, not how often. McDonald's view is that smaller women on low intakes often do better with three or four filling meals than with six tiny ones. Skipping breakfast differs from eating late, which the meal timing entry covers.

- Eat breakfast if it keeps you full to lunch, and skip it if it does not.

### Planned time off the diet did not cut total weight loss in a trial

**In 142 adults in a behavioural weight-loss programme, scheduled breaks from dieting left total loss at 5 and 11 months unchanged.**

Researchers told one group to stop all weight-loss efforts for six weeks midway, and another for two weeks three times, hoping to provoke relapse. Logging, dieting and self-weighing lapsed in the breaks (exercise did not), loss slowed or reversed slightly, and people resumed quickly. Overall loss matched the control group. McDonald's view is that a break the dieter chooses keeps a sense of control, so a holiday that would derail the diet anyway is better planned as one. See the diet break entry for trials at maintenance.

- If a holiday or busy spell will stop your diet, McDonald's practice is to plan it as a break with an end date.

### McDonald keeps carbohydrate at 120 to 150 g a day or more on a diet break

**McDonald sets a carbohydrate floor of about 120 to 150 g a day for a diet break, on the view that thyroid hormone needs it.**

In obese patients on low-calorie diets of 360 to 1,200 kcal, blood T3, the active thyroid hormone, fell only when carbohydrate was 120 g a day or less, whatever the calories, and reverse T3 stayed raised at 40 to 50 g. It is one 1982 study of hormone levels during dieting, not of fat loss, and it did not test a break at maintenance. McDonald's view is that a break kept very low carb misses part of its point. Fat-loss results from breaks in trials are mixed (see the diet break entry).

- On a break, McDonald's practice is at least 120 to 150 g of carbohydrate a day.

### McDonald spaces diet breaks by body fat, so leaner dieters take them sooner

**In McDonald's practice, lean dieters take a break every 4 to 6 weeks, while people carrying more fat go 12 to 16 weeks between breaks.**

His reasoning is that the hormonal and metabolic response to dieting grows as people get leaner (see the bodycomp entry on lean tissue loss speeding up), so the leanest need relief most often. He sets the break near estimated maintenance, often 5 to 10 percent under it, for 10 to 14 days, and wants contest dieters to start early enough to fit one in. No trial has compared spacing by body fat, and fat-loss results from breaks in trials are mixed (see the diet break entry).

- McDonald's advice: put the break in the timeline before the diet starts, so a deadline cannot squeeze it out.

### Higher protein after a diet halved weight regain in one trial

**In 148 overweight to moderately obese adults after a diet, about 48 g a day of extra protein halved regain over 3 months, and the regain was fat-free mass.**

Participants first lost weight on a 4-week very low-energy diet of about 500 kcal a day, an intake for medical supervision only, then were randomised for 3 months of maintenance. The extra-protein group ate 18 percent of energy as protein against 15 percent, regained half as much weight, consisting only of fat-free mass, and felt fuller while energy intake did not differ. McDonald keeps protein at the dieting level after a diet ends, mainly because post-diet hunger is high. This was one trial in overweight to moderately obese adults; lean lifters were not studied.

- McDonald's practice: keep protein at your dieting level for the first weeks of maintenance.

### After a physique contest, regain came first as water and later mostly as fat

**In 15 natural physique athletes, weight and body water rose soon after competing, and fat mass climbed about 2 kg by 4 to 6 weeks later.**

Seven men and eight women were measured within a week before a show, within a week after and 4 to 6 weeks later. Weight rose 2 kg between the first two visits, body water rose about 1.9 L in the 8 athletes tested for it, and lean mass by ultrasound rose and then held. By the last visit fat mass had gone from 7.7 to 10.0 kg. Resting metabolic rate, 92 percent of predicted before the show, was back near normal. It was a pilot study. McDonald's view is that the post-diet 'rebound' only regains lost muscle and glycogen, not new size.

- McDonald's practice: return to maintenance calories before any surplus; see the entry on dieting with no end plan.

### McDonald puts the low days of an intermittent restriction plan on rest days

**Two very low days a week lost as much as a daily cut in overweight women. McDonald's view is that the low days must miss hard training.**

In 107 premenopausal women with overweight or obesity, about 2,710 kJ (650 kcal) on 2 days a week lost 6.4 kg over 6 months against 5.6 kg on a daily 25 percent cut, a difference that was not significant. McDonald's view is that this kind of plan, or alternate-day fasting with low days near a quarter of maintenance, only suits a lifter whose low days can fall on rest days. The trial did not test placement or include lifters. Days near 650 kcal are a large cut; with a medical condition or medication, check with a clinician first.

- McDonald's practice: set low days on rest days, or choose a daily cut if your training days are fixed.

### Gut bacteria went with a difference of about 150 kcal in energy absorbed

**In 21 inpatients, shifts in gut bacteria went with changes in the calories lost in stool, about 150 kcal in lean people.**

Twelve lean and nine obese adults ate diets of 2,400 and 3,400 kcal on a ward while stool energy was measured. Changing the amount of food changed the gut bacteria quickly, and in lean participants a 20 percent rise in one bacterial group went with about 150 kcal more energy harvested. ### Alcohol cut the fat burned by about a third on the day it was drunk

**In 8 men in a chamber, alcohol at a quarter of daily energy cut fat burning by about a third that day, while carbohydrate and protein burning held.**

On the test day the men drank about 96 g of alcohol, either on top of their usual food or in place of some fat and carbohydrate. The authors concluded that drinking beyond energy needs probably favours fat storage. McDonald's view is that the body keeps no store of alcohol, so it is always burned off. ### The higher protein in low-carb diets, not the low carb, drove loss in one trial

**In 132 adults with obesity, high-protein diets lost more over a year than normal-protein ones, while low-carb against normal-carb made no difference.**

Four diets crossed protein (about 1.1 against 0.7 g per kg) with carbohydrate (low or normal), on a third of energy needs for 3 months, a very low intake, then two thirds for 9 months. At 12 months the high-protein groups had lost 12.8 kg against 8.9 kg, low-carb groups did no better than normal-carb ones, and changes were unrelated to fat content. McDonald's view is that low-carb diets may work mainly through the protein they bring, and that more protein is the single most useful diet change. Intakes that low belong under medical supervision.

- Set protein first, then choose carbohydrate and fat to suit your food preferences.

### The same surplus added 4 to 13 kg in different men, and twins responded alike

**Twelve pairs of young identical twin men overfed by 1,000 kcal a day gained 4.3 to 13.3 kg, with brothers more alike than unrelated men.**

The men were overfed on 84 days within a 100-day period, about 84,000 kcal in total. Mean gain was 8.1 kg. ### A ketogenic diet lost no more fat than a moderate-carb diet in a controlled trial

**In 20 sedentary adults with obesity on controlled food for 6 weeks, ketogenic and moderate-carb diets did not differ significantly in weight or fat lost.**

One diet began near 5 percent of energy as carbohydrate and 60 percent fat; the other was about 40 percent carbohydrate and 30 percent fat. ### In a diet, McDonald cuts heavy volume from the start and keeps the load

**McDonald's practice is to drop heavy volume by up to two thirds when a diet begins, hold the usual loads, and fill any time saved with cardio or light work.**

His reasoning is that recovery falls in a deficit and a few quality heavy sets beat many poor ones. Support comes from maintenance studies outside a deficit (see the maintenance dose entry in recovery). In one trial of 70 adults, a third or even a ninth of the build-up dose kept muscle size for 32 weeks in those aged 20 to 35, but not in those aged 60 to 75. - McDonald's practice: in a diet, keep 2 to 4 heavy sets per muscle group, as little as once a week, at your usual loads.

### Antidepressants differ in their effect on weight, so raise changes with the prescriber

**A meta-analysis of 116 studies linked some antidepressants to weight gain and others to little change or slight loss, with wide individual variation.**

Amitriptyline, mirtazapine and paroxetine went with a greater risk of gain. Fluoxetine and bupropion went with some loss, for fluoxetine only early in treatment. McDonald's view is that these drugs change weight mainly through appetite, sometimes activity, so ordinary diet and activity changes still apply. A weight change is a reason to talk to the prescriber, never to stop or switch a medicine on your own.

### After surgery or injury, McDonald's advice is to heal first and diet later

**In McDonald's view, healing raises energy needs and a deficit may slow repair, so a little fat gained while recovering is the smaller problem.**

He advises eating at maintenance or slightly above while recovering, and dieting only after the injury has healed. Whether to lose fat before a planned operation is a decision for the surgical team, who may also set rules on eating, fasting and supplements beforehand. This entry rests on his judgment, not on a trial cited here.

## Common mistakes

### Losing over 1 percent of body weight a week without high protein and heavy lifting

**Bigger deficits take a larger share of the loss from lean mass, and athletes who lost more slowly gained lean mass. Going faster needs high protein and heavy lifting.**

In 24 athletes lifting four times a week, a group set to lose 1.4 percent a week (1.0 percent achieved) kept lean mass unchanged and cut fat mass 21 percent, while a 0.7 percent loss gained 2.1 percent lean mass and cut fat mass 31 percent. A review found that lean tissue makes up a growing share of the loss as the deficit deepens, and a bigger share in leaner people. McDonald's view is that the old warnings about speed came from low-protein diets without lifting, and that lean dieters with high protein and heavy loads can go faster for short spells. Reviews recommend 0.5 to 1 percent a week.

- Use 0.5 to 1 percent of body weight a week as the default pace.
- If you go faster, keep protein high, keep lifting heavy and keep the fast spell short.

### Cutting protein and stopping lifting while dieting

**The two habits that protect muscle in a deficit are protein and heavy training, and dropping both costs lean mass.**

Higher protein held on to lean mass in trials of lean lifters, and training reduced lean loss by about 0.8 kg across an overview of reviews.

- Keep protein high and lifts heavy until the diet ends.

### Cutting calories after a short stall

**Short stalls are mostly water and weekend noise, and cutting on them stacks deficit on top of a working plan.**

A two-week plateau can come from water, a missed weekend or logging drift, as 48 adults who stopped losing on weekends showed. Cutting calories on a stall moves you toward the 500 kcal limit sooner. McDonald's view is that cutting harder can also add stress-related water retention, which hides fat loss for longer. Wait three to four weeks, check the log, and only then change calories or steps.

- Check the four-week trend and the log before changing calories.

### Dieting with no end date and no plan for after

**Regain follows the diet for most people, and a plan for the exit matters as much as the deficit.**

In 171 women, 52 percent of lost weight was regained after one year and 89 percent after two. Registry members who kept it off kept activity, regular weighing and steady eating. Set an end date for the diet. McDonald's rule for the exit is to reach maintenance calories at once or within about two weeks, then hold there; no study here tests a slower climb.

- Plan the exit: a target date, a maintenance range and a weekly weigh-in.
- Return to maintenance calories at once or within about two weeks.

### Treating a gaining phase as a licence to eat anything

**A large surplus mainly added fat in a trial, and extra fat has to be dieted off later.**

In 17 lifters, a 15 percent surplus mostly increased fat gain rather than strength or muscle thickness. A review suggests 10 to 20 percent for physique athletes, less for advanced lifters. The extra fat then has to be dieted off.

- Use a measured surplus and watch the rate of weight gain.

### Running a 10 percent deficit while logging food loosely

**A deficit of 200 to 300 kcal a day is within reach of label and logging error, so it can vanish without you noticing.**

US rules let a label run up to 20 percent over the declared calories. Restaurant calories were accurate on average, but 19 percent of 269 items held at least 100 kcal more per portion than stated. In a review of 59 studies against doubly labelled water, most studies found people under-reported intake (see the label tolerance and under-reporting entries). On 2,000 kcal a day, a 10 percent error cancels a 10 percent deficit. McDonald's view is that small deficits suit lean, experienced dieters who weigh their food; for others a larger cut leaves room for error.

- Weigh food and log everything if you choose a small deficit.
- If your logging is rough, use a 20 to 25 percent cut instead.

**An early drop on low carb is mostly stored glycogen and the water held with it, and fat loss did not speed up on a ketogenic diet in a ward study.**

### Treating one off-plan snack as a ruined diet

**A 200 kcal treat is under a tenth of a 3,500 kcal weekly deficit. Giving up on the day because of it is what costs you.**

All-or-nothing rules make a small lapse feel like failure. McDonald describes a common response: the day feels lost, so the person eats freely until it ends. - After an unplanned treat, carry on with the next meal, or trim it slightly.
- Call planned off-plan meals free meals, and plan them in advance.

### Dropping whole fruit from a fat-loss diet

**In a six-week trial in adults with obesity, a calorie-restricted diet with fruit lost more weight than one that kept fructose very low.**

In 131 adults, mostly women, on restricted diets of 1,500 to 2,000 kcal, a group eating under 20 g of fructose a day lost 2.83 kg, while a group getting 50 to 70 g from natural fruit lost 4.19 kg. - Keep a few portions of whole fruit in the day and count them in your calories.
- Choose whole fruit over juice.

### Cutting out dairy for a diet or contest prep

**Dairy did not change weight overall in a meta-analysis, but in calorie-restricted trials it gave slightly more weight and fat loss.**

Across 29 randomised trials (2,101 people), dairy had no overall effect on weight. In long-term trials or ones without a calorie limit it went with slightly more weight, so count it. McDonald's guess is that dropping it before shows dates from bulking on whole milk; he found no mechanism, and adds that dairy is a main source of calcium. ### Setting protein as a percentage of calories during a diet

**A fixed share of calories gives less protein as calories fall, just when lean lifters in a deficit likely need more. Set protein in grams per kg instead.**

At 25 percent of calories, protein is 175 g on 2,800 kcal but 125 g on 2,000 kcal, a drop of 50 g. A review of six studies in lean, resistance-trained athletes in a deficit concluded the need likely rises with the size of the deficit and with leanness, and set it per kg of fat-free mass. McDonald makes the same point for every nutrient: needs follow body size, so a percentage means little until you know the calories behind it. Fix protein in grams first and share out the rest.

- Fix protein in grams first, for example 2.3 to 3.1 g per kg of lean mass for a lean lifter in a deficit.

Keeping muscle · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Helms 2014a](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2013-0054), [McDonald 2008k](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/problem-dieting-by-percentages), [McDonald 2007a](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/comparing-the-diets)

### Eating more of a food because it is labelled low-fat or diet-approved

**Women told a yogurt was low-fat ate more at the next meal than after a yogurt of similar calories labelled high-fat.**

The study had 48 healthy, non-dieting women, and half were shown fat-content labels. In that half the label changed lunch intake, and intake with dinner counted was still higher after the 'low-fat' yogurt. Women given no label showed the opposite pattern. McDonald's view is that every popular diet soon gets its own approved snacks and bars, often as calorie-dense as what they replace, and that people eat more of them because they feel allowed, until loss stalls.

- Log diet-approved foods by their calories, not their label.

Hunger and fullness · Beginner · Thin evidence · Sources: [Shide 1995](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0002-8223(95)00273-1), [McDonald 2017a](https://bodyrecomposition.com/nutrition/introduction-to-dieting)

### Setting a goal of losing a third of your body weight in one go

**Sixty women with obesity aimed to lose 32 percent of their weight. After 48 weeks and a 16 kg loss, nearly half had not reached even the result they called disappointing.**

Before treatment the women named a goal, a dream, a happy, an acceptable and a disappointed weight. They rated a 17 kg loss disappointing and 25 kg acceptable, far beyond the modest losses guidelines recommend. McDonald's view is that goals set too large or too fast make a good result feel like failure, and people quit when progress slows. Expect the rate to fall as you go (see the fixed calorie cut entry).

- Count a lasting loss of about 10 percent as success, the clinical yardstick McDonald cites.
- McDonald's practice: break a large loss into stages with maintenance between them.

Deficit and rate of loss · Beginner · Thin evidence · Sources: [Foster 1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.65.1.79), [McDonald 2016u](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/the-causes-diet-failure), [McDonald 2009ar](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/the-full-diet-break)

## What any fat-loss diet needs before its details matter

McDonald's list. Check a plan against it before worrying about meal timing or macro ratios. The first group is essential; the second decides whether you can keep it up.

**Essential**

- A real energy deficit
- Enough protein for your body fat and training
- Some essential fats
- Fruit and vegetables for vitamins and minerals

**Makes it last**

- Hunger you can live with
- Foods you actually like
- A plan for lapses and breaks
- Training quality you can hold
- Habits you can carry into maintenance

## Terms

- **Adaptive thermogenesis (metabolic adaptation)**: A fall in energy use beyond what the loss of body mass explains, seen during and after weight loss. Studies find it ranges from small to modest.
- **Diet break and refeed**: A diet break is a planned stretch, often one to two weeks, at maintenance calories. A refeed is shorter, usually one or two days of higher carbohydrate.
- **Energy deficit**: Eating less energy than you use. It is the one requirement for losing body fat. A daily deficit of 500 kcal is about 3,500 kcal a week.
- **False hope syndrome**: Polivy and Herman's name for a cycle of repeated self-change attempts driven by unrealistic hopes about how fast, how far and how easily change will come, and what it will bring. Failure is followed by a fresh attempt.
- **Glycaemic index (GI)**: How far a food's carbohydrate raises blood glucose against the same amount from glucose or white bread. Fat, protein and fibre in a meal lower it, and in studies GI did not change how much fat people burned.
- **NEAT (non-exercise activity)**: Non-exercise activity thermogenesis: energy spent on walking, standing, fidgeting and chores. It can fall during a diet without you noticing.
- **Negative calorie illusion (dieter's paradox)**: The mistaken belief that adding a healthy food to an unhealthy meal lowers its calories. In Chernev's studies the error was larger in people most concerned with their weight.
- **Nutrient balance (protein, carbohydrate and fat balance)**: Intake minus oxidation for one nutrient. In 54 adults in a chamber, the body matched carbohydrate and protein burning to intake, so day-to-day surpluses and gaps landed almost entirely on fat balance.
- **Recomposition**: Gaining muscle and losing fat over the same period. Body weight may hold steady or fall while the body changes shape.
- **Rigid and flexible restraint**: Two styles of holding back on food. Rigid control runs on fixed rules and forbidden foods, all or nothing. Flexible control allows any food in limited amounts and evens out over days.
- **Set point and settling point**: Two models of body weight: a defended set point, or a settling point where weight drifts with food and activity. A review finds both incomplete. McDonald's view is that the body resists loss far more than gain.
- **Targeted and cyclical ketogenic diets**: Ketogenic diets adapted for hard training. A targeted version adds a small dose of carbohydrate around sessions; a cyclical one alternates several low-carbohydrate days with one or two days of high carbohydrate.
- **Time-restricted eating**: Eating all meals within a set daily window, such as eight hours, and fasting for the rest. It is one form of intermittent fasting.
