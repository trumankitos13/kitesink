# Powerlifting: KiteSink fitness field guide

A bigger squat, bench press and deadlift. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, how well supported it is, and its sources.

## Guidance

### Heavy loads build strength better than light loads

**For strength, heavy and moderate loads beat light loads, so most working sets should sit in the heavy to moderate range.**

A network meta-analysis of 28 studies in 747 healthy adults, all taken to failure, found muscle growth was similar across loads. Strength gains were larger with high loads (8 repetitions or fewer to failure) and moderate loads (9 to 15) than with low loads (over 15). High versus moderate was not clearly different. Light work still has a place for skill and recovery.

- Keep most working sets on the competition lifts in the 1-8 repetition range.
- Use light sets for warm-ups, skill work and easy days.

Percentages and zones · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Lopez 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000002585)

### Practise the lifts in competition style and hold four principles

**Strength in the squat, bench press and deadlift is partly skill, so the lifts themselves, done to competition standard, make up most of the training.**

This is coaching convention, not a trial result. Train squat depth, the bench pause and the deadlift lockout the way they are judged. Four principles apply to all three lifts: a stable base, full-body tightness before the bar moves, a bar path close to a straight line over mid-foot, and the same set-up every rep. Assistance exercises support the lifts and do not replace them.

- Film your heaviest set of each lift every week, from the side.
- Use the same set-up routine on warm-ups and on heavy sets.

Technique and leverages · Beginner · Coaching practice

### RPE with reps in reserve lets you pick the load by feel

**A set rated RPE 9 left one repetition in reserve and RPE 10 left none; the scale worked on all three lifts in powerlifters.**

The scale pairs effort with repetitions in reserve (RIR): RPE 10 is 0 RIR and RPE 9 is 1 RIR. In 29 lifters, experienced squatters rated a 1RM higher than novices, and bar speed fell as RPE rose. In 15 powerlifters, RPE at 1RM was 9.6 to 9.7 on all three lifts, and RPE tracked percent of 1RM closely (r = 0.88 to 0.91).

- Rate each top set right after it ends, before looking at the bar speed or the numbers.

RPE and velocity · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Zourdos 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001049), [Helms 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001517)

### Loading by RPE or bar speed works about as well as percentages

**Trials and a meta-analysis found no clear strength difference between autoregulated loading and fixed-percentage loading.**

A meta-analysis found no significant 1RM difference between autoregulated and standard percentage loading in resistance-trained people (mean difference 2.07 kg, 95% CI -0.32 to 4.46). In an 8-week trial of 21 trained men, RPE-based and percent-based loading both raised squat and bench press 1RM, and the gap between them was not significant. Both are valid ways to set load.

- Use RPE when daily readiness swings; use percentages when you want a fixed plan to compare weeks.

RPE and velocity · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Hickmott 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9), [Helms 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247), [Larsen 2021](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.10663)

### Velocity loss limits did not change strength gains in a large review

**Ending a set when bar speed has dropped by a set percentage is a valid way to cap fatigue; the threshold changed strength little.**

A meta-analysis of 19 longitudinal studies found the velocity loss chosen within sets did not change strength gains, while higher velocity loss went with more muscle growth. A second meta-analysis found thresholds of 25% or less gave slightly more 1RM strength (2.32 kg, 95% CI 0.33 to 4.31). Bar speed at 1RM differs by lift (0.23, 0.10 and 0.14 m/s for squat, bench and deadlift in 15 powerlifters), so each lift needs its own profile.

- Record bar speed on your own lifts for several weeks before using a velocity cut-off.

RPE and velocity · Advanced · Some support · Sources: [Jukic 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01754-4), [Hickmott 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9), [Helms 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001517)

### Strength does not need sets taken to failure

**Stopping sets short of failure gave similar strength gains to failure training, so heavy work can end with repetitions in reserve.**

A meta-analysis of 8 studies found non-failure training gave a 0.6 to 1.3% larger strength increase than failure training, a gap the authors judged unlikely to be meaningful. A later meta-regression found a negligible link between reps in reserve and strength gain, although muscle growth did show a negative slope with reps in reserve, meaning more growth nearer failure.

- Stop heavy sets with 1-3 repetitions in reserve; a missed rep costs recovery and, on the platform, a lift.

RPE and velocity · Intermediate · Well supported · Sources: [Davies 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0451-3), [Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)

### More weekly sets build more strength, with sharply diminishing returns

**Strength rises with weekly sets and with training frequency, but the gains flatten faster than muscle growth does.**

A meta-regression of 67 studies (2,058 participants) found strength gains rose with weekly set volume and with frequency, with diminishing returns that were more pronounced for strength than for muscle size. An earlier meta-analysis found high weekly sets beat low weekly sets (effect size 0.18), and a frequency meta-analysis found no significant effect once volume was equated.

- Add sets only when progress stalls, and add them to the lift that is lagging.

Frequency and volume · Intermediate · Well supported · Sources: [Pelland 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w), [Ralston 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0762-7), [Ralston 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9)

### A small dose of heavy work can still raise a powerlifter's 1RM

**About 3 to 6 heavy working sets per lift each week was enough to expect strength gains in powerlifters over 6 to 12 weeks.**

A multi-part study (interviews, surveys and two interventions with 25 intermediate to advanced powerlifters) proposed a minimum effective dose: about 3 to 6 working sets of 1 to 5 repetitions per lift each week, spread over 1 to 3 sessions, at loads above 80% of 1RM and RPE 7.5 to 9.5, for 6 to 12 weeks. It describes a floor for busy periods, not an ideal.

- Use this dose in busy or injured weeks, not as a long-term plan.

Frequency and volume · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Androulakis-Korakakis 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655)

### Training days per week usually rise with level

**Beginners do well on 2 to 3 sessions a week; advanced lifters often spread the lifts across 4 to 6.**

This is coaching convention. Beginners can practise each lift two or three times a week with little fatigue. Intermediates commonly train 3 to 4 days, with each lift twice a week. In a survey of 117 Norwegian powerlifters that included world, European and national champions, the most common answer (58.1%) was 5 to 6 sessions a week, with the most common load at 71-80% of 1RM.

- Add a session only when recovery between sessions is good and the lifts are improving.

Frequency and volume · Beginner · Coaching practice · Sources: [Shaw 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003584)

### Planned variation beats a fixed routine for 1RM strength

**Periodised programs raised 1RM more than non-periodised ones, including when total volume was matched.**

A meta-analysis of 18 studies (81 effects) found periodised training gave larger 1RM gains than non-periodised training (effect size 0.43). A later meta-analysis of 35 volume-equated studies agreed for strength (effect size 0.31) but found no difference for muscle size. These trials were short, so the long-term picture in elite lifters is less clear.

- Change volume and intensity on a plan, not at random.

Periodisation and peaking · Intermediate · Well supported · Sources: [Williams 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0734-y), [Moesgaard 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1)

### Linear, block and undulating plans differ little in trials

**No clear winner between linear and undulating models; too few trials exist to compare block models.**

Linear plans raise load week by week, block plans stack phases (volume, then intensity, then peaking), and daily undulating plans vary reps and load between sessions. A meta-analysis of 17 studies (510 participants) found no difference between linear and undulating for strength. A later one found a small edge for undulating in trained people (effect size 0.61, 95% CI 0.00 to 1.22) and could not compare other models.

- Pick the model you will follow for a full cycle and judge it on your own logbook.

Periodisation and peaking · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Harries 2015](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000000712), [Moesgaard 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1)

### Lifters taper for 5 to 10 days by cutting volume and keeping heavy singles

**Surveys report a short step taper: volume down about 40-50%, intensity kept high, last heavy squat and deadlift 7-10 days out.**

These are self-reports, not trials proving a taper works. In 364 North American raw powerlifters, a step taper over 7 to 10 days with 41-50% less volume was typical, with full rest about 2.8 days before the meet. A small trial of 19 athletes found 3 or 5 days of full rest kept lower-body strength; upper-body strength held at 3 days but not 5.

- Cut volume sharply but keep the competition lifts and a few heavy singles.
- Rest fully for the last 2 to 3 days before the meet.

Periodisation and peaking · Advanced · Thin evidence · Sources: [Travis 2021a](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004177), [Winwood 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02500-w), [Travis 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004183)

### Most strength athletes deload for about a week every 5 to 6 weeks

**Deloading is common but little tested: athletes cut sets and load, keep frequency and exercises, and add reps in reserve.**

An expert Delphi (34 coaches began, 21 finished the third round) defined a deload as a period of reduced training stress to mitigate fatigue and promote recovery. A survey of 246 strength and physique athletes found all deloaded, typically for 6.4 days every 5.6 weeks, keeping frequency and exercises. No trial has yet shown the best length or timing.

- Plan a lighter week roughly every 5 to 6 weeks, and move it earlier when joints ache and performance stalls.

Periodisation and peaking · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Bell 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0), [Rogerson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y)

### Low-bar squats lean forward and load the hips; high-bar squats stay upright

**Bar position changes the squat: low-bar uses more hip and back, high-bar more knee and quadriceps.**

A review of back squat biomechanics found high-bar squats showed more knee bend, less hip bend, a more upright torso and greater depth. Low-bar squats showed more hip bend and forward lean, with more erector spinae, adductor and gluteal activity. A systematic review of classic powerlifting research suggested low-bar squats generally let lifters move more weight.

- Try each position for several weeks and keep the one you can repeat at competition depth.

Technique and leverages · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Glassbrook 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002007), [Ferland 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003099)

### Build the bench from the setup: tight back, planted feet, paused bar

**A stable arch and tight upper back shorten the bar path and make the pause on the chest repeatable.**

This is coaching convention. Keep head, shoulders and buttocks on the bench and feet flat on the floor. Pull the shoulder blades together and down, grip with the thumbs around the bar, and lower under control to the lower chest. Pause until the press command, then press back over the shoulders. Wider grips shorten the range, but federations cap grip width, so check the rulebook.

- Pause every training rep on the chest for the same count as the meet.

Technique and leverages · Beginner · Coaching practice

### Sumo and conventional deadlifts load the joints differently

**Sumo uses a wide stance and a more upright trunk; conventional moves the bar farther and does more mechanical work.**

In a video analysis of 12 sumo and 12 conventional lifters at a national championship, sumo lifters used a wider stance (70 cm against 32 cm; about 28 against 13 in), turned the feet out more and kept a more upright trunk. Bar distance, mechanical work and predicted energy cost were about 25 to 40% greater for conventional. The study did not test which style lifts more.

- Test both styles for a few weeks and keep the one that moves more weight with a steady back.

Technique and leverages · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Escamilla 2000](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-200007000-00013)

### Limb and torso lengths change which set-up suits you

**Proportions shift the best stance, depth and grip, so copy principles from top lifters and not their exact positions.**

This is coaching convention, not a tested rule. Long thighs relative to the torso tend to lean the squat forward and favour a wider stance. Long arms shorten the pull in the deadlift and lengthen the bench press path; short arms do the reverse. Use these as starting guesses and let filmed sets and bar speed decide.

- Film from the side and compare two set-ups on the same weight before changing for good.

Technique and leverages · Intermediate · Coaching practice

### Choose accessories for the point where the lift slows or fails

**Find where the bar slows, then train the muscles and positions that fail there with 1 to 3 exercises.**

A review describes the sticking point as the position in the range where difficulty rises disproportionately, and says working out its cause helps choose training. Powerlifters in a minimum-dose study typically used 1 to 3 accessory exercises per lift at RPE 7 to 9 and about 6 to 10 repetitions.

- Film a slow or missed rep, locate the sticking point, then pick exercises that train that range.

Accessories and bands · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Kompf 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0615-9), [Androulakis-Korakakis 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655)

### Bands and chains help trained lifters when loads are 80% of 1RM or more

**In a meta-analysis, variable resistance beat constant resistance for trained lifters at 80% of 1RM and above.**

A meta-analysis of 14 studies found variable-resistance training (bands or chains) beat constant resistance for maximum strength (effect size 0.80). In trained people the benefit appeared at 80% of 1RM or more (0.76) and not below (0.00). In surveys, 39% of 28 elite British lifters used bands and 57% chains, and 76.9% of 117 Norwegian lifters used variable resistance.

- Add bands or chains to heavy work once the base technique is stable.

Accessories and bands · Advanced · Some support · Sources: [Lin 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19148559), [Swinton 2009](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e31819424bd), [Shaw 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003584)

### Pick the class you can hold in training; move up when cutting costs lifts

**A lifter whose normal weight sits at a class limit can compete there with little cutting; large cuts are a reason to move up.**

This is coaching convention. Consider moving up when making weight takes more than a modest, gradual diet, when training quality drops in the weeks before weigh-in, or when you are growing into the next class anyway. An ACSM consensus statement on weight-category sports advises classifying athletes by preseason body composition and shortening the gap between weigh-in and competition.

- Weigh yourself in the morning for several weeks and compare the trend with the class limit.

Weight class and cutting · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [Burke 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/JSR.0000000000000831)

### Rapid weight cuts are common in powerlifting, and fluid loss costs strength

**Many lifters cut weight, mostly by fluid methods; fluid loss lowers strength and can harm health.**

Among 64 lifters at the 2018 IPF classic worlds, 83% cut weight (average 2.9% of body mass) and most reported negative effects on mood. In a survey of 321 lifters, 85.8% used rapid weight loss, and doctors and dietitians were rarely an influence. A meta-analysis of 28 manuscripts found hypohydration cut muscle strength by 5.5% on average. Extreme cuts can harm health; involve a sports dietitian or a doctor.

- Do not use fluid restriction, sauna or laxatives to make weight.
- Ask a sports dietitian or doctor before any planned weight loss.

Weight class and cutting · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Kwan 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004199), [Nolan 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003488), [Savoie 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0349-0)

### Short sleep lowers performance, with evening sessions hit harder

**A meta-analysis found sleep loss cut exercise performance by about 7.6% on average.**

Across 69 publications, sleep loss (6 hours or less in 24) lowered performance by a mean of 7.56% (95% CI -11.9 to -3.13), including strength tasks. Tests done in the morning were largely unaffected; those done in the evening were consistently worse. A systematic review of 17 studies found several nights of restricted sleep could cut force in compound lifts.

- Protect sleep in the week before a meet and keep a normal bedtime.

Recovery and injuries · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Craven 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y), [Knowles 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2018.01.012)

### Injury rates look low, but most lifters report pain in the back, shoulder or hip

**Counted as time lost, injuries are infrequent; counted as pain that affects training, most lifters have one.**

A systematic review of 9 studies found 1.0 to 4.4 injuries per 1,000 training hours in powerlifting, with the spine, shoulder and knee most affected. A survey of 104 Swedish subelite lifters counted pain or impaired function and found 70% currently injured and 87% injured in the past year, mostly lumbopelvic, shoulder and hip; only 16% of those currently injured stopped training completely.

- See a physio or doctor for pain that lasts more than a week, and adjust loads while it settles.

Recovery and injuries · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Aasa 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096037), [Strömbäck 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967118771016)

## Common mistakes

### Testing a true max every few weeks

**Frequent max attempts add fatigue and risk without adding much information.**

This is coaching convention. A true max is the heaviest single you can complete with legal technique. It is a hard, slow-to-recover effort, and a bad day gives a misleading number. Work from a training max set from recent RPE 8 to 9 sets, and keep true maxes for meets or planned tests every few months.

- Test at a meet or a planned test day, not on impulse.

Percentages and zones · Intermediate · Coaching practice

### Running percentages off an old or lucky max

**A percentage is only as good as the max behind it, so reset the number when strength changes.**

This is coaching convention. If the max is months old, the percentages are too light or too heavy, and a lucky one-off single makes the plan too hard to finish. Reps at a given percentage also vary between lifters, so use RPE alongside the plan and rebase when weights feel clearly off.

- Rebase percentages after every cycle using your best recent set, not one lucky rep.

Percentages and zones · Beginner · Coaching practice

### Treating RPE as exact when most lifters misjudge reps in reserve

**People tend to underpredict reps left in the tank, so RPE is a rough guide that improves with practice.**

A meta-analysis of 12 studies (414 participants) found people underpredicted repetitions to failure by 0.95 reps on average, with wide variation. Accuracy improved when the prediction was made nearer failure, with heavier loads and in later sets. A set called RPE 8 may have been closer to RPE 9.

- Calibrate by comparing your RPE with bar speed or filmed sets over several weeks.

RPE and velocity · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Halperin 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x)

### Cutting weight with fluid restriction in the last days

**Fluid restriction is a common but risky way to make weight, and it lowers strength and endurance.**

In the 2018 IPF classic worlds survey, fluid restriction after fluid loading was among the most common methods, used by about a third of lifters, alongside fasting and laxatives. A meta-analysis found hypohydration lowered muscle strength by 5.5% and muscle endurance by 8.3%. Where weigh-in is within about two hours of lifting, there is little time to recover.

- Stay near your class limit with normal eating, and see a sports dietitian before any cut.

Weight class and cutting · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Savoie 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0349-0), [Kwan 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004199)

### Changing technique, equipment or exercises in the final weeks

**Late novelty brings soreness and risk with no time to adapt; keep the final weeks specific and familiar.**

This is coaching convention, and tapering interviews match it. Ten Croatian champions reported taper training that was highly specific, with assistance exercises removed and the same equipment as in competition. New Zealand elite lifters said poor tapers happened when they took a week or more off.

- Wear competition gear in the last heavy sessions and drop new exercises.

Periodisation and peaking · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [Grgic 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001699), [Pritchard 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001292)

## Intensity zones as a percent of 1RM

| Zone | % of 1RM | Typical reps per set | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light | 50-65% | 8-12 or more | Warm-ups, skill work, easy days |
| Moderate | 65-75% | 5-8 | Volume work and technique practice |
| Working | 75-85% | 3-6 | Main strength work |
| Heavy | 85-92% | 1-3 | Heavy strength work, peaking blocks |
| Near-max | 92-100% | 1 | Singles, openers, tests, attempts |

## RPE to reps in reserve chart

RPE 5 to 10 matched to repetitions in reserve (RIR); 9.5 is a half step many lifters add.

|---|---|---|---|

| 9.5 | 0-1 | No rep left, but you could add a little load | Heavy top singles |
| 9 | 1 | One more repetition was possible | Heavy top sets |
| 8 | 2 | Two more possible; bar speed still decent | Main working sets |

## A 12-week cycle outline

A common layout from base volume to meet week; adjust loads by RPE and your own recovery.

| Weeks | Phase | Main work | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-4 | Accumulation | More sets and reps at 65-80% of 1RM | RPE 6-8 |
| 5-8 | Intensification | Fewer reps at 75-90%; competition-style lifts | RPE 7-9 |
| 9-10 | Peaking | Heavy doubles and singles at 85-95%; practise commands | RPE 8-9 |
| 11 | Taper | Volume down 40-50%; last heavy squat and deadlift | RPE 7-8 |
| 12 | Meet week | Short, light sessions early; rest the last 2-3 days | RPE 5-7 |

## Terms

- **Accessory exercise**: An exercise outside the three competition lifts, chosen to build a muscle or fix a weak point.
- **Daily undulating periodisation (DUP)**: A plan that varies rep ranges and loads between sessions within the week, such as heavy, medium and light days.
- **Deload**: A planned lighter week or block, with less volume or load, to clear fatigue between harder blocks.
- **One-repetition maximum (1RM)**: The heaviest weight you can lift once with legal technique; the base for percentage programming.
- **Rating of perceived exertion (RPE)**: A 1 to 10 rating of how hard a set was; in strength training it is tied to how many repetitions were left.
- **Repetitions in reserve (RIR)**: How many more clean repetitions you could have done when the set ended; 2 RIR means two more were possible.
- **Sticking point**: The position in a lift where the bar slows most or where failures happen.
- **Taper (peaking)**: A planned drop in training stress, mostly volume, in the last days or weeks before a meet so fatigue clears and strength shows.
- **Velocity-based training (VBT)**: Setting load or stopping sets from measured bar speed, using a device that reports mean concentric velocity in metres per second.
- **Working max (training max)**: A weight you can repeat on a normal day, often set below a true max, and used as the base for training percentages.
