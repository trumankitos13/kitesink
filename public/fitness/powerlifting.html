Percentages and zonesHeavy loads build strength better than light loadsFor strength, heavy and moderate loads beat light loads, so most working sets should sit in the heavy to moderate range.BeginnerWell supported

A network meta-analysis of 28 studies in 747 healthy adults, all taken to failure, found muscle growth was similar across loads. Strength gains were larger with high loads (8 repetitions or fewer to failure) and moderate loads (9 to 15) than with low loads (over 15). High versus moderate was not clearly different. Light work still has a place for skill and recovery.

  • Keep most working sets on the competition lifts in the 1-8 repetition range.
  • Use light sets for warm-ups, skill work and easy days.

Lopez 2021

Percentages and zonesTesting a true max every few weeksFrequent max attempts add fatigue and risk without adding much information.MistakeIntermediateCoaching practice

This is coaching convention. A true max is the heaviest single you can complete with legal technique. It is a hard, slow-to-recover effort, and a bad day gives a misleading number. Work from a training max set from recent RPE 8 to 9 sets, and keep true maxes for meets or planned tests every few months.

  • Test at a meet or a planned test day, not on impulse.
Percentages and zonesRunning percentages off an old or lucky maxA percentage is only as good as the max behind it, so reset the number when strength changes.MistakeBeginnerCoaching practice

This is coaching convention. If the max is months old, the percentages are too light or too heavy, and a lucky one-off single makes the plan too hard to finish. Reps at a given percentage also vary between lifters, so use RPE alongside the plan and rebase when weights feel clearly off.

  • Rebase percentages after every cycle using your best recent set, not one lucky rep.
RPE and velocityRPE with reps in reserve lets you pick the load by feelA set rated RPE 9 left one repetition in reserve and RPE 10 left none; the scale worked on all three lifts in powerlifters.IntermediateSome support

The scale pairs effort with repetitions in reserve (RIR): RPE 10 is 0 RIR and RPE 9 is 1 RIR. In 29 lifters, experienced squatters rated a 1RM higher than novices, and bar speed fell as RPE rose. In 15 powerlifters, RPE at 1RM was 9.6 to 9.7 on all three lifts, and RPE tracked percent of 1RM closely (r = 0.88 to 0.91).

  • Rate each top set right after it ends, before looking at the bar speed or the numbers.

Zourdos 2016b · Helms 2017

RPE and velocityLoading by RPE or bar speed works about as well as percentagesTrials and a meta-analysis found no clear strength difference between autoregulated loading and fixed-percentage loading.IntermediateSome support

A meta-analysis found no significant 1RM difference between autoregulated and standard percentage loading in resistance-trained people (mean difference 2.07 kg, 95% CI -0.32 to 4.46). In an 8-week trial of 21 trained men, RPE-based and percent-based loading both raised squat and bench press 1RM, and the gap between them was not significant. Both are valid ways to set load.

  • Use RPE when daily readiness swings; use percentages when you want a fixed plan to compare weeks.

Hickmott 2022 · Helms 2018 · Larsen 2021b

RPE and velocityVelocity loss limits did not change strength gains in a large reviewEnding a set when bar speed has dropped by a set percentage is a valid way to cap fatigue; the threshold changed strength little.AdvancedSome support

A meta-analysis of 19 longitudinal studies found the velocity loss chosen within sets did not change strength gains, while higher velocity loss went with more muscle growth. A second meta-analysis found thresholds of 25% or less gave slightly more 1RM strength (2.32 kg, 95% CI 0.33 to 4.31). Bar speed at 1RM differs by lift (0.23, 0.10 and 0.14 m/s for squat, bench and deadlift in 15 powerlifters), so each lift needs its own profile.

  • Record bar speed on your own lifts for several weeks before using a velocity cut-off.

Jukic 2023 · Hickmott 2022 · Helms 2017

RPE and velocityStrength does not need sets taken to failureStopping sets short of failure gave similar strength gains to failure training, so heavy work can end with repetitions in reserve.IntermediateWell supported

A meta-analysis of 8 studies found non-failure training gave a 0.6 to 1.3% larger strength increase than failure training, a gap the authors judged unlikely to be meaningful. A later meta-regression found a negligible link between reps in reserve and strength gain, although muscle growth did show a negative slope with reps in reserve, meaning more growth nearer failure.

  • Stop heavy sets with 1-3 repetitions in reserve; a missed rep costs recovery and, on the platform, a lift.

Davies 2016 · Robinson 2024

RPE and velocityTreating RPE as exact when most lifters misjudge reps in reservePeople tend to underpredict reps left in the tank, so RPE is a rough guide that improves with practice.MistakeIntermediateSome support

A meta-analysis of 12 studies (414 participants) found people underpredicted repetitions to failure by 0.95 reps on average, with wide variation. Accuracy improved when the prediction was made nearer failure, with heavier loads and in later sets. A set called RPE 8 may have been closer to RPE 9.

  • Calibrate by comparing your RPE with bar speed or filmed sets over several weeks.

Halperin 2022

Frequency and volumeMore weekly sets build more strength, with sharply diminishing returnsStrength rises with weekly sets and with training frequency, but the gains flatten faster than muscle growth does.IntermediateWell supported

A meta-regression of 67 studies (2,058 participants) found strength gains rose with weekly set volume and with frequency, with diminishing returns that were more pronounced for strength than for muscle size. An earlier meta-analysis found high weekly sets beat low weekly sets (effect size 0.18), and a frequency meta-analysis found no significant effect once volume was equated.

  • Add sets only when progress stalls, and add them to the lift that is lagging.

Pelland 2026 · Ralston 2017 · Ralston 2018

Frequency and volumeA small dose of heavy work can still raise a powerlifter's 1RMAbout 3 to 6 heavy working sets per lift each week was enough to expect strength gains in powerlifters over 6 to 12 weeks.IntermediateThin evidence

A multi-part study (interviews, surveys and two interventions with 25 intermediate to advanced powerlifters) proposed a minimum effective dose: about 3 to 6 working sets of 1 to 5 repetitions per lift each week, spread over 1 to 3 sessions, at loads above 80% of 1RM and RPE 7.5 to 9.5, for 6 to 12 weeks. It describes a floor for busy periods, not an ideal.

  • Use this dose in busy or injured weeks, not as a long-term plan.

Androulakis-Korakakis 2021

Frequency and volumeTraining days per week usually rise with levelBeginners do well on 2 to 3 sessions a week; advanced lifters often spread the lifts across 4 to 6.BeginnerSome support

For beginners there is trial evidence: a meta-analysis of 140 strength studies found untrained people gained most when each muscle group was trained 3 days a week, and trained people at 2. Past that it is coaching convention. Intermediates commonly train 3 to 4 days, with each lift twice a week. In a survey of 117 Norwegian powerlifters that included world, European and national champions, the most common answer (58.1%) was 5 to 6 sessions a week, with the most common load at 71-80% of 1RM.

  • Add a session only when recovery between sessions is good and the lifts are improving.

Shaw 2022 · Rhea 2003

Frequency and volumeIn trained men, eight sets of squats beat one set within three weeksSquat 1RM rose on one, four or eight sets at 80% to failure, but the eight-set group was ahead of the one-set group by week 3 and stayed ahead.IntermediateThin evidence

Thirty-two resistance-trained men squatted twice a week for six weeks at 80% of 1RM, every set to failure, then all ran the same four-week peaking block. Every group was stronger at 6 and 10 weeks; at week 3 the eight-set group led the one-set group by 7.9%. Quadriceps activation did not change and early force development fell, which the authors tied to heavy sets to failure. For the wider dose picture, see the volume-dose, minimum-dose and not-to-failure entries.

  • On one set a session, check within three weeks whether your squat is responding, as the authors advise.

Marshall 2011 · McDonald 2011d

Periodisation and peakingPlanned variation beats a fixed routine for 1RM strengthPeriodised programs raised 1RM more than non-periodised ones, including when total volume was matched.IntermediateWell supported

A meta-analysis of 18 studies (81 effects) found periodised training gave larger 1RM gains than non-periodised training (effect size 0.43). A later meta-analysis of 35 volume-equated studies agreed for strength (effect size 0.31) but found no difference for muscle size. These trials were short, so the long-term picture in elite lifters is less clear.

  • Change volume and intensity on a plan, not at random.

Williams 2017 · Moesgaard 2022

Periodisation and peakingLinear, block and undulating plans differ little in trialsNo clear winner between linear and undulating models; too few trials exist to compare block models.IntermediateSome support

Linear plans raise load week by week, block plans stack phases (volume, then intensity, then peaking), and daily undulating plans vary reps and load between sessions. A meta-analysis of 17 studies (510 participants) found no difference between linear and undulating for strength. A later one found a small edge for undulating in trained people (effect size 0.61, 95% CI 0.00 to 1.22) and could not compare other models.

  • Pick the model you will follow for a full cycle and judge it on your own logbook.

Harries 2015 · Moesgaard 2022

Periodisation and peakingLifters taper for 5 to 10 days by cutting volume and keeping heavy singlesSurveys report a short step taper: volume down about 40-50%, intensity kept high, last heavy squat and deadlift 7-10 days out.AdvancedThin evidence

These are self-reports, not trials proving a taper works. In 364 North American raw powerlifters, a step taper over 7 to 10 days with 41-50% less volume was typical, with full rest about 2.8 days before the meet. A small trial of 19 athletes found 3 or 5 days of full rest kept lower-body strength; upper-body strength held at 3 days but not 5. A review found limited evidence that a short, heavy, low-volume session in the 48 hours before competition can help performance.

  • Cut volume sharply but keep the competition lifts and a few heavy singles.
  • Rest fully for the last 2 to 3 days before the meet.

Travis 2021a · Winwood 2026 · Travis 2022 · Harrison 2019

Periodisation and peakingMost strength athletes deload for about a week every 5 to 6 weeksDeloading is common but little tested: athletes cut sets and load, keep frequency and exercises, and add reps in reserve.IntermediateThin evidence

An expert Delphi (34 coaches began, 21 finished the third round) defined a deload as a period of reduced training stress to mitigate fatigue and promote recovery. A survey of 246 strength and physique athletes found all deloaded, typically for 6.4 days every 5.6 weeks, keeping frequency and exercises. No trial has yet shown the best length or timing.

  • Plan a lighter week roughly every 5 to 6 weeks, and move it earlier when joints ache and performance stalls.

Bell 2023 · Rogerson 2024

Periodisation and peakingMcDonald argues daily maxing does not carry over from weightlifting to powerliftingHis view: a weightlifting max is a split-second explosive effort well short of maximal force, while a squat, bench or deadlift max grinds for seconds and costs far more.AdvancedCoaching practice

McDonald reasons that both Olympic lifts are limited by a brief drive near the end of the pull, and by his rough figures a best clean and jerk is only about four fifths of a best deadlift. A max powerlift is hard from start to finish and can be fought for several seconds. He argues the Bulgarian method suited only selected, full-time, drug-assisted elite lifters after years of build-up, and that most lifters who copy it get hurt. These are his judgments; the daily-max evidence entry has two small direct tests.

  • If you train a lift often, keep most sessions well below max and only one or two truly heavy (McDonald's rule).

McDonald 2015j · McDonald 2016m

Periodisation and peakingIn a high-volume block, keep a little heavy work so top-end strength holdsMcDonald's practice is to open sessions in a muscle-building block with a heavy single or triple at about 80 to 90% of max, then do the volume work.IntermediateCoaching practice

His view is that strength athletes who drop all heavy lifting during a hypertrophy phase lose top-end strength quickly, and that a small dose of heavy work holds it. He treats volume and intensity blocks as two steps: first add muscle to raise the ceiling, then train the nervous system to use it. This is coaching practice from his article. The minimum-dose entry gives data on how little heavy work keeps 1RM rising.

  • Work up to one single or triple at about 80 to 90% before the volume sets (McDonald's practice).

McDonald 2016i · Androulakis-Korakakis 2021

Periodisation and peakingTwo small studies of daily maxing point in opposite directionsThree lifters who maxed the squat daily for five weeks gained 2 to 11%. Eleven trained men lifting 100% of 1RM daily on a squat machine lost about 12 kg (27 lb) in two weeks.AdvancedThin evidence

In a case series, two powerlifters and one weightlifter worked up to a squat 1RM every day for 35 days, plus back-off sets; on day 37 they were 5, 13.5 and 21 kg (11, 30 and 46 lb) stronger, with no control group. In a controlled study, 11 weight-trained men squatted on a machine at 100% of 1RM daily for two weeks; 1RM fell by a mean 12.2 kg (27 lb) and other force measures dropped, while a light once-weekly group barely changed. Neither study was built to test safety; see the daily-max mistake entry for the injury concern.

Zourdos 2016a · Fry 1994

Periodisation and peakingChanging technique, equipment or exercises in the final weeksLate novelty brings soreness and risk with no time to adapt; keep the final weeks specific and familiar.MistakeIntermediateCoaching practice

This is coaching convention, and tapering interviews match it. Ten Croatian champions reported taper training that was highly specific, with assistance exercises removed and the same equipment as in competition. New Zealand elite lifters said poor tapers happened when they took a week or more off.

  • Wear competition gear in the last heavy sessions and drop new exercises.

Grgic 2017 · Pritchard 2016

Technique and leveragesPractise the lifts in competition style and hold four principlesStrength in the squat, bench press and deadlift is partly skill, so the lifts themselves, done to competition standard, make up most of the training.BeginnerCoaching practice

This is coaching convention, not a trial result. Train squat depth, the bench pause and the deadlift lockout the way they are judged. Four principles carry across the lifts: a stable base, full-body tightness before the bar moves, a planned bar path, and the same set-up every rep. In the squat and deadlift that path stays close to a straight line over mid-foot. The bench differs: McDonald teaches a shallow arc, with the bar touching low on the chest and finishing above the shoulders. Assistance exercises support the lifts and do not replace them.

  • Film your heaviest set of each lift every week, from the side.
  • Use the same set-up routine on warm-ups and on heavy sets.

McDonald 2008b

Technique and leveragesLow-bar squats lean forward and load the hips; high-bar squats stay uprightBar position changes the squat: low-bar uses more hip and back, high-bar more knee and quadriceps.IntermediateSome support

A review of back squat biomechanics found high-bar squats showed more knee bend, less hip bend, a more upright torso and greater depth. Low-bar squats showed more hip bend and forward lean, with more erector spinae, adductor and gluteal activity. A systematic review of classic powerlifting research suggested low-bar squats generally let lifters move more weight.

  • Try each position for several weeks and keep the one you can repeat at competition depth.

Glassbrook 2017 · Ferland 2019

Technique and leveragesBuild the bench from the setup: tight back, planted feet, paused barA stable arch and tight upper back shorten the bar path and make the pause on the chest repeatable.BeginnerCoaching practice

This is coaching convention. Keep head, shoulders and buttocks on the bench and feet flat on the floor. Pull the shoulder blades together and down, grip with the thumbs around the bar, and lower under control to the lower chest. Pause until the press command, then press back over the shoulders. Wider grips shorten the range, but federations cap grip width, so check the rulebook.

  • Pause every training rep until the bar is still, and sometimes hold longer: the referee decides when to call 'Press'.

IPF 2026a

Technique and leveragesSumo and conventional deadlifts load the joints differentlySumo uses a wide stance and a more upright trunk; conventional moves the bar farther and does more mechanical work.IntermediateThin evidence

In a video analysis of 12 sumo and 12 conventional lifters at a national championship, sumo lifters used a wider stance (70 cm against 32 cm; about 28 against 13 in), turned the feet out more and kept a more upright trunk. Bar distance, mechanical work and predicted energy cost were about 25 to 40% greater for conventional. The study did not test which style lifts more.

  • Test both styles for a few weeks and keep the one that moves more weight with a steady back.

Escamilla 2000

Technique and leveragesLimb and torso lengths change which set-up suits youProportions shift the best stance, depth and grip, so copy principles from top lifters and not their exact positions.IntermediateCoaching practice

This is coaching convention, not a tested rule. Long thighs relative to the torso are thought to tip the squat forward and favour a wider stance. The one direct test, in 11 men squatting to parallel, found that ankle mobility went with trunk angle and segment-length ratios did not. Long arms shorten the pull in the deadlift and lengthen the bench press path; short arms do the reverse. Use these as starting guesses and let filmed sets and bar speed decide.

  • Film from the side and compare two set-ups on the same weight before changing for good.
  • If your squat folds forward, check ankle mobility before blaming your proportions.

Fuglsang 2017

Technique and leveragesA pause on the chest takes away the rebound that helps a touch-and-go benchPressing straight out of the lowering adds force from the stretch. When the bar sits still, that help fades within a second or two, so a paused bench moves less weight.IntermediateThin evidence

In 12 experienced male lifters benching 95% of their max, the extra force carried over from the lowering phase decayed as the pause grew, losing half of it about every 0.85 seconds. The effect is the stretch-shortening cycle (see the term entry). McDonald's estimate is that a pause can take 5 to 10% off a touch-and-go best; his first-meet guide says about 5%. Both are rules of thumb, not measured results.

  • Before a meet, pause your training benches the way a referee will expect (McDonald's practice).
  • Compare a paused max only with other paused maxes, not with touch-and-go lifts.

Wilson 1991 · McDonald 2016h · McDonald 2021a

Technique and leveragesThe first deadlift rep starts cold, so reset reps match the meetA pull from the floor has no lowering before it. Later reps in a set borrow a stretch from the controlled descent, which the single you pull at a meet never gets.IntermediateCoaching practice

McDonald's view is that rep two of a deadlift set often feels easier than rep one because the controlled lowering adds a stretch before the pull and often sets a better start position. A meet pull is a single from a dead stop, which is why many deadlifters reset between reps (see the between-reps table in technique). A dynamic start, where the lifter sinks quickly and pulls out of the dip, chases the same stretch; he argues it helps only if the lifter stays in position, and that most let the hips shoot up.

  • To make training reps match a meet pull, let the bar settle and reset before each rep (a common deadlifters' practice he describes).

McDonald 2016h

Technique and leveragesMental rehearsal raised strength in healthy adults, but less than real trainingAcross 13 studies, rehearsing a movement in the mind raised maximal strength more than doing nothing, and physical training raised it more than imagery.BeginnerSome support

A 2018 meta-analysis pooled 370 healthy adults. Motor imagery, rehearsing a contraction mentally without moving, moderately improved maximal voluntary strength in arms and legs against no training. Physical practice beat it by a small margin, and adding imagery to physical practice gave no clear gain over physical practice alone. The authors suggest imagery to keep muscle function when athletes cannot train at maximal loads. McDonald writes that it can help strength once correct technique is known. The abstract does not say that lifters were studied.

  • When you cannot train heavy for a while, rehearse your lifts mentally to help hold strength, as the authors suggest.

Paravlic 2018 · McDonald 2016i

Technique and leveragesTreat warm-up sets as focused technique practice, not fillerMcDonald's view is that warm-up reps done with full attention to position and bar path add up to a large amount of practice over a year.BeginnerCoaching practice

Every lifter does warm-up sets anyway. McDonald argues that most people stop paying attention once a lift feels competent, and that this is where their technique stops improving. Watching a point such as elbow position, bar path or where the bar touches turns those light reps into deliberate practice at little extra cost. It is coaching practice, not a trial result; the technique guide's spacing entry covers how often to practise.

  • On warm-up sets, watch a point such as elbow position, bar path or where the bar touches (McDonald's practice).

McDonald 2009h

Accessories and bandsChoose accessories for the point where the lift slows or failsFind where the bar slows, then train the muscles and positions that fail there with 1 to 3 exercises.IntermediateThin evidence

A review describes the sticking point as the position in the range where difficulty rises disproportionately, and says working out its cause helps choose training. Powerlifters in a minimum-dose study typically used 1 to 3 accessory exercises per lift at RPE 7 to 9 and about 6 to 10 repetitions.

  • Film a slow or missed rep, locate the sticking point, then pick exercises that train that range.

Kompf 2017 · Androulakis-Korakakis 2021

Accessories and bandsBands and chains help trained lifters when loads are 80% of 1RM or moreIn a meta-analysis, variable resistance beat constant resistance for trained lifters at 80% of 1RM and above.AdvancedSome support

A meta-analysis of 14 studies found variable-resistance training (bands or chains) beat constant resistance for maximum strength (effect size 0.80). In trained people the benefit appeared at 80% of 1RM or more (0.76) and not below (0.00). In surveys, 39% of 28 elite British lifters used bands and 57% chains, and 76.9% of 117 Norwegian lifters used variable resistance.

  • Add bands or chains to heavy work once the base technique is stable.

Lin 2022 · Swinton 2009 · Shaw 2022

Weight class and cuttingPick the class you can hold in training; move up when cutting costs liftsA lifter whose normal weight sits at a class limit can compete there with little cutting; large cuts are a reason to move up.IntermediateCoaching practice

This is coaching convention. Consider moving up when making weight takes more than a modest, gradual diet, when training quality drops in the weeks before weigh-in, or when you are growing into the next class anyway. An ACSM consensus statement on weight-category sports advises classifying athletes by preseason body composition and shortening the gap between weigh-in and competition.

  • Weigh yourself in the morning for several weeks and compare the trend with the class limit.

Burke 2021

Weight class and cuttingRapid weight cuts are common in powerlifting, and fluid loss costs strengthMany lifters cut weight, mostly by fluid methods; fluid loss lowers strength and can harm health.IntermediateSome support

Among 64 lifters at the 2018 IPF classic worlds, 83% cut weight (average 2.9% of body mass) and most reported negative effects on mood. In a survey of 321 lifters, 85.8% used rapid weight loss, and doctors and dietitians were rarely an influence. A meta-analysis of 28 manuscripts found hypohydration cut muscle strength by 5.5% on average. Extreme cuts can harm health; involve a sports dietitian or a doctor.

  • Do not use fluid restriction, sauna or laxatives to make weight.
  • Ask a sports dietitian or doctor before any planned weight loss.

Kwan 2022 · Nolan 2022 · Savoie 2015

Weight class and cuttingCutting weight with fluid restriction in the last daysFluid restriction is a common but risky way to make weight, and it lowers strength and endurance.MistakeIntermediateSome support

In the 2018 IPF classic worlds survey, fluid restriction after fluid loading was among the most common methods, used by about a third of lifters, alongside fasting and laxatives. A meta-analysis found hypohydration lowered muscle strength by 5.5% and muscle endurance by 8.3%. Where weigh-in is within about two hours of lifting, there is little time to recover.

  • Stay near your class limit with normal eating, and see a sports dietitian before any cut.

Savoie 2015 · Kwan 2022

Recovery and injuriesShort sleep lowers performance, with evening sessions hit harderA meta-analysis found sleep loss cut exercise performance by about 7.6% on average.BeginnerWell supported

Across 69 publications, sleep loss (6 hours or less in 24) lowered performance by a mean of 7.56% (95% CI -11.9 to -3.13), including strength tasks. Tests done in the morning were largely unaffected; those done in the evening were consistently worse. A systematic review of 17 studies found several nights of restricted sleep could cut force in compound lifts.

  • Protect sleep in the week before a meet and keep a normal bedtime.

Craven 2022 · Knowles 2018

Recovery and injuriesInjury rates look low, but most lifters report pain in the back, shoulder or hipCounted as time lost, injuries are infrequent; counted as pain that affects training, most lifters have one.IntermediateSome support

A systematic review of 9 studies found 1.0 to 4.4 injuries per 1,000 training hours in powerlifting, with the spine, shoulder and knee most affected. A survey of 104 Swedish subelite lifters counted pain or impaired function and found 70% currently injured and 87% injured in the past year, mostly lumbopelvic, shoulder and hip; only 16% of those currently injured stopped training completely.

  • See a physio or doctor for pain that lasts more than a week, and adjust loads while it settles.

Aasa 2017 · Strömbäck 2018

Recovery and injuriesCopying weightlifters' daily maxes into the squat, bench and deadliftMcDonald's view is that near-max singles in the powerlifts every day break most lifters who try them without years of build-up, and that connective tissue is usually what gives out.MistakeIntermediateCoaching practice

The injury claim is coaching experience, not a trial result. Tendons adapt more slowly than strength rises (see the tendon entry in basics). One controlled trial measured strength, not injury: two weeks of daily machine squats at 100% of 1RM cut trained men's 1RM by about 12 kg (27 lb) (see the daily-max evidence entry). Frequency alone is not the issue; see the six-day and elite-loads entries in recovery.

  • Do not take up daily maxing on your own; McDonald advises most lifters against it, and any trial of it needs an experienced coach.
  • Have pain that changes how you lift checked by a clinician before you train through it.

McDonald 2016m · McDonald 2015j · Fry 1994

Talent and practiceGenes and environment count about equally in strength differences between untrained peopleHeritability estimates for strength average about 0.5. That describes differences within a group, not a fixed ceiling for any one lifter.IntermediateSome support

A 2017 meta-analysis pooled 58 measurements from 24 studies of healthy people in an untrained state. Heritability averaged 0.52, similar for grip, isometric, isotonic and isokinetic strength and for jumping (0.49 to 0.56), though results varied widely between studies. The authors read this as genetic and environmental influence being comparable, with environment counting for more at older ages. These are baseline strength tests, not gains from training or meet totals. McDonald's view is that genes set a limit that work cannot pass; the response-spread entry covers trainability.

Zempo 2017 · McDonald 2011f

Talent and practiceOn the same 12-week plan, arm strength gains ranged from none to more than doubleIn 585 men and women who trained one arm on the same 12-week programme, 1RM gains ran from 0 to 250%, and some barely changed muscle size.BeginnerThin evidence

Subjects at eight centres followed the same progressive elbow-flexor programme. Biceps size changed by -2 to +59% and 1RM by 0 to +250% (0 to 10.2 kg, 0 to 22 lb). Women gained more strength in relative terms, and men slightly more size. It is one small muscle group, so it says nothing direct about the squat, bench or deadlift. McDonald argues that spreads like this in supervised trials reflect biology rather than effort. The hypertrophy guide's responder entries cover muscle growth.

  • Compare your lifts with your own past numbers, not with another lifter's rate (McDonald's advice).
