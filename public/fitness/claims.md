# Fitness claims, checked: KiteSink

Common things people say about training, eating and health. Each has a verdict, the reason, who was studied, and the source.
Verdicts: Holds up, Mostly holds, Depends, Overstated, Not supported.

## Hypertrophy

### Do you have to train to failure to grow muscle?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Getting close to failure helps muscle growth, but reaching failure itself showed no clear extra benefit over stopping a little short.

Across training trials, muscle growth rose as sets ended closer to failure, while strength gains were similar across a wide range of effort. A separate meta-analysis found no advantage for sets taken to momentary failure over sets stopped short (effect size 0.12, interval from -0.13 to 0.37). How close people stopped was often estimated, so the exact curve is unclear.

Who was studied: Refalo 2023: 15 studies in healthy adults of any age and experience. Robinson 2024: meta-regressions of training trials, with proximity to failure estimated.

- Finish most hard sets with a few reps left in reserve, meaning reps you could still have done.
- If you do go to failure, pick exercises where a missed rep is safe, such as machines or cables.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Refalo 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y), [Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)

### Do only the last five reps of a set count?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Research on closeness to failure shows a gradual trend, not a cut-off at five reps.

The 'effective reps' idea says only the final reps before failure build muscle. The meta-analyses cited here did not test a five-rep cut-off. They found that growth tended to rise as sets ended closer to failure, and one concluded the relationship may not be a straight line. Both rely on rough estimates of reps in reserve, so the true shape is unknown.

Who was studied: Refalo 2023: 15 studies in healthy adults. Robinson 2024: exploratory meta-regressions of training trials. Neither tested a five-rep rule.

- Take working sets close to failure instead of counting a fixed number of final reps.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Thin evidence · Sources: [Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2), [Refalo 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y)

### Do light weights build as much muscle as heavy ones?

**Verdict: Mostly holds.** For muscle size, yes when sets are hard; heavier loads still build more one-rep-max strength.

A network meta-analysis of sets taken to failure found no differences in muscle growth between low, moderate and high loads, but strength gains were larger with high and moderate loads than with low. A second meta-analysis of 45 studies found the same split. A third pooled 119 studies on growth and found all prescriptions promoted it comparably. Light sets only work when they are taken close to failure.

Who was studied: Lopez 2021: 28 studies, 747 healthy adults. Refalo 2021: 45 studies. Currier 2023: 119 studies on growth (3,364 people) and 178 on strength (5,097 people).

- For size, pick any load from heavy to light, as long as the set ends near failure.
- If you want a bigger one-rep max, include sets above 80% of your max, the range that ranked best for strength.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Lopez 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000002585), [Refalo 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2021.1898094), [Currier 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807)

### Does every muscle need training twice a week?

**Verdict: Depends.** For size, weekly sets matter more than how they are split; for strength, training more often may help, though findings differ.

A meta-regression of 67 studies found growth rose with weekly set volume, while frequency's effect on growth was compatible with negligible effects. Strength gains did rise with frequency, with diminishing returns. A separate meta-analysis found no significant effect of frequency on strength when volume was equal. In one network analysis the top-ranked growth plan was twice weekly, so twice a week is a sensible default, not a requirement.

Who was studied: Pelland 2026: 67 studies, 2,058 participants (about 79% male, average age about 25). Ralston 2018: 12 studies on strength. Currier 2023: network analysis of 12 plans.

- Set your weekly sets first, then choose the number of days that lets you train each session hard.
- Add a day if sessions get so long that your effort drops.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Pelland 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w), [Ralston 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9), [Currier 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807)

### Is muscle soreness a sign of a good workout?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Soreness is a poor guide to muscle damage, and muscle growth does not need it.

In 110 male students doing maximal eccentric arm exercise, soreness correlated weakly or not at all with other markers of damage, and the authors called it a poor reflector of damage. In an eight-week trial, people who ramped up gradually and showed little damage gained the same muscle size and strength as people who started with a damaging bout. A review concluded damage does not mediate growth.

Who was studied: Nosaka 2002: 110 male students, 12 to 60 maximal eccentric arm actions, tracked for 4 days. Flann 2011: pre-trained versus naive adults, 8 weeks. Damas 2018: review.

- Judge progress by the reps, loads and measurements you log, not by how sore you feel.
- Ease into a new exercise or a jump in volume so soreness does not cost you sessions.

Evidence: Mixed · Some support · Sources: [Nosaka 2002](https://doi.org/10.1034/j.1600-0838.2002.10178.x), [Flann 2011](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.050112), [Damas 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3792-9)

### Does cardio kill your muscle gains?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Adding cardio did not reduce overall muscle growth in a 43-study review, though a small effect showed at fibre level, mainly with running.

A meta-analysis of 43 studies found adding aerobic work to the same lifting program did not compromise muscle hypertrophy (effect -0.01) or maximal strength. Explosive strength was reduced, more so when both were done in the same session. A second meta-analysis of 15 studies measuring muscle fibre size found a small negative effect overall (-0.23); for type I fibres it was seen with running but not cycling. The fibre data are preliminary.

Who was studied: Schumann 2022: 43 studies in healthy adults, at least 4 weeks of supervised concurrent training. Lundberg 2022: 15 studies on muscle fibre size, same design.

- Keep your lifting volume the same when you add cardio and, if you can, leave 3 hours or more between the sessions.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Schumann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7), [Lundberg 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01688-x)

### Do you need to change your exercises often to keep growing?

**Verdict: Depends.** Two small trials gave mixed results: varied exercises may help strength a little, and muscle growth was similar with or without variety.

In 49 active people training twice a week for 12 weeks, groups with constant or varied exercises gained similar thigh muscle size, while the varied-exercise, constant-intensity group gained the most squat strength. In 17 strength-trained men over 9 weeks, choosing exercises session by session produced a small advantage in lean mass and bench press strength over a fixed list. Both trials are small and short.

Who was studied: Fonseca 2014: 49 physically active people, twice a week for 12 weeks. Rauch 2020: 17 strength-trained men, 3 sessions a week for 9 weeks.

- Keep the main lifts you want to improve, and rotate accessory movements when one stalls or irritates a joint.

Evidence: Randomised trial · Thin evidence · Sources: [Fonseca 2014](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000000539), [Rauch 2020](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002272)

### Do you need a deload every fourth week?

**Verdict: Depends.** Deloads are common coaching practice, but outcome trials are few; one found reduced-volume weeks did not cost muscle gain in beginners.

A Delphi panel of expert coaches (34 in round one, 21 in round three) agreed a definition: a period of reduced training stress meant to lower fatigue and prepare for later training. That is expert opinion, not an outcome trial. In one trial, 19 untrained young men trained with or without reduced weeks at weeks 4 and 8; muscle thickness and strength-endurance rose similarly in both. No study here tests a fixed fourth-week rule in trained lifters.

Who was studied: Bell 2023: 34 expert coaches in round one, 21 in round three. Pancar 2026: 19 untrained young men, 8 weeks, reduced weeks at 4 and 8, arms and legs compared.

- Plan a lighter week when fatigue, sleep or motivation drop, rather than on a fixed calendar.
- In a deload, cut sets and sessions but keep the same exercises.

Evidence: Mixed · Thin evidence · Sources: [Bell 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0), [Pancar 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-40612-5)

## Powerlifting

### Is a calculated one-rep max as good as a tested one?

**Verdict: Mostly holds.** A set of about 5 reps predicted a max well on two exercises; accuracy fell as reps rose, and the tested lifts were not squat or deadlift.

In 70 adults tested at 1, 5, 10 and 20 reps, a 5-rep test gave the best prediction of a one-rep max on the chest press and the leg press (R2 of 0.993 and 0.974). Typical error was about 3 kg (6.6 lb) on the chest press and 16 kg (36 lb) on the leg press. The authors advise using no more than 10 reps in these equations. Squat and deadlift were not tested.

Who was studied: Reynolds 2006: 70 adults (34 men, 36 women) aged 18 to 69, tested at 1, 5, 10 and 20 reps on the chest press and leg press.

- Use a set of about 5 hard reps to estimate a max; the more reps, the rougher the estimate.
- Use the number to choose training loads, and test a heavy single when the exact figure matters.

Evidence: Cohort study · Thin evidence · Sources: [Reynolds 2006](https://doi.org/10.1519/R-15304.1)

### Do lifting belts weaken your core?

**Verdict: Overstated.** No cited study shows a lifting belt weakening the core; short trials found little change in muscle activity and more reps with a belt.

In 28 men squatting at 75% of predicted max, a belt alone did not change muscle activity, while abdominal bracing raised abdominal activity with or without a belt. In 49 lifters, a belt raised reps with a 6-rep-max load from 6.5 to 8.1. A review of 35 studies of lumbar supports, which are medical braces and not lifting belts, found inconsistent effects on trunk muscles. The two belt studies were single-session, so months of belt use are not covered.

Who was studied: Lynch 2025: 28 men, one session. Veltzke 2026: 49 men and women, two sessions a week apart. Azadinia 2017: review of 35 studies of lumbar supports.

- Learn to brace your abdomen without a belt, then add a belt for heavy sets if you like.

Evidence: Mixed · Thin evidence · Sources: [Lynch 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2025.2535048), [Veltzke 2026](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000005426), [Azadinia 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spinee.2016.12.005)

### Do you need to max out in training to get stronger?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Strength gains were similar across a wide range of effort, so regular true maxes are not required, though practising heavy lifts matters.

A meta-regression found strength gains were similar across a wide range of reps in reserve. A systematic review of six studies found one set of 6 to 12 reps at about 70 to 85% of 1RM, 2 to 3 times a week and near failure, raised squat and bench maxes in resistance-trained men over 8 to 12 weeks. In 38 untrained adults, practising the max test alone matched the strength gain of high-volume training, so skill with the lift counts.

Who was studied: Robinson 2024: meta-regressions of trials. Androulakis-Korakakis 2020: 6 studies in resistance-trained men. Mattocks 2017: 38 untrained adults, 8 weeks.

- Train most sets with reps left in reserve, and test a max only now and then.
- Practise heavy singles on your competition lifts before a meet so the feel is familiar.

Evidence: Mixed · Some support · Sources: [Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2), [Androulakis-Korakakis 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01236-0), [Mattocks 2017](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001300)

### Are deep squats bad for your knees?

**Verdict: Overstated.** One literature review found no good evidence that deep squats harm healthy knees and backs when technique is sound and load is built up gradually.

A review of over 164 articles found the compressive force behind the kneecap peaks around 90 degrees of knee bend and falls with deeper bending as the load spreads across more joint surface. It argued that half and quarter squats with very heavy loads may favour joint wear more than deep squats. It stressed learning the lift accurately under expert supervision and adding load progressively. This was biomechanics and review evidence, not a long trial comparing depths.

Who was studied: Hartmann 2013: literature review of over 164 articles on squat biomechanics, including calculations and cadaver joint studies.

- Squat to a depth you control with good form, and add load gradually.
- See a clinician or experienced coach if a squat causes knee pain.

Evidence: Review · Thin evidence · Sources: [Hartmann 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0073-6)

### Is powerlifting dangerous?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Injury rates in powerlifting were similar to other non-contact strength sports and low compared with contact sports, though the studies are weak.

A systematic review of 9 studies found 1.0 to 4.4 injuries per 1,000 hours of powerlifting training, and named the spine, shoulder and knee as the most common sites. A review of 20 studies across weight-training sports also found relatively low injury rates versus common team sports. Most studies were low quality or retrospective, and little is known about risk factors.

Who was studied: Aasa 2017: 9 studies in weightlifting and powerlifting. Keogh 2017: 20 studies of injuries in competitive weight-training sports.

- Build loads gradually and keep technique consistent as the weight gets heavy.
- Have pain that lasts checked by a clinician rather than lifting through it.

Evidence: Review · Some support · Sources: [Aasa 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096037), [Keogh 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0575-0)

### Do you need a periodised program to get stronger?

**Verdict: Mostly holds.** Planned variation in volume and intensity gave a moderate edge for maximal strength in meta-analyses, and no clear edge for muscle size.

A meta-analysis of 18 studies found 1RM improved more after periodised than non-periodised plans (effect size 0.43), with undulating plans looking more favourable. A second meta-analysis of 35 studies with volume matched found periodisation favoured 1RM strength (0.31) but not muscle size (0.13, interval crossing zero). The edge for undulating over linear plans appeared in trained lifters only.

Who was studied: Williams 2017: 18 studies, 81 effects. Moesgaard 2022: 35 studies with volume equated between plans, strength and size outcomes.

- Plan changes in load and reps across the weeks instead of repeating the same session.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Williams 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0734-y), [Moesgaard 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1)

## Meet day

### Is a sumo deadlift allowed in competition?

**Verdict: Holds up.** Under the IPF rules the deadlift sets no stance, so sumo and conventional are both legal.

The IPF Technical Rules Book (section 4.3) describes the deadlift as the bar laid in front of the feet, gripped with an optional grip and lifted until the lifter stands erect with knees locked. It does not name or restrict stance. Lifts fail for causes such as downward movement of the bar, supporting the bar on the thighs, or stepping or moving the feet laterally. Other federations publish their own books, so check yours.

Who was studied: Not a study. IPF Technical Rules Book effective 1 March 2026, sections 4.3 and 4.3.1.

- Pick the stance that suits your build and train it, since the same lockout rules apply to both.
- Read your own federation's rulebook if your meet is not run under IPF rules.

Evidence: Rulebook · Rulebook · Sources: [IPF 2026a](https://www.powerlifting.sport/rules/codes/info/technical-rules)

### Do you need a singlet at your first meet?

**Verdict: Holds up.** Under IPF rules, yes: a non-supportive singlet from the approved list is required in Classic (raw) competition.

Section 3.1 requires a one-piece, form-fitting singlet in Classic competitions, with the straps over the shoulders in every lift and legs 3 to 25 cm (1.2 to 9.8 in) long. It must come from a manufacturer on the approved list. A t-shirt is worn under it, and the same type of singlet is worn for all three lifts. At weigh-in, clothing is limited to the singlet, one approved t-shirt (if the division uses one) and standard underwear. Local meets may set their own entry rules.

Who was studied: Not a study. IPF Technical Rules Book effective 1 March 2026, sections 3.1, 3.2 and 5.

- Buy a singlet from your federation's approved list well before the meet and train in it once or twice.
- Check your meet's entry details for gear rules.

Evidence: Rulebook · Rulebook · Sources: [IPF 2026a](https://www.powerlifting.sport/rules/codes/info/technical-rules)

### Can you wear any lifting belt you like at an IPF meet?

**Verdict: Not supported.** No: the belt must come from the approved list and meet size and construction limits, including a maximum width of 10 cm (3.9 in).

Section 3.6 allows a belt, worn outside the suit, only from manufacturers on the approved list. The main body must be non-stretch leather, vinyl or similar, with no added padding, bracing or supports. Maximum width is 10 cm (3.9 in) and maximum thickness 13 mm (0.5 in) along the main length. The buckle may have one or two prongs or a quick-release lever.

Who was studied: Not a study. IPF Technical Rules Book effective 1 March 2026, section 3.6.

- Check the approved list before buying a belt for a meet.
- Measure the width and thickness of any belt you already own.

Evidence: Rulebook · Rulebook · Sources: [IPF 2026a](https://www.powerlifting.sport/rules/codes/info/technical-rules)

### Can you change an attempt after you hand it in?

**Verdict: Depends.** It depends on the attempt: the first can be changed once, second attempts cannot be changed, and the third deadlift can be changed twice.

Under section 6.1, you declare starting weights at weigh-in. One change of weight is allowed on the first attempt of each lift, higher or lower, up to three minutes before the first round of that lift begins for the first group. Second-round weights cannot be changed. Third attempts on squat and bench cannot be changed. On the third deadlift two changes are allowed if the bar is not yet loaded and called. Hand in each next attempt within one minute.

Who was studied: Not a study. IPF Technical Rules Book effective 1 March 2026, section 6.1 (the round system).

- Agree your three attempts with your coach before the meet, and decide in advance what would change them.
- Hand in your second and third attempts within one minute of the lights, or the rules set the weight for you.

Evidence: Rulebook · Rulebook · Sources: [IPF 2026a](https://www.powerlifting.sport/rules/codes/info/technical-rules)

### Is a water cut a good idea for a first meet?

**Verdict: Not supported.** Dehydration cut strength in a meta-analysis, and an IPF weigh-in about two hours before lifting leaves little time to recover, so a first meet is a poor place to try one.

A meta-analysis of 28 papers found dehydration reduced muscle endurance by 8.3%, strength by 5.5% and anaerobic power by 5.8% on average, with smaller drops in trained people. IPF weigh-in takes place no earlier than two hours before the session, over a 90-minute window. Large fluid losses carry health risks, so talk to an experienced coach and a clinician before attempting any cut.

Who was studied: Savoie 2015: 28 papers on dehydration and muscle performance. IPF Technical Rules Book effective 1 March 2026, section 5 (weighing in).

- Enter the class you already weigh near instead of cutting.
- If you want a different class, plan a gradual change over months with a coach.

Evidence: Mixed · Some support · Sources: [Savoie 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0349-0), [IPF 2026a](https://www.powerlifting.sport/rules/codes/info/technical-rules)

### Will one bad night's sleep before a meet ruin your lifts?

**Verdict: Depends.** One short night often has little effect on strength, but sleep restricted over several nights, and afternoon sessions after a bad night, showed bigger drops.

A meta-analysis of 69 publications found sleep loss lowered performance across exercise types (average drop 7.56%), with consistent effects only after deprivation or late restriction. Tasks done in the afternoon were consistently affected, and tasks done in the morning were largely unaffected. A systematic review of 17 studies found total sleep deprivation had little effect on strength, while consecutive nights of restricted sleep could reduce force in multi-joint lifts.

Who was studied: Craven 2022: 69 publications, 227 outcome measures, mostly male participants. Knowles 2018: 17 studies of sleep loss and resistance exercise.

- Protect your sleep across the whole week before the meet, not just the last night.
- If you sleep badly the night before, keep to your usual routine and warm-up.

Evidence: Mixed · Some support · Sources: [Craven 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y), [Knowles 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2018.01.012)

## Nutrition

### Do you have to eat protein within an hour of training?

**Verdict: Overstated.** No beneficial effect of a specific protein timing showed up on lean mass or strength, so total daily protein matters more.

A systematic review of 65 trials (2,907 participants) found protein supplementation raised lean mass by 0.62 kg (1.4 lb) in adults, independent of when it was taken, with no beneficial effect of a specific timing on lean mass, handgrip or leg-press strength. The ISSN position stand says the best time is likely a matter of individual tolerance, and that the anabolic effect of exercise lasts at least 24 hours.

Who was studied: Wirth 2020: 65 trials with 2,907 healthy adults. Jager 2017: ISSN position stand reviewing the protein and exercise literature.

- Hit your daily protein target first.
- Eat a protein-rich meal before or after training whenever it suits you.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Wirth 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxaa049), [Jäger 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)

### Can your body only use 30 grams of protein per meal?

**Verdict: Not supported.** In one tracer study, 100 g of protein gave a bigger and longer muscle response than 25 g, so a fixed per-meal cap is not supported.

In 36 young men after whole-body exercise, 100 g of protein produced a greater and more prolonged (over 12 hours) anabolic response than 25 g, with a negligible effect on amino acid oxidation. This measured short-term protein synthesis, not muscle gained over months. The ISSN suggests 20 to 40 g per serving, spread every 3 to 4 hours, as a practical guide.

Who was studied: Trommelen 2023: 36 healthy, recreationally active young men, one feeding of 0, 25 or 100 g protein after exercise, followed for 12 hours.

- Use 20 to 40 g per meal as a practical guide for spreading protein through the day.
- A larger protein meal now and then is not wasted.

Evidence: Randomised trial · Thin evidence · Sources: [Trommelen 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2023.101324), [Jäger 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)

### Does a high-protein diet harm healthy kidneys?

**Verdict: Not supported.** In 28 trials of adults without kidney disease, higher protein did not change the drop or rise in kidney filtration rate compared with lower protein.

A meta-analysis defined high protein as at least 1.5 g per kg of body weight, at least 20% of energy, or at least 100 g a day. The change in glomerular filtration rate, a measure of kidney filtering, did not differ between higher and lower protein diets, and the authors concluded high intakes did not adversely affect kidney function in healthy adults. It included trials as short as 5 days and noted an unclear risk of selection bias. It says nothing about people with kidney disease.

Who was studied: Devries 2018: 28 randomised trials, 1,358 adults without kidney disease, high versus normal or lower protein for more than 4 days.

- If you have kidney disease or a family history of it, ask your doctor before changing your protein intake.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Devries 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxy197)

### Does a bigger calorie surplus build more muscle?

**Verdict: Overstated.** In one small trial, eating much more mostly added body fat, not muscle, and the best surplus size is still unknown.

In an eight-week trial of 21 trained lifters (17 finished) at maintenance, a 5% surplus or a 15% surplus, there was no evidence of group differences in muscle thickness or squat max. Faster body-mass gain mainly predicted skinfold (fat) gain, though the 15% group did raise bench press more. A review says the surplus needed to support muscle gain is unknown and has not been validated in lifters.

Who was studied: Helms 2023: 21 trained lifters randomised to maintenance, 5% or 15% surplus for 8 weeks, 17 completers. Slater 2019: narrative review.

- Start with a small surplus and adjust to your progress instead of eating as much as you can.

Evidence: Mixed · Thin evidence · Sources: [Helms 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-023-00651-y), [Slater 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131)

### Is creatine bad for your kidneys?

**Verdict: Not supported.** In studies of healthy people, creatine did not damage the kidneys at the amounts and durations tested.

A systematic review and meta-analysis (15 studies in the review, 6 in the pooled analysis) concluded creatine supplementation does not induce renal damage in the studied amounts and durations. The ISSN position stand states that short and long-term supplementation, up to 30 g a day for 5 years, is safe and well tolerated in healthy people. The pooled analysis had only 6 studies, and none of this speaks for people with kidney disease.

Who was studied: De Souza 2019: 15 studies, 6 pooled. Kreider 2017: ISSN review covering healthy people taking up to 30 g a day for 5 years.

- If you have kidney disease or take medicines that affect the kidneys, ask your doctor before using creatine.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [de Souza e Silva 2019](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.jrn.2019.05.004), [Kreider 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z)

### Does creatine cause hair loss?

**Verdict: Overstated.** The worry comes from one small study showing higher DHT; a 12-week trial that measured hair found no difference from placebo.

The concern is that creatine raises dihydrotestosterone (DHT), a hormone linked to hair loss. In 20 rugby players, creatine raised DHT by 56% after a loading week and 40% after maintenance, but hair was not measured. In a 12-week trial of 45 resistance-trained men, 5 g a day made no difference to DHT, hair density, follicle count or thickness. Both studies were in young men, and only one trial measured hair.

Who was studied: Lak 2025: 45 resistance-trained men (38 finished), 5 g a day or placebo for 12 weeks. Van der Merwe 2009: 20 college rugby players, 3 weeks, crossover.

- See a doctor or dermatologist for new hair loss, since creatine is one of many possible causes.

Evidence: Randomised trial · Thin evidence · Sources: [Lak 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2025.2495229), [van der Merwe 2009](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0b013e3181b8b52f)

### Do you need supplements to make progress?

**Verdict: Not supported.** The IOC says supplements make a minor contribution and that only a few have good evidence of benefit.

The IOC consensus statement on supplements for high-performance athletes says nutrition usually makes a small but valuable contribution to performance, and supplements a minor part of that. Only a few, including caffeine, creatine, specific buffering agents and nitrate, have good evidence. Some products can harm health, and inadvertently taking banned substances is a known risk. It advises a full nutritional assessment before any decision.

Who was studied: Maughan 2018: IOC consensus statement by 25 authors on supplements for high-performance athletes. It is an expert review, not a trial.

- Fix training, protein, total food and sleep first, and add a supplement only if a gap remains.
- If you compete in a tested federation, check any product against its anti-doping rules.

Evidence: Position stand · Well supported · Sources: [Maughan 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027)

### Does caffeine help you lift more?

**Verdict: Mostly holds.** Caffeine gave a small boost to strength and power in a meta-analysis, clearer for upper-body strength than lower-body.

A meta-analysis of 10 studies on strength and 10 on power found caffeine improved both (effect sizes 0.20 and 0.17). Upper-body strength improved significantly (0.21), but lower-body strength did not reach significance (0.15). The authors noted a shortage of data in women. The IOC lists caffeine among the few supplements with good evidence and advises trying any supplement in training first.

Who was studied: Grgic 2018: 10 placebo-controlled studies on one-rep-max strength and 10 on vertical jump power.

- Test caffeine in training before using it on meet day.
- Count your daily caffeine and watch your sleep.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Grgic 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-018-0216-0), [Maughan 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027)

## Diet

### Is low-carb better than low-fat for fat loss?

**Verdict: Not supported.** Trials and pooled analyses found similar weight loss on low-carb and low-fat diets, with differences too small to matter.

In a 12-month trial of 609 adults, weight change was -5.3 kg (11.7 lb) on a healthy low-fat diet and -6.0 kg (13.2 lb) on a healthy low-carb diet, a gap of 0.7 kg that was not significant. A meta-analysis of 32 controlled feeding studies found fat loss slightly greater on lower-fat diets (16 g a day). A network analysis of 48 trials found the largest losses with low-carb and low-fat diets and minimal differences between named diets.

Who was studied: Gardner 2018: 609 adults, 12 months. Hall 2017: 32 controlled feeding studies. Johnston 2014: 48 trials, 7,286 adults with overweight or obesity.

- Pick the pattern you can keep up, since both lose weight when calories fall.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Gardner 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.0245), [Hall 2017](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2017.01.052), [Johnston 2014](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2014.10397)

### Does intermittent fasting burn more fat than ordinary dieting?

**Verdict: Overstated.** With calories matched, trials of time-restricted eating mostly found no significant extra weight loss, though one meta-analysis found a small edge.

In a 12-month trial of 139 adults, time-restricted eating lost 8.0 kg (17.6 lb) versus 6.3 kg (13.9 lb) with calorie restriction, not a significant gap. In a 12-week feeding trial with matched calories, weight fell 2.3 versus 2.6 kg. A 12-week trial of 16:8 eating found no difference between groups. A meta-analysis of 30 studies found about 1.5 kg (3.2 lb) more weight lost than isocaloric controls. This covers eating windows, not alternate-day fasting.

Who was studied: Liu 2022: 139 adults with obesity, 12 months. Maruthur 2024: 41 adults, 12 weeks. Lowe 2020: 116 adults, 12 weeks. Fernandes-Alves 2026: 30 studies, 1,341 adults.

- Use a fasting window only if it makes eating less easier for you, and keep the calorie deficit the same.
- If you take medication or have a medical condition, ask your clinician before fasting.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Some support · Sources: [Liu 2022](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833), [Maruthur 2024](https://doi.org/10.7326/M23-3132), [Lowe 2020](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.4153), [Fernandes-Alves 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuaf053)

### Do six small meals a day speed up your metabolism?

**Verdict: Not supported.** Eating more often did not raise energy expenditure, and fewer meals did not hurt; meal count mainly affects hunger.

A network meta-analysis of 22 trials (647 participants) found that, on equal calories, 2 meals a day probably reduced body weight slightly versus 3 meals (-1.02 kg) or 6 meals (-1.29 kg), and energy intake was not affected. The authors saw little robust evidence that reducing meal frequency helps. The ISSN position says more frequent meals do not appear to raise thermogenesis, total energy expenditure or resting metabolic rate, but may reduce hunger.

Who was studied: Schwingshackl 2020: 22 trials, 647 participants, at least 2 weeks each. LaBounty 2011: ISSN position stand on meal frequency.

- Choose the number of meals that keeps hunger manageable and fits your day.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Schwingshackl 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/advances/nmaa056), [La Bounty 2011](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-8-4)

### Is fasted cardio better for fat loss?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Fasted exercise burns slightly more fat during the session, but body-mass and body-fat changes were similar in the small review that tracked them.

A meta-analysis of 27 studies (273 participants) found fat oxidation during exercise was higher fasted, by about 3 g. A systematic review of 5 studies (96 participants) compared fasted and fed exercise on weight and body composition and found trivial differences between groups, while warning that the data were limited.

Who was studied: Hackett 2017: 5 studies, 96 healthy adults. Vieira 2016: 27 studies, 273 adults, exercise of 120 minutes or less.

- Train at the time of day you will stick to, since the evidence shows no clear fat-loss edge from fasting.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Some support · Sources: [Hackett 2017](https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk2040043), [Vieira 2016](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114516003160)

### Do diet breaks speed up fat loss?

**Verdict: Depends.** Results are mixed: one trial found more fat loss with breaks, two syntheses found no difference in fat lost, with less hunger or less metabolic slowing.

In 51 men with obesity, 2-week diet blocks alternating with 2-week breaks gave more fat loss among the 36 who completed the protocol (12.3 versus 8.0 kg). In 61 resistance-trained adults over 12 weeks, 3-week diet blocks with 1-week breaks gave no difference in fat or lean mass but lower hunger. A meta-analysis of 12 trials found no group difference in body measures, and a smaller drop in resting metabolic rate with breaks.

Who was studied: Byrne 2018: 51 men with obesity randomised, 16 weeks of dieting. Peos 2021: 61 resistance-trained adults, 12 weeks. Poon 2025: 12 trials, 881 participants.

- If long diets drain you, try a planned week at maintenance calories, and expect similar fat loss.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Some support · Sources: [Byrne 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2017.206), [PEOS 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002636), [Poon 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuad168)

### If you track everything and cannot lose weight, is your metabolism broken?

**Verdict: Overstated.** In one careful study, people who said they could not lose weight had normal energy expenditure and were eating more than they reported.

In 10 adults with a history of diet resistance, measured energy expenditure was within 5 percent of predicted. They under-reported food intake by 47% on average and over-reported activity by 51%. Metabolic rate does fall a little during a diet, by about 54 kcal a day in 171 women who lost weight, far too little to explain no weight loss. The first study was small and specific, so it cannot prove this for everyone.

Who was studied: Lichtman 1992: 10 adults with diet resistance, compared with 80 adults without it, over 14 days. Martins 2020: 171 overweight women followed for 2 years.

- Weigh your food for two weeks and log it all, including oils, drinks and weekend meals.
- Judge progress by weekly average weight, not daily readings.

Evidence: Mixed · Thin evidence · Sources: [Lichtman 1992](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199212313272701), [Martins 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqaa086)

### Does starvation mode stop weight loss?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Energy expenditure does fall during a diet, but measured extra drops were tens to about a hundred kcal a day, not a stop to weight loss.

In 171 overweight women who lost an average of 12 kg, metabolic adaptation was about 54 kcal a day after weight loss and was not present at 1 or 2 years in a subset. In 32 men on a half-calorie diet for 3 weeks, resting energy expenditure fell 266 kcal a day, of which 108 kcal was adaptive thermogenesis (72 kcal after accounting for lean mass). A review notes restriction brings changes that attenuate weight loss and promote regain.

Who was studied: Martins 2020: 171 overweight women, 2 years. Muller 2015: 32 non-obese men through overfeeding, 3 weeks at half energy needs, and refeeding. Trexler 2014: review.

- Keep your deficit moderate, and recheck your intake and weight trend over 2 to 4 weeks before changing anything.

Evidence: Mixed · Some support · Sources: [Martins 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqaa086), [Müller 2015](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.109173), [Trexler 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-7)

### Does spot reduction work?

**Verdict: Not supported.** Training one body part did not reduce fat there in most studies, though one small trial of overweight men found otherwise.

A meta-analysis of 13 controlled studies (1,158 participants) comparing trained and untrained limbs found no local effect (pooled effect size -0.03). Six weeks of abdominal exercise in 24 sedentary adults did not reduce abdominal fat, and 12 weeks of single-leg training reduced arm and trunk fat but not leg fat. A 2023 trial of 16 overweight men found more trunk fat loss when abdominal work was added to running, so the question is not fully closed.

Who was studied: Ramirez-Campillo 2022: 13 studies, 1,158 participants. Vispute 2011: 24 adults, 6 weeks. Ramirez-Campillo 2013: 11 adults, 12 weeks. Brobakken 2023: 16 men, 10 weeks.

- Lose fat through your overall energy balance, and train any muscle for strength and shape.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Some support · Sources: [Ramirez-Campillo 2021](https://doi.org/10.5114/hm.2022.110373), [Vispute 2011](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181fb4a46), [Ramírez-Campillo 2013](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e31827e8681), [Brobakken 2023](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.15853)

### Is there one best diet for weight loss?

**Verdict: Not supported.** Pooled trials found modest weight loss on most diets, with minimal differences between them and effects fading by 12 months.

A network meta-analysis of 48 randomised trials (7,286 people) found the largest weight loss with low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets, and minimal differences between named diets. A larger network analysis of 121 trials (21,942 patients) found low-carb and low-fat diets similar at six months (4.63 versus 4.37 kg), and that at 12 months the effects on weight and cardiovascular risk largely disappeared. These trials cannot say which diet suits one person.

Who was studied: Johnston 2014: 48 trials, 7,286 adults with overweight or obesity. Ge 2020: 121 trials, 21,942 adults, 6 and 12 month follow-up.

- Choose a pattern you can sustain, and track the result over months, not weeks.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Well supported · Sources: [Johnston 2014](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2014.10397), [Ge 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.m696)

### Does eating late at night make you gain fat?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Two small crossover trials found late eating changed hunger or energy use, but on matched calories weight loss did not differ in the trial that measured it.

In 16 adults with overweight or obesity eating identical food early or late, late eating raised hunger and lowered waketime energy expenditure. In 30 adults on two 4-week calorie-restricted diets, morning-loaded or evening-loaded meals produced no difference in energy expenditure or weight loss, though the morning-loaded diet reduced hunger. Both studies were small and short, so the effect of meal timing on long-term weight is not settled.

Who was studied: Vujovic 2022: 16 adults with overweight or obesity, randomised crossover. Ruddick-Collins 2022: 30 adults with overweight or obesity, two 4-week diets.

- Eat when it helps you control hunger and total intake, since matched calories gave the same weight loss.

Evidence: Randomised trial · Thin evidence · Sources: [Vujović 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2022.09.007), [Ruddick-Collins 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2022.08.001)

### Can you out-train a bad diet?

**Verdict: Overstated.** In pooled trials, exercise alone produced modest weight loss, so it is a weak tool for offsetting a poor diet.

An overview of 12 systematic reviews (149 studies) in adults with overweight or obesity found exercise training led to weight loss of 1.5 to 3.5 kg (3.3 to 7.7 lb) and fat loss of 1.3 to 2.6 kg. Resistance training reduced lean mass loss during weight loss. In the one review that looked at it, exercise had no significant effect on weight maintenance. The overview did not test diet quality directly, but it shows exercise helps body composition even though it moves weight modestly.

Who was studied: Bellicha 2021: overview of 12 systematic reviews and meta-analyses covering 149 studies in adults with overweight or obesity.

- Use food to set your calorie deficit and training to protect muscle and fitness.

Evidence: Review · Some support · Sources: [Bellicha 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13256)

## Health

### Does sleep matter for fat loss if your calories are right?

**Verdict: Mostly holds.** In one small trial on the same diet, short sleep cut the share of weight lost as fat by 55% and raised the loss of fat-free mass.

In a crossover trial, 10 overweight adults ate a moderate calorie deficit for 14 days with 8.5 or 5.5 hours of sleep opportunity. With less sleep, the share of weight lost as fat fell by 55% (1.4 versus 0.6 kg) and fat-free mass loss rose by 60% (1.5 versus 2.4 kg), with increased hunger. The study was small, short and run in a lab, so treat the size of the effect with care.

Who was studied: Nedeltcheva 2010: 10 overweight adults (3 women, 7 men), mean age 41, 14 days at 8.5 or 5.5 hours of sleep opportunity, crossover.

- Protect your sleep while dieting, and do not treat it as optional.

Evidence: Randomised trial · Thin evidence · Sources: [Nedeltcheva 2010](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-153-7-201010050-00006)

### Is Zone 2 the best cardio intensity for health?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Higher fitness is linked to lower risk of death, but that is about being fit, and a 2025 review found evidence does not support Zone 2 as the best intensity.

An overview of 26 systematic reviews found high versus low cardiorespiratory fitness associated with lower all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 0.47). In 122,007 patients, the lowest risk of death was in the fittest group. These are associations with fitness, not with a training zone. A narrative review concluded current evidence does not support Zone 2 (low intensity below the lactate threshold) as the best intensity, and suggested higher intensities matter, especially at lower training volumes.

Who was studied: Lang 2024: overview of 26 systematic reviews. Mandsager 2018: 122,007 adults having treadmill tests, 13,637 deaths. Storoschuk 2025: narrative review.

- Build fitness with the sessions you will repeat.
- If time is short, include some harder efforts alongside easy ones.

Evidence: Mixed · Some support · Sources: [Mandsager 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.3605), [Lang 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-107849), [Storoschuk 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02261-y)

### Do you need 10,000 steps a day?

**Verdict: Overstated.** Risk of death fell steeply up to roughly 5,000 to 7,000 steps in a large review, with 10,000 not a threshold; these are observational data.

A review of 57 studies found the link between steps and mortality bending at around 5,000 to 7,000 steps a day. Compared with 2,000, 7,000 steps was associated with 47% lower all-cause mortality. An earlier analysis of 15 cohorts found risk fell until 6,000 to 8,000 steps in adults 60 and over and 8,000 to 10,000 in younger adults. These are observational, so fitter, healthier people may also simply walk more.

Who was studied: Ding 2025: 57 studies from 35 cohorts of adults. Paluch 2022: 15 cohorts, 47,471 adults, 3,013 deaths, median follow-up 7.1 years.

- Aim to walk somewhat more than you do now; benefits appeared well below 10,000 steps.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Some support · Sources: [Ding 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2468-2667(25)00164-1), [Paluch 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2468-2667(21)00302-9)

### Does lifting weights lower the risk of early death?

**Verdict: Mostly holds.** People who lifted had lower mortality in cohort studies, with the biggest drop at roughly 30 to 60 minutes a week; this shows association, not proof.

A meta-analysis of 10 studies found any resistance training was associated with 15% lower all-cause mortality, with the largest reduction (27%) at about 60 minutes a week. A meta-analysis of 16 cohort studies found muscle-strengthening linked to 10 to 17% lower risk of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease and cancer, flattening at 30 to 60 minutes a week. People who lift may differ in other ways. WHO guidelines recommend regular muscle strengthening.

Who was studied: Shailendra 2022: 10 studies of adults, 6 for all-cause mortality. Momma 2022: 16 prospective cohort studies of adults. Bull 2020: WHO guidelines.

- Include muscle-strengthening work most weeks; the link was strongest around 30 to 60 minutes a week.

Evidence: Meta-analysis · Some support · Sources: [Shailendra 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2022.03.020), [Momma 2022](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061), [Bull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955)

### Does lifting weights stunt growth in teenagers?

**Verdict: Not supported.** A review of 22 youth training programs found no effect on height or weight growth in pre- and early-pubertal youth, with supervised programs relatively safe.

An evidence-based review of 22 reports found experimental resistance training programs, mostly of 8 to 12 weeks, did not influence growth in height and weight of pre- and early-adolescent youth. Only 10 studies monitored injuries and only three injuries were reported. The authors judged supervised, low instructor-to-participant programs relatively safe. The American Academy of Pediatrics has a policy statement on benefits and risks. The data cover short programs, not years of training.

Who was studied: Malina 2006: 22 reports of resistance training in pre- and early-pubertal youth, mostly 8 to 12 weeks. Stricker 2020: American Academy of Pediatrics policy statement.

- Start under qualified supervision with light loads and technique first, and add load gradually.
- Ask a paediatrician before starting if there is a medical condition.

Evidence: Review · Some support · Sources: [Malina 2006](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.jsm.0000248843.31874.be), [Stricker 2020](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2020-1011)

### Is BMI useless for lifters?

**Verdict: Overstated.** BMI misclassifies some muscular athletes, but it is a rough screen that also misses excess fat in many other people.

In 226 college athletes and 213 non-athletes, BMI had high sensitivity but low specificity for fatness in male athletes, and the authors called for different cut points; football linemen had an optimal cut point of 34.1. A meta-analysis of 25 articles found common BMI obesity cut-offs miss about half of people with excess body fat. A review of 31 papers found waist-to-height ratio discriminated cardiometabolic risk better than BMI.

Who was studied: Ode 2007: 226 college athletes and 213 non-athletes. Okorodudu 2010: 25 articles, 31,968 people. Ashwell 2012: 31 papers, over 300,000 adults.

- Track waist circumference, photos and lifts alongside body weight.
- Ask a clinician to interpret health numbers instead of relying on BMI alone.

Evidence: Mixed · Some support · Sources: [Ode 2007](https://doi.org/10.1249/01.mss.0000247008.19127.3e), [Okorodudu 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2010.5), [Ashwell 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-789x.2011.00952.x)
