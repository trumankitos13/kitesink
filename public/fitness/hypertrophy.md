# Hypertrophy: KiteSink fitness field guide

Training for muscle size. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, how well supported it is, and its sources.

## Guidance

### More weekly sets build more muscle, with diminishing returns

**Muscle growth rises with weekly hard sets per muscle, but each added set gives less than the one before it.**

A meta-regression of 67 studies and 2,058 participants (79% men, average age about 25) found that gains in muscle size rose as weekly set volume rose, with diminishing returns. Strength gains levelled off much sooner. The model gave a set that only trains a muscle as a helper half the credit of a direct set.

- Add sets slowly, and only when progress at your current number has stalled.

Volume · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Pelland 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)

### 12 to 20 weekly sets per muscle is a reasonable range for trained lifters

**A review of trained young men found 12-20 sets per muscle per week a sensible target. Treat it as a guide, not a cap.**

A systematic review of seven trials in men with at least a year of lifting compared under 12, 12-20 and over 20 weekly sets (six trials were pooled). Moderate and high volumes did not differ for the quadriceps or biceps. High volume did better for the triceps. The newer 67-study analysis found diminishing returns but no clear ceiling, so 20 is not a hard limit.

- If you train a muscle fewer than 12 sets a week and progress is slow, test a small increase.

Volume · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Baz-Valle 2022](https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2022-0017), [Pelland 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)

### Almost any structured programme beats no training, and multiple sets rank highest for size

**In a network meta-analysis, every prescription beat no exercise, and multi-set programmes ranked highest for hypertrophy.**

The hypertrophy network pooled 119 trials and 3,364 healthy adults. All 12 prescriptions beat non-exercise controls and promoted size comparably. The highest-ranked for hypertrophy was higher-load, multiple-set, twice-weekly training. Rankings are not proof that the top option is better than the rest.

- Choose a plan you can follow for months, with more than one set per exercise.

Volume · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Currier 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807)

### Sets ended closer to failure tended to build more muscle

**In a meta-regression, hypertrophy improved as sets ended nearer to failure, while strength gains were similar across a wide range.**

The analysis estimated reps in reserve (RIR, the reps left before failure) for each study from its description, so the exact shape of the curve is unclear, as the authors state. Muscle size had a clear negative slope with RIR. For strength, the intervals included zero, meaning no clear relationship.

- End most hard sets with only a few reps left in reserve.

Effort · Intermediate · Well supported · Sources: [Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)

### Going to absolute failure has not shown a clear edge over stopping just short

**Training to momentary failure was not clearly better for muscle than stopping close to it.**

A meta-analysis of 15 studies found no advantage for momentary failure (effect size 0.12, interval -0.13 to 0.37). In a trial of 18 trained people over 8 weeks, legs trained to failure or to about 1-2 reps in reserve grew the same. A 13-study review found no hypertrophy difference when volume was equal.

- Use 1-2 reps in reserve on most sets, and save true failure for safe exercises such as machines.

Effort · Intermediate · Well supported · Sources: [Refalo 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y), [Refalo 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021), [Vieira 2021](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003936)

### Reps in reserve is a useful guide, but most lifters misjudge it

**RIR tracks how hard a set was, but people tend to underpredict how many reps they have left, by about one rep on average.**

In 29 squatters, an RIR-based effort rating moved in step with bar speed. A scoping review and meta-analysis of 12 studies and 414 people found lifters underpredicted reps to failure by 0.95 reps on average, with wide variation. Guesses were better close to the end of a set and on sets of 12 reps or fewer.

- Rate RIR on sets of about 12 reps or fewer.
- Occasionally check yourself with one set to failure on a safe exercise.

Effort · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Zourdos 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001049), [Halperin 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x), [Pelland 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01667-2)

### The effective-reps idea is a useful rule of thumb, not a measured law

**The idea that only the last few reps near failure drive growth fits the direction of the data. No fixed cut-off has been shown.**

Effective (or stimulating) reps is a coaching model. It matches the finding that sets nearer failure tend to build more size. The meta-regression showed a gradual slope with estimated RIR, not a cliff, and its authors note the RIR estimates are rough. The reviews of RIR methods find they differ in accuracy, so a hard rule such as "the last five reps" is not supported.

- Use the idea to remind yourself to make sets hard, not to count reps.

Effort · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2), [Pelland 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01667-2), [Halperin 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x)

### Where the effective-reps model stops working

**The model assumes a rep near failure is worth the same at any load and that you can tell which reps those are. Both are shaky.**

It also leaves out exercise choice, muscle length and fatigue across sets. A trial that took sets to failure with heavier and lighter loads found similar fibre glycogen depletion in both fibre types, which fits with load mattering less at failure. Sets that stopped about 1-2 reps short still grew muscle as well as failure sets, and RIR is hard to judge exactly.

- Treat the model as a teaching tool and judge your plan by results over months.

Effort · Advanced · Thin evidence · Sources: [Morton 2019](https://doi.org/10.1113/jp278056), [Refalo 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021), [Halperin 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x)

### Light and heavy loads build similar muscle when sets are hard

**Muscle growth was similar with light and heavy loads when sets were taken close to failure.**

A meta-analysis of 45 studies found similar hypertrophy for loads above and at or below 60% of one-rep max (1RM). Another of 28 studies (747 adults), using sets to volitional failure, agreed. In 49 resistance-trained men over 12 weeks, 20-25 reps at about 30-50% 1RM grew muscle as much as 8-12 reps at 75-90%.

- Pick a load you can control through the full range and take the set close to failure.

Load and reps · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Refalo 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2021.1898094), [Lopez 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000002585), [Morton 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00154.2016)

### Heavier loads give better strength gains

**If one-rep-max strength matters to you, train with heavier loads at least part of the time.**

The network meta-analysis found loads above 80% of 1RM maximised strength gains. Pooled results of sets to failure showed high-load training beat low-load for strength. A separate analysis found higher loads improved 1RM strength, with an effect size of 0.34. Muscle size did not follow the same pattern.

- Include some sets in the 5-8 rep range if you also want a stronger one-rep max.

Load and reps · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Currier 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807), [Lopez 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000002585), [Refalo 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2021.1898094)

### Adding reps and adding weight both work as progression

**In new lifters, progressing by reps or by load gave the same strength and muscle gains over 10 weeks.**

Thirty-nine previously untrained people (20 men, 19 women) had one leg progress by load and the other by reps. Leg extension 1RM and thigh muscle area rose by similar amounts in both. This was a short trial in beginners, so it may not transfer to advanced lifters.

- Add reps within a range first, then raise the load and drop back to the low end of the range.

Programming · Beginner · Thin evidence · Sources: [Chaves 2024](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2256-5857)

### With weekly sets matched, training a muscle more often adds little growth

**Once total weekly sets are equal, how often you train a muscle makes little difference to size.**

The meta-regression found the effect of frequency on hypertrophy compatible with a negligible effect. In 21 people given the same weekly volume over 9 weeks, two sessions a week and four sessions a week gave the same muscle and strength gains. In 24 men, training one or three times a week gave similar thigh results.

- Pick the number of weekly sessions you can keep, then spread your sets across them.

Frequency and splits · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Pelland 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w), [Hamarsland 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2021.789403), [Neves 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0276154)

### Full-body and split routines give similar results

**A meta-analysis found no meaningful difference in strength or muscle size between split and full-body routines.**

Fourteen studies with 392 participants compared the two. Gains in bench press and leg strength, arm and thigh muscle area and lean mass were similar. The review could not say what happens when volume differs, so choose the layout by schedule and preference.

- Choose the split that fits your week and that you enjoy.

Frequency and splits · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Ramos-Campo 2024](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000004774)

### Use a full range of motion by default; lengthened partials also work

**Full range of motion is the safe default. Partial reps done in the stretched half held up well in newer trials.**

A meta-analysis of 16 studies found full range beat partial range for lower-limb muscle growth (effect size 0.88) and strength. No clear difference appeared in muscle thickness overall. A 12-week trial across 15 sites (163 and 134 people in the two arms) found lengthened-partial and full-range training practically equivalent for arm and thigh size, and an 8-week trial in 13 trained people agreed.

- Move through the full range you can control, and keep partial reps for the stretched end.

Exercises and rest · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Pallarés 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14006), [Gschneidner 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2025.2567805), [Havers 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsc.70087)

### Loading a muscle in its stretched position grew more in several trials

**Exercises that load a muscle when it is long gave bigger gains than versions that load it short, in trials that compared them.**

Seated leg curls grew the whole hamstrings more than prone leg curls (+14% against +9%, 20 adults, 12 weeks). Overhead cable triceps extensions beat neutral-arm ones (+19.9% against +13.9%, 21 adults, 12 weeks). In 42 young women, calf raises done in the lower, stretched half grew the medial calf more than full or top-half range.

- Include one exercise per muscle that loads it stretched, such as seated leg curls or overhead triceps extensions.

Exercises and rest · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Maeo 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000002523), [Maeo 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279), [Kassiano 2023](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000004460)

### Rest long enough to repeat your reps; 2 to 5 minutes gave similar growth in trained men

**Longer rest keeps reps up across sets. In trained men, resting 2 or 5 minutes gave similar muscle gains.**

A review of 35 studies found 3-5 minutes rest allowed more reps over several sets. In 13 strength-trained men, 2 and 5 minute rests gave similar muscle and strength gains over two 3-month blocks. A bench press needed 3-5 minutes to hold reps, a single-joint exercise about 2. In 12 untrained men, 2.5 minutes beat 1 minute for arm size.

- Rest about 3 minutes between heavy compound sets and about 2 minutes between isolation sets.

Exercises and rest · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [de Salles 2009](https://doi.org/10.2165/11315230-000000000-00000), [Ahtiainen 2005](https://doi.org/10.1519/15604.1), [Senna 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001142), [Buresh 2009](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e318185f14a)

### Lift under control; very slow reps are not needed

**Controlled reps work. Reps of 10 seconds or more are best avoided, and slowing the lowering phase had no clear extra effect on size.**

An umbrella review of 14 meta-analyses suggested avoiding very slow reps of 10 seconds or more. A meta-analysis of nine studies found uncertain effects of slower lowering on hypertrophy. A review of six studies in 119 untrained people was mixed, and a tempo review notes the evidence is not clear-cut.

- Lower the weight in about 2 seconds and lift without bouncing.

Exercises and rest · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Bernárdez-Vázquez 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2022.949021), [Amdi 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2025.2535198), [Hackett 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2018.1434563), [Wilk 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01465-2)

### Drop sets match straight sets for growth and can save time

**Drop sets built about the same muscle as normal sets in a pooled analysis, so the case for them is time and variety.**

A meta-analysis of six studies (142 adults) found both drop sets and normal sets increased muscle size, with no significant difference between them. A systematic review of advanced techniques also lists drop sets as a time-saving tool but says evidence is too thin for firm dosing guidance.

- Add one drop set at the end of an isolation exercise when you are short on time.

Special techniques · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Sødal 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-023-00620-5), [Krzysztofik 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph16244897)

### Supersets let you do the same work in less time

**Supersets gave similar volume and similar strength and size results in shorter sessions.**

A meta-analysis of 19 studies with 313 people compared supersets with straight sets. Total reps and volume load were similar and sessions were shorter. Supersets felt harder and raised markers of exertion. Pairing opposing muscles kept volume up better than pairing the same muscle.

- Pair opposing muscles, such as chest and back, when you are short on time.

Special techniques · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Zhang 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02176-8)

### Rest-pause and cluster sets are fatigue and time tools, not growth boosters

**Pooled reviews found cluster sets and similar set structures give about the same size and strength as straight sets.**

A review of 17 studies found no set structure beat another for strength or hypertrophy, and a review of 29 studies found cluster sets matched traditional sets, possibly with less fatigue. One trial in 18 trained people found rest-pause grew thigh thickness more but not other sites.

- Use cluster or rest-pause sets when you want heavy reps with less fatigue, not as a growth hack.

Special techniques · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Prestes 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001923), [Jukic 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01423-4), [Davies 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01408-3)

### Blood-flow restriction can build size with light loads, but needs care

**Light loads with a cuff that restricts blood flow built muscle similarly to heavy training, but built less strength.**

A meta-analysis found similar hypertrophy for high-load training and low-load training with blood-flow restriction (BFR), while strength favoured high load. A position stand sets out methods and safety for practitioners. Cuff width and pressure matter, and people with vascular or clotting conditions should involve a clinician first.

- Learn cuff width and pressure from a qualified practitioner, and ask a clinician if you have a vascular condition.

Special techniques · Advanced · Some support · Sources: [Lixandrão 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0795-y), [Patterson 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2019.00533)

### Put the exercises that matter most first

**Reps and volume are higher for an exercise placed early in a session, and pre-exhaustion gave no extra benefit.**

A review of exercise order found total repetitions were higher when an exercise was done at the start of a session. A 12-week trial in 39 trained people found pre-exhaustion (isolation before compound) gave no greater benefit than putting compound exercises first.

- Start with the lifts and muscles you most want to improve.

Programming · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Simão 2012](https://doi.org/10.2165/11597240-000000000-00000), [Fisher 2014](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2014-0162)

### Warm up with lighter sets of the first lift; save long stretches for later

**Warming up helps performance. Long static stretches right before lifting can reduce it slightly.**

A meta-analysis of 32 studies found warm-ups improved performance in 79% of measured outcomes. A review found static stretches of 60 seconds or more per muscle lowered performance by 4.6%, against 1.1% for shorter holds, when tested right afterwards. A squat trial found extra heavy or fast warm-up sets gave no clear gain in total reps or volume.

- Do two or three lighter, increasing sets of your first lift before working sets.
- Hold long stretches for after training.

Programming · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Fradkin 2010](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e3181c643a0), [Behm 2016](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2015-0235), [Souza 2024](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.17347)

### Deloads are common practice, but trials do not yet show they are needed

**Coaches use deloads regularly. The one small trial in beginners found cutting volume in weeks 4 and 8 neither helped nor hurt.**

A deload is a period of reduced training stress, defined by consensus among 21 coaches who finished a Delphi study. In 246 strength and physique athletes, deloads came about every 5.6 weeks and lasted about 6.4 days. In 19 untrained men, deload weeks did not change muscle growth over 8 weeks. Experienced lifters were not tested.

- Take a lighter week when performance drops for several sessions or joints ache, rather than on a fixed date.

Programming · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Bell 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0), [Rogerson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y), [Pancar 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-40612-5)

### Periodising your training helps strength more than muscle size

**When volume was equal, planned changes in volume and load helped strength but not muscle size.**

A meta-analysis of 35 studies found periodised training had a small advantage for 1RM strength over non-periodised training. Hypertrophy did not differ between periodised and non-periodised, or between linear and undulating, plans. Undulating beat linear plans for 1RM only in trained people.

- Vary rep ranges across the week if it keeps training interesting, but do not expect it to change size.

Programming · Intermediate · Well supported · Sources: [Moesgaard 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1)

### Muscle size gains in the first weeks partly reflect swelling

**Measured size rises in the first weeks, but some of that early gain is swelling from training stress, not new muscle tissue.**

In 25 sedentary men, thigh muscle area rose 9.6% over 8 weeks, and the authors judged real hypertrophy likely appeared around weeks 3-4. Other researchers found early increases came with swelling markers, and a review argues true hypertrophy appears after roughly 18 sessions. The timing is debated.

- Judge progress over months, not the first three weeks.

Programming · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [DeFreitas 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-011-1905-4), [Damas 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-015-3243-4), [Damas 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3792-9)

### How to read a training study before you act on it

**Check who was studied, for how long, how muscle was measured and how failure was defined before copying a result.**

Most volume and frequency data come from short trials in young people: in the 67-study set, the average age was about 25 and 79% were men. Studies also define failure differently and often do not report reps in reserve. A single trial is weak evidence next to a meta-analysis, and an interval that includes zero means no clear difference.

- Ask who was studied and for how many weeks before applying a finding.

Programming · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [Pelland 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w), [Pelland 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01667-2)

### Short sleep lowers lifting performance

**Sleep loss lowered exercise performance in a large meta-analysis, and one small study found lower muscle protein synthesis after a sleepless night.**

A meta-analysis of 69 publications defined sleep loss as 6 hours or less in 24 and found an average 7.56% drop in performance. A review of 17 studies found restricted sleep reduced force on compound lifts. In 13 adults, one night without sleep cut muscle protein synthesis by 18%. Long-term growth effects were not tested.

- Protect regular, full nights of sleep, especially before heavy sessions.

Recovery · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Craven 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y), [Knowles 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2018.01.012), [Lamon 2021](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.14660)

## Common mistakes

### Judging a session by how sore you are

**Soreness does not show that a session worked, and its absence does not show that it failed.**

A review found muscle damage is not what drives hypertrophy, and protocols causing little damage still grow muscle. Soreness is largest early on and fades as you adapt. Chasing it can lead to piling on volume or exercises you cannot recover from.

- Judge sessions by reps, load and effort recorded in a log.

Recovery · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Damas 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3792-9)

### Repeating the same weights and reps with no log

**Without a record, you cannot tell whether you are adding reps or load, which is what progression means.**

In 39 beginners, adding load and adding reps each raised strength and thigh muscle area over 10 weeks. Both methods need you to know last week's numbers. Writing down weight, reps and effort for each set takes little time and makes stalls easy to spot.

- Log weight, reps and reps in reserve for every working set.

Programming · Beginner · Coaching practice · Sources: [Chaves 2024](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2256-5857)

### Cutting the range of motion to lift heavier

**Shortening the rep to add weight tended to give up size in the lower body. Partials in the stretched half are different.**

A meta-analysis of 16 studies found full range gave larger lower-limb muscle gains than partial range. Lengthened partials, done on purpose in the stretched half with control, matched full range in a large 12-week trial. Half reps that skip the stretched part, done only to move more weight, are the mistake.

- Lower under control to the stretched position and drive up without bouncing.

Exercises and rest · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Pallarés 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14006), [Gschneidner 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2025.2567805)

### Ending every set far from failure

**Sets that stop well short of failure leave growth on the table, and self-chosen loads are often light.**

The meta-regression found muscle size improved as sets ended closer to failure. People who choose their own weights average about 53% of 1RM. Sets that stop when the work first feels uncomfortable can sit many reps from failure. Many beginners find it hard to judge how many reps remain.

- Raise the load or reps until the last rep of each working set looks slow and hard.

Effort · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2), [Steele 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01717-9)

### Jumping to very high volume straight away

**More sets add less each time, so a big jump from your current volume mostly adds fatigue.**

The dose-response analysis shows diminishing returns on weekly sets. A small trial of 16 trained people found a volume built from each person's own logs beat a fixed 22 weekly sets. Coaches therefore add sets in small steps and watch whether performance holds.

- Add 1-3 sets per muscle per week, then check performance for two weeks.

Volume · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Pelland 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w), [Scarpelli 2022](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003558)

### Cutting rest so short that your reps fall

**Very short rests lower the reps you can do on later sets, and with it the total work.**

In a trial, a 1-minute rest gave fewer total reps over five sets than 3-5 minutes on a bench press. A review of 35 studies found 3-5 minutes allowed more reps over several sets. Short rests are fine for small isolation moves, but for compound lifts they cost volume.

- Rest long enough to repeat most of your first set's reps.

Exercises and rest · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Senna 2016](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001142), [de Salles 2009](https://doi.org/10.2165/11315230-000000000-00000)

## Rep ranges and what each is good for

Approximate loads for each rep range, with what research suggests about the result. Percentages are rough and vary by person and lift.

| Reps per set | Rough load (% of 1RM) | What it is good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-5 | Over 85% | Maximal strength; practising heavy lifts | Long rests; technique slips when tired |
| 6-8 | About 75-85% | Strength and size; high-load range in Lopez 2021 | Joint load on heavy compounds |
| 8-12 | About 65-75% | Size; middle ground for most lifts | Stopping far from failure |
| 12-20 | About 55-65% | Size on single-joint and machine lifts | Pump and burn limiting sets before muscle is tired |
| 20-30 | About 30-50% | Size when taken to failure (49 trained men, 12 weeks) | Very hard sets; lower strength gains |

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full body | 2-4 | 2-4 | Beginners; busy people | Long sessions; fatigue from big lifts |
| Upper/lower | 4 | 2 | Most lifters at 4 days | Large sessions in the middle of the week |
| Push/pull/legs | 3 or 6 | 1 or 2 | People who enjoy daily training | 6 days a week is hard to sustain |
| Body-part split | 5-6 | 1 | Experienced lifters wanting high volume per muscle | Each muscle only once a week |
| Hybrid (upper/lower plus extras) | 4-5 | About 2 | People wanting variety | Needs more planning |

## Rest between sets and sets per week: a guide

A reading of the research on rest and weekly sets. Treat each line as a starting point, then adjust to how you recover.

| Question | Starting point | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy compound lift | 3-5 minutes | Held reps across sets on bench press (Senna 2016) |
| Single-joint, lighter lift | About 2 minutes | 2 minutes held reps on a chest fly (Senna 2016) |
| Short on time | Superset opposing muscles | Similar volume and gains, shorter sessions (Zhang 2025) |
| Weekly sets, general | At least 10 per muscle | Umbrella review: at least 10 sets is optimal |
| Weekly sets, trained men | 12-20 per muscle | Baz-Valle 2022; newer data show no clear ceiling |
| Rest vs growth | 2 or 5 minutes | Similar growth in trained men (Ahtiainen 2005) |

## Terms

- **Deload**: A period of reduced training stress meant to lower fatigue and prepare you for later training, as defined by a coach consensus.
- **Effect size and confidence interval**: Effect size is how big a difference is, in standard deviations. The interval shows a plausible range. If it spans zero, there is no clear difference.
- **Hard set**: A working set taken close to failure, usually within a few reps. Warm-up sets and easy sets do not count towards weekly volume.
- **Lengthened-position training**: Loading a muscle while it is stretched, as at the bottom of a curl or squat. Several trials found it grew more than loading it short.
- **Momentary muscular failure**: The point where you cannot finish another rep despite maximal effort. Volitional failure means you stop by choice.
- **One-rep max (1RM)**: The heaviest load you can lift for one full rep. Training loads are often given as a percent of it, such as 70% of 1RM.
- **Periodisation**: Planned changes in volume, load or exercises over weeks and months, such as linear steps or weekly undulation.
- **Progressive overload**: Gradually asking more of the muscle over time, by adding reps, load or sets, so it keeps having a reason to adapt.
- **Rate of perceived exertion (RPE)**: A 1-10 effort scale. On the RIR-based version, RPE 10 means 0 RIR, RPE 9 means 1 RIR, and so on.
- **Repetitions in reserve (RIR)**: The number of reps you could still have done when the set ended. 0 RIR means failure. 2 RIR means two more reps were possible.
- **Volume**: The amount of work. In this page, usually the number of hard sets per muscle per week. Volume load is sets times reps times weight.
