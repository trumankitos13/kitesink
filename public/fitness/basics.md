# Training basics: KiteSink fitness field guide

Starting out, and building a first programme. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, how well supported it is, and its sources.

## Guidance

### A first programme looks much the same whatever the long-term goal

**Health, physique, sport and powerlifting beginners need the same base first. The goal starts to shape the plan at the intermediate stage.**

Lyle McDonald's position is that the first months do one job for everyone: learn the movements, add some muscle and strength across the whole body, let joints and tendons catch up, and form the habit of turning up. A 2026 American College of Sports Medicine overview of 137 systematic reviews fits that view. Resistance training improved strength, size, power and physical function against no exercise, and few details of the prescription changed the main results. He makes one exception: Olympic weightlifting, whose lifts need a coach from the first session.

- Pick a simple whole-body plan and run it for months before you specialise.

### Start with the least training that still makes you progress

**If a small amount of training produces gains, extra work adds risk and uses up the room you will need later.**

McDonald's rule for all training is to get the most adaptation from the least work, and to add more only when progress stops. A beginner who starts on an advanced lifter's volume has nothing left to raise once gains slow, and carries more risk of injury and burnout in the meantime. A meta-analysis of 140 strength studies points the same way: untrained people gained most at a mean load of 60% of one-rep max, trained people at 80%, and the authors read this as support for raising the dose as training status rises. The pull toward doing more is strong because more looks as though it must work better.

- Raise sets, days or load when progress stalls, and not because another routine looks more serious.

### You stop being a beginner when simple training stops producing gains

**Expect roughly 6 to 12 months of proper training before a beginner routine runs out, by McDonald's estimate.**

McDonald lists three signs: sound, repeatable technique on the main exercises, the capacity to finish a full 60 to 90 minute session without being flattened, and some visible muscle gain. His simplest test is that the beginner routine no longer moves strength or size. He keeps a new lifter on beginner training for at least 3 months and often 6, and puts the usual switch at 6 to 12 months of proper training. The 2009 ACSM position stand draws a similar line, describing intermediates as people with about 6 months of consistent training. Switching early gains nothing, because progress never comes this easily again.

- Stay on the simple plan for as long as weights or reps keep rising from week to week.

### Tendons adapt more slowly than strength rises

**Strength can climb by almost a third in two months while tendon stiffness has not yet changed, so add load more slowly than strength allows.**

Eight people did knee-extension training for three months. Strength was up 29.6% after two months, but tendon stiffness and muscle size had not changed by then and rose only by the end. A meta-analysis of 27 studies with 264 participants found that tendons do stiffen with training, that the effect depends on high loading, and that programmes of 12 weeks or more tended to do more. McDonald's coaching reading is that beginners who go heavy or train often too early are the ones who pick up elbow and knee trouble, and that a joint injured once is easily injured again.

- Add load in small steps for the first months, even when a bigger jump feels possible.
- Hold the weight if a joint or tendon stays sore, and see a clinician if the pain persists.

### The ability to work hard is learned in small steps

**New lifters stop long before real fatigue. Pushing a little past the point where you would normally stop resets that limit over time.**

A scoping review found that people left to choose their own weights picked an average of 53% of their one-rep max, which is light for most goals. McDonald describes the same pattern in beginners: they believe they are far more tired than they are, and they stop when a set first becomes uncomfortable. His method is gradual. Each time a lifter does slightly more than they thought they could, the ceiling moves, and over months this adds up to a level of effort they would not have believed at the start. A coach helps by telling real fatigue from giving up. None of this means wrecking yourself in a session.

- Once technique is steady, take one set per exercise a rep or two past where you would usually stop.

### Beginners gain strength on loads as light as 60% of their max

**Untrained people gained the most strength at about 60% of one-rep max in a large meta-analysis, so there is no need to start heavy.**

A meta-analysis of 140 studies found a mean load of 60% of one-rep max (1RM) gave untrained people their largest strength gains, against 80% for trained people. The 2009 ACSM stand gives novices loads they could lift 8 to 12 times. McDonald's reasons for starting light: early gains come mainly from learning the movement, technique is easier to learn below heavy loads, and joints get time to adapt. He keeps an average beginner under about 80% of max, roughly an eight-rep limit, for the first month or two. Heavy loads matter more later: the 2026 ACSM stand found loads of 80% or more enhanced strength.

- Start with a weight you could lift for well over the target reps, and add load while technique holds.

### One set per exercise is enough to start; build to two or three

**A single set gives a new lifter most of the early strength gain. Extra sets add practice and a little more, at the cost of time.**

McDonald starts beginners on one set per exercise and builds toward three over the first weeks. A session of 8 to 10 exercises done once each takes 20 to 30 minutes and still works. The later sets mainly add practice and work capacity, and they help only if fatigue does not spoil technique. Pooled evidence agrees that more is somewhat better: a meta-analysis of 9 studies found higher weekly set volumes gave greater strength gains than low ones (effect size 0.18), and the 2026 ACSM stand found 2 to 3 sets enhanced strength. For basic fitness, he considers one hard set per exercise enough indefinitely.

- Do one set per exercise in the first sessions.
- Add a second and third set over the following weeks if you want more than basic fitness.

### Sets of 8 to 12 suit most beginners; sets of 5 need restraint

**Moderate reps keep the weight modest and give plenty of practice. Low reps work when someone stops you loading the bar too fast.**

The 2009 ACSM stand gives novices loads they can lift 8 to 12 times, and McDonald used the same range with his beginners. He explains the trade-offs. Very high reps bring fatigue that breaks technique late in the set, and a burn that puts new lifters off. Sets of 5 are short enough to keep every rep clean, but uncoached lifters tend to add weight faster than their technique or joints can follow. For most people he advises 8 to 12 reps for one to three sets, with three to five sets of 5 as an option for lifters who have a coach or real self-control.

- Use 8 to 12 reps unless a coach is watching your lifts.

### Train the whole body two or three times a week at first

**Three weekly sessions gave untrained people the largest strength gains in one meta-analysis. Two is close and easier to schedule.**

A meta-analysis of 140 studies found untrained people gained most strength training each muscle group 3 days a week, and trained people 2 days. The 2009 ACSM stand recommends 2 to 3 days a week for novices. A later meta-analysis found frequency had no significant effect on strength once weekly volume was matched, so the gain from a third day probably comes from the extra practice and work. Two sessions can be moved around a busy week, while three need alternate days. McDonald regards two as enough indefinitely for general fitness, and three as close to required for anyone whose sport is lifting.

- Choose two or three non-consecutive days you can keep every week.
- Repeat the same exercises each session so every lift is practised often.

### Free weights and machines build similar strength and size

**A meta-analysis found no difference in muscle growth, and strength rose most on whichever equipment people had trained with.**

Thirteen studies with 1,016 participants compared the two for at least six weeks. Muscle growth, jump height and strength compared across test types did not differ. Strength tested with free weights rose more after free-weight training, and machine-tested strength tended to rise more after machine training, so the gain is specific to the tool. The 2026 ACSM stand also found that equipment type did not consistently change outcomes. The authors of the meta-analysis conclude that the choice comes down to preference and goals. A lifter who will be tested on a barbell lift has to train that lift.

- Choose the equipment you can use well and consistently.
- If a barbell lift is your test, train it with a barbell.

### Multi-joint lifts save time; single-joint lifts fill the gaps

**With total work matched, multi-joint training gave larger strength gains than single-joint training and the same change in body composition.**

Thirty-six active men trained three times a week for eight weeks using only multi-joint or only single-joint exercises, with total work matched. Fat-free mass rose equally. The multi-joint group gained more one-rep-max strength (squat 13.8% against 8.3%, bench press 10.9% against 8.1%) and more aerobic capacity. McDonald's practical point is efficiency: one lower-body lift, one push, one pull and a trunk exercise cover most of the body. He keeps single-joint work for muscles that a compound lift trains poorly in a given person, and for the biceps, which have no true compound exercise.

- Build each session around a lower-body lift, a push and a pull.
- Add single-joint work where the big lifts leave a muscle undertrained.

### A machine circuit is a sound first programme

**For an untrained adult who wants general fitness, simple machines taught over a few sessions do the job with little to learn.**

McDonald started most beginner clients, mainly untrained adults over 30, on machines. He added exercises across the first three sessions, used one easy set of 8 to 12 on each, and added weight once 12 came easily. His reasoning: a leg press or machine chest press is safe after a session or two of teaching, and squatting or benching with a barbell is not. After six to eight weeks he brought in free-weight or more complex exercises on one day of the week, while the familiar ones carried the hard work on the other days. Trials showing machines and free weights build similar size support the approach.

- Learn a few machine exercises first if nobody is available to teach you barbell lifts.
- Introduce new exercises a few at a time after six to eight weeks.

### Keep the same exercises for the first six to eight weeks

**A new movement needs weeks of practice before you can load it or judge it, so early variety slows learning.**

McDonald prefers a beginner to repeat one set of exercises at every session, because frequent practice of the same movements is the quickest way through the early learning phase. Plans that alternate two different workouts halve the practice each lift gets. He holds a set of exercises for about six to eight weeks before trialling others, so that each movement gets a fair test. To judge whether an exercise suits a person he uses three signs: which muscle is felt working, where the pump appears, and where soreness shows up the next day. He calls all three imperfect but worth noting.

- Run one set of exercises for six to eight weeks before swapping any.
- Give each new exercise several weeks before you judge it.

### Find first weights by starting with the lightest option available

**Begin with the empty bar, the empty sled or the first plate on the stack, and add weight in later sets or sessions.**

McDonald's rule is to err light. Too little weight costs nothing, and too much can injure a new lifter or leave them too sore to come back. The first sessions are for technique, breathing and a steady speed, and nobody can attend to those under a load that scares them. He notes one catch: a weight can be so light that the lifter cannot feel the movement, so finding the right load may take two or three weeks. Starting points differ widely. A 20 kg (45 lb) bar suits most young men on the bench press, while many women begin with a dumbbell of about 2.5 kg (5 lb) in each hand.

- Start each new exercise with the lightest load the equipment allows.
- Add a little each set or session until you can feel the target muscles working.

### Use a rep range and add weight when you reach the top of it

**Work in a range such as 8 to 12. When the top number arrives in good form, add a little weight and build the reps back up.**

The usual name is double progression: reps rise first, then weight. McDonald uses it with beginners doing one work set. When 12 clean reps arrive and the final two are not a grind, the load goes up slightly, reps fall to 8 or 9, and the climb starts again. The 2009 ACSM stand gives a similar rule: raise the load by 2 to 10% once you can do one to two reps more than the target. In 39 previously untrained people followed for 10 weeks, progressing by adding reps or by adding load gave the same gains in strength and muscle size, so the exact method matters less than having one.

- Pick a rep range for each exercise and log every set.
- Add the smallest available weight when you reach the top of the range with clean reps.

### Judge a weight jump as a percentage of what is on the bar

**The same plate is a small step on a heavy lift and a huge one on a light lift, which hits smaller lifters and small exercises hardest.**

Going from 9 kg to 11.3 kg (20 to 25 lb) on a bench press is a 25% increase, far outside the 2 to 10% that the 2009 ACSM stand suggests, and it can turn ten good reps into none. McDonald points out that women meet this problem on many exercises, and that older machines with large stack increments cause it for everyone. A squat can usually take a 5 kg (10 lb) jump where a curl needs 1 to 2.5 kg (2.5 to 5 lb). His preference is always the smaller step, on the reasoning that a slow climb with intact technique ends up further ahead after three months than a fast one interrupted by injury.

- Work out the next jump as a percentage before you load it.
- Use the smallest plates, or add reps first, when the jump would exceed about 10%.

### After two weeks without progress, drop the load and build back up

**A stall means about two full weeks with no gain in weight or reps. The standard answer is a 10 to 20% step back and a steady rebuild.**

McDonald expects a well-fed beginner on a simple plan to progress for three or four months before the first real stall. The two-week definition rules out reacting to a single bad day. The usual fix is to cut the load by 10 to 20% and climb again. Someone stuck at 85 kg (185 lb) for sets of five would restart at roughly 65 to 75 kg (145 to 165 lb) and should be back at the old weight, and able to pass it, within three to four weeks. The lighter weeks also let technique settle. He suggests repeated resets can carry a beginner through a first year. This is coaching practice, untested in trials.

- Call a stall only after two weeks without a gain in weight or reps.
- Cut the load 10 to 20% and rebuild over three to four weeks.

### The usual next step is more sets, spread over upper and lower days

**When simple training stops working, most lifters need more weekly work, and splitting the body in two keeps sessions a sensible length.**

McDonald's test for moving on is a failed reset: you step the weights back, rebuild, and cannot pass your old best. For lifters who want more size or strength, the common change is higher volume. Full-body sessions then run too long, so he moves people to upper-body and lower-body days, each done twice a week. Sets for a muscle in one session rise from about 3 to about 6 and later 8, usually split between one compound and one single-joint exercise. The 2009 ACSM stand likewise lists 3 to 4 days a week for intermediates. Someone training for health can stay on the beginner plan and maintain.

- Move on only after a reset and rebuild fails to beat your previous best.
- Raise weekly sets in steps before changing anything else.

### Strength is muscle size multiplied by how well the nervous system uses it

**Bigger muscle raises the ceiling for strength. Skill, coordination and leverage decide how much of that ceiling you reach.**

Reviews attribute strength to morphological factors, chiefly muscle cross-sectional area, and to neural ones: motor unit recruitment, firing rate, coordination between muscles and reduced inhibition. Limb lengths and tendon attachment points also matter and cannot be trained. McDonald writes this as force equals muscular factors times neural factors, which explains a common puzzle: a smaller lifter with good leverage and years of practice in a lift can out-lift a bigger one. Heavy low-rep work is often called neural training because it mainly improves the second term, though enough sets of it still build muscle.

- To lift more, build muscle and also practise the specific lift under heavy loads.

### Maximal strength work means heavy loads, few reps and long rests

**The 2026 ACSM stand found strength was enhanced by loads of 80% of max or more. Convention puts dedicated strength work at 85% and up for 1 to 5 reps.**

The 2026 ACSM overview of 137 reviews found voluntary strength was enhanced by loads of at least 80% of one-rep max, 2 to 3 sets, a full range of motion, doing the lift early in the session, and two or more weekly sessions. McDonald describes the classic maximal strength zone as 85 to 100% for about 1 to 5 reps, and stresses that the boundary is soft: loads of 75 to 85% also build strength, especially when the bar is moved as fast as possible. Rests run long: a review of strength training for athletes found 2 to 5 minutes between sets may give the greatest strength and power benefit.

- Put the lift you most want to strengthen first in the session.
- Rest 2 to 5 minutes between heavy sets.

### Not everyone needs true maximal strength training

**Lifting sports and weight-class athletes need it most. For physique goals it is occasional, and for general health sets of 5 to 8 are heavy enough.**

McDonald sorts the need by goal. Powerlifters and weightlifters are judged on a one-rep max, and athletes in weight classes want strength without added mass. Other power athletes need it as a base. For physique training he sees it as an occasional tool, a few weeks every two or three months, because a higher max lets a lifter use more weight in growth rep ranges. For people training for health he would rarely go below about 5 reps unless a coach is present, since low-rep work is unforgiving of loose technique. Sets of 5 to 8 reach the 80% of max that the 2026 ACSM stand found enhanced strength.

- Decide from your goal whether sets of 1 to 3 belong in your plan at all.

### For most sports, strength is a base to build and then maintain

**Stronger athletes sprint, jump and change direction better, but most sport actions last 0.1 to 0.2 seconds, too short to use maximal strength.**

One review concluded that greater strength is strongly associated with better jumping, sprinting and change of direction, and may have no substitute. A companion review advises weaker athletes to build strength first, and stronger ones to shift toward power. McDonald's version is that once strength is adequate, pushing gym numbers higher does little for performance, because sport gives so little time to apply force. He cites coaching targets of about 1.5 times body weight in the bench press and 2 times in the squat as the point where emphasis moves. These are conventions, untested.

- If you are weak for your sport, get stronger first.
- Once strong, hold strength with less work and spend the time on power and the sport itself.

### Grinding lifts cost more recovery than lifts that are made or missed quickly

**A rep fought through for several seconds taxes muscle, joints and nervous system far more than a fast lift, which limits how often it can be done.**

McDonald contrasts the two barbell sports. An Olympic lift is limited by speed, so a missed attempt simply does not go, and the lifts are technical enough to need near-daily practice. Powerlifts can be ground out over several seconds, and lifters have to practise that, but he judges such reps far more costly. His reading of high-frequency strength systems is that most of their work sits at about 75 to 85% of max, little is above 90% and almost none goes to failure. Truly heavy days still number about two a week for a lift, which matches the two days found best for trained people in a meta-analysis of 140 studies.

- Count your truly heavy, slow sessions for a lift and keep them to about two a week.
- Add extra practice of a lift only as lighter, faster sets.

### Prilepin's chart describes elite weightlifters, so its upper volumes are too high for most

**The chart's logic is sound: keep reps per set well under the most you could do, and cut total reps as the load rises. Its numbers come from a narrow group.**

The chart's principle is that sets stop well short of the reps the load would allow, so bar speed and technique stay high, and that total reps in a session shrink as the weight climbs. McDonald lists its limits. It was observation, not an experiment. The lifters were elite young men training full time in a state system, very probably with anabolic drugs. It says nothing about how often they trained, and it was built for fast lifts. He advises ordinary lifters to use the lower to middle part of its ranges. The powerlifting coach Louie Simmons roughly halved its totals for the slower powerlifts.

- Use the chart's low to middle volumes if you follow it at all.
- Keep the principle: several crisp sets instead of one set to the limit.

### Ordinary lifts make poor power exercises because the bar has to be slowed

**A squat or bench press starts and ends at rest, so part of every fast rep is spent braking. Throws, jumps and Olympic lifts let you accelerate to the end.**

McDonald's explanation is mechanical. Training power means accelerating through the whole movement. In a normal lift the bar must stop at lockout, so the lifter eases off or the joints take the shock, and the lighter the load the longer the braking. Exercises in which the load or the body is released avoid this. A review of power training names ballistic, plyometric and weightlifting exercises as effective main exercises, and the 2026 ACSM stand found power was enhanced by Olympic-style lifting and fast lifting. His practical list is medicine-ball throws, kettlebell swings and jumps.

- For power, choose exercises you can finish at full speed: throws, jumps, swings.
- Avoid releasing a loaded bar without equipment built to catch it.

### Olympic lifts build power only once they can be done well with real loads

**The lifts are fast by nature and produce very high power, but they take a long time to learn. Short on time, use simpler explosive exercises.**

In a snatch or clean the bar is in effect thrown upward while the lifter drops under it, so nothing has to be braked. A meta-analysis found power cleans produced their highest power at 70% of max or more, well above the best load for a squat. McDonald's reservation is the learning time. With years to develop an athlete and a coach who can teach the lifts, he thinks they repay the effort. With four to eight weeks, or a large group, he would use jumps and throws that can be loaded after minutes of practice. Versions caught high, without the full squat, need less mobility but are still technical.

- Learn the lifts from a qualified coach or leave them out.
- Use jumps and throws for power while your lifting technique is still developing.

Strength and power training · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2014f](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training-13), [Soriano 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0341-8)

### Use a mark, a tape or a stopwatch to judge power work

**A target to reach on every rep shows when a set should end and whether you are improving, with no electronics.**

Devices that read bar speed exist, but McDonald describes cheaper checks that coaches have long used. For a vertical jump, find the day's best height and set a mark there; the set ends when you miss it. For medicine-ball throws, measure distance and stop when it falls short. For bounds, count the distance covered in a fixed number of jumps. For sprints, time each run and stop once you are clearly off your best. A measurable target also motivates, and it shows over weeks whether power is rising. Without any measure, he says the lifter can usually feel the rep where the spring has gone.

- Set a visible target for each power exercise.
- Stop the exercise for the day when you miss the target on a first rep.

### For fast lifting on ordinary exercises, take 20 to 30% off your working weight

**A lifter who normally works at 80 to 85% of max lands at about 50 to 65% this way, inside the range where squats and bench presses produce most power.**

McDonald's quick rule avoids testing a max. Someone who benches 100 kg (220 lb) for sets of 5 would use 70 to 80 kg (155 to 175 lb), for sets of 3 to 5 reps and about 15 to 25 reps in all, with rests long enough to keep every rep fast. He stays toward the heavier end of the power range on ordinary lifts, because the lighter the bar, the more of each rep is spent braking it. Meta-analyses place peak power for the squat and bench press between 30 and 70% of max. He holds the load steady for weeks and raises it only when a small test increase leaves bar speed unchanged.

- Take 20 to 30% off your usual working weight for fast sets.
- Do 3 to 5 reps a set and stop when the bar slows.

### Try to move a heavy bar fast even when it moves slowly

**In a 16-week trial, trying to move explosively produced the same high-speed adaptations whether or not the limb moved.**

Sixteen people trained one ankle with explosive efforts against a resistance that could not move, and the other with fast movements. After 16 weeks both sides showed the same gains at high speed, including a 26% rise in the rate of force development. The authors concluded that the repeated attempt to contract explosively was the main stimulus. McDonald applies this to heavy lifting: once technique is secure, drive every rep as fast as possible. He still expects heavy lifting combined with truly fast exercise such as jumps to beat either alone, because only real speed conditions the tissues for it.

- On heavy sets with solid technique, push each rep up as fast as you can.
- Lower the weight under control.

### Order a session from fast and skilled work to slow and fatiguing work

**Speed, skill and power suffer most from fatigue, so they come first. Heavy strength follows, then higher-rep work, then conditioning.**

McDonald's rule is that whatever needs the highest quality goes first. In practice that gives four stages: skill and sprint work, then jumps, throws and fast lifts, then heavy strength work, then higher-rep muscle work and conditioning. Fast work done first costs later strength work little; heavy lifting done first ruins speed. Position stands agree. The 2009 ACSM stand puts multi-joint before single-joint and higher-intensity before lower-intensity exercises, and the 2026 stand found strength was enhanced when a lift was done early in the session. Few sessions hold more than two or three of these stages.

- Put jumps, throws and Olympic lifts before heavy squats or presses.
- Leave high-rep sets and conditioning for the end or another session.

### Power work is progressed slowly, by output more than by load

**Throwing further and jumping higher at the same load is the progress. Added weight changes these exercises more than it seems.**

In strength and growth training, load or reps go up as often as possible. McDonald describes power training differently. A 1 kg (2 lb) increase on a 4 kg (9 lb) medicine ball is 25%, enough to change the exercise, so ball weight is rarely raised. Jumps are loaded, if at all, with a vest starting at 2 to 3% of body weight and seldom above 10%. Progress comes from higher output on the same drill, an occasional extra set, and a slow move from easier drills to harder ones over months. He regards this work as general preparation: the thing an athlete is trying to overload is the sport, and power drills support it.

- Record jump height or throw distance, and treat an increase at the same load as progress.

### After a true maximal personal best, end that work for the day

**A new best on an all-out lift or sprint is a stress the body has not met before. Stacking more on top can cost weeks.**

McDonald passes on a rule from an elite sprint-cycling coach and agrees with it. On truly maximal work, an athlete who misses the target on the first effort is too tired to improve that day and goes home. An athlete who sets a personal best also stops, because several bests in one session left riders flat for weeks afterwards. He links this to powerlifters who feel drained for a week after a meet of only nine heavy lifts, and limits the rule to maximal efforts. In submaximal training such as most growth work, a rep record takes far less out of you and needs no special handling.

- After a new one-rep max, do no further maximal attempts that day.
- If warm-ups show you are well off your usual numbers, cut the heavy work.

### High-rep, short-rest training builds fatigue tolerance more than size or strength

**Light loads, 15 to 20 reps and rests under a minute suit muscular endurance and work capacity. McDonald rates them a poor main method for growth.**

The 2009 ACSM stand describes local muscular endurance training as light to moderate loads of 40 to 60% of one-rep max for more than 15 reps, with rests under 90 seconds. McDonald's metabolic or depletion work is close: about 60% or less, 15 to 20 reps, 30 to 60 seconds of rest and three or four sets. He lists its uses as muscular endurance, a couple of weeks of work-capacity building, a rest for the joints from heavy loading and glycogen depletion in certain diets. Endurance rests on strength, so he would not use it alone. For growth he limits it to a few finishing sets after heavier work.

- Use short blocks of high-rep work to build tolerance for training, then return to heavier loads.

### Every training style builds both contractile protein and muscle volume

**Some evidence shows the fluid part of a fibre can expand faster than its contractile protein, but no method produces one kind of growth alone.**

A 2020 review found select evidence that sarcoplasmic hypertrophy coincides with fibre growth during resistance training, alongside evidence for growth in which contractile protein keeps pace or even leads. The idea is used to explain why some large lifters are not proportionally strong. McDonald thinks the distinction has some merit, and his impression is that lifters who train only for the pump lose size faster when they stop. He is clear that the difference is one of emphasis: bodybuilders who train with high reps build real contractile tissue, and heavy lifters gain muscle volume too.

- Choose rep ranges for your goal and your joints, not to target one kind of growth.

### Mechanical tension is the main growth signal; damage and burn are secondary

**Reviews rank mechanical signals as the prime trigger for hypertrophy. Muscle damage is probably not needed, and metabolic stress looks minor.**

A 2019 review of hypertrophy stimuli names mechanical signals as the prime candidates and says exercise-induced damage is probably not essential. A separate review concluded that damage neither mediates nor potentiates growth. McDonald puts tension first and now largely discounts damage. He reads the growth seen with very light loads taken to failure as tension reached the slow way. His practical test is strength in moderate rep ranges over the years. A lifter benching the same weight for the same reps a year later has probably not grown, and one lifting clearly more for those reps probably has.

- Track a few lifts in a moderate rep range and aim to raise them over months.
- Do not use soreness or the pump as the measure of a productive session.

### The hormone rise after a session does not decide how much you grow

**In 49 trained men, the post-exercise rise in hormones was unrelated to gains in muscle or strength over 12 weeks.**

Short rests and big lifts are often promoted for the hormone rise they cause. Forty-nine resistance-trained men did 12 weeks of whole-body training with either light loads for 20 to 25 reps or heavier loads for 8 to 12, all sets to failure. Muscle fibre size and lean mass rose equally, and no significant correlation appeared between the acute rise in any measured hormone and the change in strength or size. McDonald's reading of the wider literature is the same: these brief shifts are at most a tiny part of the growth stimulus.

- Choose rest times that let you repeat your reps, not ones meant to raise hormones.

### Muscles already worked by compound lifts need about half the direct work

**After pressing or rowing, the arms and rear shoulders have done part of their work. McDonald halves their direct volume.**

A bench press trains the chest, the front of the shoulders and the triceps, and a row trains the back and the biceps. McDonald's rule of thumb, which he presents as experience and says he has not seen tested, is that a smaller muscle trained after the compound work for a larger one needs about half the usual direct volume: roughly 20 to 30 reps, or two to four sets. He applies it to triceps after chest, biceps after back and rear shoulders after rows. In his view the front of the shoulder rarely needs direct work in a plan with pressing, while the side of the shoulder gains from a raise as well as a press.

- Count pressing and rowing sets as partial work for the arms and shoulders.
- Give the arms about half the direct sets you give the chest or back that day.

### Match the exercise to the rep range

**Heavy sets of 5 to 8 suit compound lifts. Sets of 12 to 20 suit exercises with no weak link and little danger if form slips.**

McDonald's guide is practical. In low rep ranges, big compound lifts such as squats, presses, rows and leg presses handle the load well, while single-joint exercises done that heavy put a lot of stress on one joint. In high rep ranges the problem reverses. Long sets of a compound lift tend to end when a smaller muscle tires, such as the lower back in a squat or the arms in a press or row, and form can slip while the bar is still loaded. For those sets he prefers machines and single-joint exercises that keep the work on the target muscle. Between about 6 and 12 reps either kind works, and build and skill decide.

- Do your heaviest, lowest-rep work on compound lifts.
- Move high-rep sets to machines or single-joint exercises if a small muscle gives out first.

### Growth programmes that last share the same few ingredients

**Plans that look very different on paper all combine progressive loading, some fatigue work, moderate volume and regular easier weeks.**

McDonald compares several well-known hypertrophy systems, among them Bryan Haycock's HST, Dante Trudell's DC training, Borge Fagerli's Myo-reps and his own generic bulking routine. He finds the same core in each: weight on the bar is pushed up over time, there is some higher-rep or short-rest work, volume for a muscle is moderate, each muscle is trained more than once a week, and hard weeks alternate with easier ones. His conclusion is that a plan lacking those ingredients is the one to doubt. The 2026 ACSM overview likewise found few prescription details changed outcomes.

- Check a new programme for progression, sensible volume and planned easier weeks before its details.

### Use an occasional all-out last set to check your working weight

**Every three or four weeks, push the last set as far as it will go. Far more reps than the target means the weight is too light.**

People tend to underestimate how many reps they have left. A review of 12 studies found an average underprediction of about one rep, with wide variation, and McDonald reports newer lifters stopping at 10 when 5 or 6 more were possible. His check is simple. If the plan is four sets of 8, do three as written and take the fourth as far as good form allows. Getting 9 or 10 means the load is right. Getting 12 or more means it should go up. He uses the test every three or four weeks, not on every exercise, because sets to failure cost recovery and, for some lifters, spoil the sets that follow.

- Every few weeks, take the last set of a main exercise to failure with good form.
- Raise the weight if you beat the target by four or more reps.

### Describe a set with two numbers: how heavy and how hard

**The same weight can be an easy set or an all-out one. Load says how heavy the bar is; effort says how near failure the set went.**

McDonald's point is that most arguments about intensity come from people using one word for two things. Two reps at 85% of max and five reps at 85% share a load intensity but are very different efforts. Twelve reps to failure at about 75% is a moderate load and a maximal effort. Fast sets at 30 to 60% are light, yet each rep is pushed as hard as possible. He suggests recording both, an approach he credits to the powerlifting coach Mike Tuchscherer, and adds looser senses of the word: low reps are demanding on the nervous system, moderate reps on the muscle, and very high reps on energy supply.

- Log the weight and reps, then add how many reps you had left.

### Outside the lifting sports, set loads by a rep-max zone, not a percentage

**A percentage needs a tested max and assumes everyone gets the same reps at it. A rep zone with a few reps in reserve needs neither.**

McDonald lists the problems of percent-based loading for non-specialists: testing a true max takes skill and carries risk, the max shifts from week to week, and the reps possible at a given percentage differ between people. A meta-regression of 269 studies supports the last point, with a spread of about 2.5 reps around the average at 80% of max. His method is to choose a zone such as 10 to 15RM. Fewer than 10 reps means the weight is too heavy, and more than 15 means it is too light. Sets stop a few reps short of failure, and the weight goes up once the last set leaves two or three in hand.

- Pick a rep zone for each exercise and find the weight that fits it.
- Raise the weight when your last set ends with two or three reps to spare.

### All-out fast work needs a longer warm-up than its light loads suggest

**A sprint, jump or throw is a maximal effort even with no load, so the warm-up should build to full speed in steps.**

The usual rule is that harder work needs more warm-up: an easy jog needs none, and heavy singles need many build-up sets. McDonald points out that power and speed work break the pattern people expect. The load is light but nothing is held back, and going straight to full speed is how shoulders and hamstrings get hurt. Sprinters commonly spend 30 to 45 minutes on easy running, mobility work, drills and build-up runs before the first full sprint. In the gym, he suggests a few progressively faster or heavier sets of the movement itself, and notes that power work done after sprint or skill practice needs less.

- Before maximal jumps, throws or sprints, do several build-up efforts at rising speed.
- Never make the first rep of the day an all-out one.

### End the general warm-up at a light sweat

**Five to ten minutes of easy activity is typical. The endpoint is feeling warm, and many lifters can use light first sets instead.**

McDonald prefers a sign to a stopwatch: continue until sweating lightly, which takes longer in a cold room and for older or very fit people. Any easy whole-body activity works. He treats it as optional for young, uninjured lifters, who can start with light sets of the first exercise. Evidence gives it some support. Thirteen trained people leg-pressed 8.4% more on a one-rep-max test after 20 minutes of easy cycling plus warm-up sets than after the warm-up sets alone, and a meta-analysis found warm-ups improved performance in 79% of the measures examined.

- Do a few minutes of easy movement until you feel warm, then start your ramp-up sets.

**Reps in a warm-up set often feel stiff, then suddenly smooth. That change is a better stopping point than a fixed count.**

McDonald passes on a cue from his own mentor. During a warm-up set the first reps tend to feel heavy and awkward, and at some point, perhaps the fourth rep or the eighth, one moves freely. ### Stretch before lifting where tightness blocks a safe position

**Static holds up to 30 to 45 seconds did not reduce maximal muscle performance in a review of 106 studies. Longer holds did, when tested straight afterwards.**

A systematic review found no harm from static stretches under 30 seconds, none of significance at 30 to 45 seconds, and likely reductions at 60 seconds or more. McDonald adds that such studies test performance right after stretching, while a lifter goes on to ramp-up sets. His rule is practical. If tight hips make the lower back round in a squat, or tight shoulders spoil the bench press set-up, stretch them first, since position matters more than a percent or two of force. Otherwise he uses dynamic movement or progressively deeper light sets before training, and keeps long static holds for afterwards.

- Before lifting, stretch only the areas that stop you reaching a good position.
- Keep any pre-lift static hold under about 30 seconds.

### Foam rolling before lifting is optional and does not replace warming up

**Rolling before exercise gave a small gain in flexibility and a negligible change in strength in a meta-analysis of 21 studies.**

A meta-analysis of 21 studies, 14 of them on rolling before exercise, found it improved flexibility by about 4% and sprint performance by 0.7%, with negligible effects on jumping and strength. ### A single at the working weight is an optional primer for experienced lifters

**Some lifters find the first work set easier after one rep at that weight. Trial evidence for a benefit is thin.**

McDonald offers this only to lifters with a settled basic warm-up. If your second work set usually feels better than your first, one rep at the working weight before the first set may help. With higher-rep work he sometimes uses a single slightly above the working weight, or a 6 to 10 second hold of a load about 10% heavier, to make the work sets feel light. ### Five rising sets of five suit learning a lift, if someone checks the technique

**Each session gives 25 practice reps that end in one challenging set. Without a coach, lifters tend to add weight faster than their form allows.**

In the beginner version the five sets climb to a single hard set, and the starting weights move up a little each session. ### The 300, 400, 500 rule is a long-term target for an average-sized man

**A strict bench press of 300 lb (136 kg), squat of 400 lb (181 kg) and deadlift of 500 lb (227 kg) is an old estimate of where a drug-free lifter nears his muscular limit.**

The rule was popularised by Stuart McRobert, and Lyle McDonald sets out the conditions people leave off. It assumes a man of about 86 to 91 kg (190 to 200 lb) and average height, so smaller lifters should expect less and bigger ones more. It assumes strict lifts: a controlled bench press with no bounce or help, a squat below parallel, a deadlift without hitching. It assumes equal work on all three, and ideally the weights are handled for several reps. McDonald says it does not translate to women. It is a rule of thumb from coaching, not a population norm, and many lifters never reach it.

- Use the figures as distant goals, scaled to your body weight.

### In unequipped lifting the deadlift is biggest, then the squat, then the bench press

### The stronger you are, the slower strength comes

**Across 15 years of competition results, the strongest quarter of men gained strength at about half the daily rate of the least strong quarter.**

### Goals about what you do beat goals about what you get

**In a meta-analysis of goal setting in sport, process goals improved performance far more than performance or outcome goals.**

Twenty-seven studies were pooled. Process goals had the largest effect on performance (d = 1.36), against 0.44 for performance goals and 0.09 for outcome goals. McDonald describes the same split in athletes. Those who live by results tend to be driven in the short term and burn out, because nobody wins or sets a record every time and each bad day feeds tension into the next. Those who judge a session by how well they carried it out still care about results, but they can take something from a bad day: the weak point it exposed. He prefers to coach the second kind.

- Give each session one thing to do well, such as every squat to depth.
- Review a bad session for the weak point it showed.

Goals and self-coaching · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Williamson 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984x.2022.2116723), [McDonald 2008n](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/goal-vs-process-oriented-training)

### Two questions can stand in for a coach

**Ask what you would tell another lifter in your position. And if you are wondering whether to do one more set, take that as a no.**

McDonald argues that self-coached lifters lack distance, so they talk themselves into what they want to do. His first check is to picture someone else in the same situation and follow the advice he would give them, unless there is a concrete reason his case differs. The second comes from his skating coach: doubt about another set or interval is itself the answer. By then the training effect is banked, and the extra one is where injuries happen. The second rule suits driven trainees, not people who look for reasons to stop. He once ignored it on a deadlift and lost nine months to a back strain.

- Before changing the plan mid-session, ask what you would tell a friend to do.
- When in doubt about one more set, stop.

Goals and self-coaching · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2019f](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/coaching-yourself)

### A coach adds technique, restraint and a plan

**Supervised lifters gained more strength than unsupervised ones in pooled trials. The benefit is partly being pushed and partly being held back.**

McDonald lists three things a coach supplies. The first is technical feedback, which he thinks most gym-goers badly need. The second is effort in the right amount: reviews show people left alone choose loads averaging 53% of max, while driven athletes need the opposite, so he asks them whether they should do another set, never whether they want to. The third is control of the process, meaning how much, how hard and when to rest. Pooled trials back the general point, with a moderate strength advantage for supervised training across 12 studies. A coach must be able to teach good technique, which is not a given.

- If you cannot hire a coach, film your lifts and have an experienced lifter review them.

Goals and self-coaching · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Fisher 2022](https://doi.org/10.47206/ijsc.v2i1.101), [Steele 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01717-9), [McDonald 2019f](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/coaching-yourself)

### A simple plan carried out fully beats an elaborate one

**Coaches tend to overcomplicate in mid-career and simplify with experience. Evidence agrees that most programme details change little.**

McDonald describes three stages in a coach's development: knowing nothing, believing you know a great deal, and learning how much you do not know. The middle stage produces intricate periodisation charts that he found no more productive than hard work on basics. The 2026 ACSM overview of 137 reviews gives the same picture from data: few prescription variables affected the main adaptations, and set structure, time under tension, periodisation and training to failure did not consistently change outcomes. What remains is load heavy enough for the goal, enough weekly sets, progression and showing up.

- Choose the simplest plan that covers load, weekly sets and progression.
- Change one thing at a time, and only when progress has stopped.

Goals and self-coaching · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [McDonald 2016e](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/all-you-need-to-know-about-training), [Currier 2026](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000003897)

### Sports select body types more than they build them

**There is no single athletic physique. Elite athletes look alike within a sport because those with the right build rise to the top.**

The slogan that athletic training gives an athletic look assumes the training creates the body. A review of athlete size over the twentieth century describes elite sport as selection: in many sports athletes grew taller and heavier faster than the population, while sports needing a narrow build kept the same sizes. McDonald's point: a sumo wrestler, a gymnast and a distance runner are all athletes, and copying their training will not give you their frames. When weightlifters need more muscle they add bodybuilding-style work after their lifting. For looks, train for muscle and manage body fat directly.

- If appearance is the goal, use growth training and diet, not a sport's programme.

Goals and self-coaching · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Norton 2001](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200131110-00001), [McDonald 2016m](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/train-to-look-like-an-athlete)

### Lifting the weight fast adds a little to physical function in older adults

**In adults over 60, training with a fast lifting phase improved physical function modestly more than conventional strength training.**

A meta-analysis of 20 trials in 566 community-living adults aged 60 and over compared lifting as fast as possible with ordinary strength training. Power training was associated with a modest extra improvement in physical function (standardised difference 0.30), on low-certainty evidence. The 2026 ACSM stand also found power training enhanced physical function. McDonald notes that lost muscle power is part of declining function with age. His caution is about method: jumps and Olympic lifts carry too much risk for an unconditioned older person, so fast work belongs on simple, stable exercises.

- Older lifters can push the lifting phase fast on machines or other stable exercises.
- Get a clinician's clearance first if you have joint, heart or balance problems.

Health and general fitness · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Balachandran 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.11623), [Currier 2026](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000003897), [McDonald 2014c](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training-10)

### For general health, two or three short full-body sessions cover the lifting

**Guidelines ask for resistance exercise for each major muscle group on two or three days a week. One to three sets of one or two exercises per muscle is enough.**

The ACSM's 2011 position stand recommends resistance exercise for each major muscle group on 2 to 3 days a week, and the World Health Organization recommends regular muscle-strengthening activity at every age. McDonald's reading is that health needs far less than size or performance: one to three sets, one or two exercises for each muscle, mostly moderate reps, and more sets when reps are lower. Any exercises that can be done safely will do, and getting the sessions done matters more than their details. Thirty minutes of lifting and thirty of cardio, three times a week, go a long way.

- Lift two or three days a week, covering every major muscle group.
- Keep sessions short enough that you never skip them.

Health and general fitness · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Garber 2011](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e318213fefb), [Bull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955), [McDonald 2019h](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/exercise-for-health-and-fitness)

### Occasional heavier sets and loaded standing lifts are worth including for bone

**Bone responds to heavy loading through the skeleton. A health-focused lifter gains from some sets of 5 or 6 without living there.**

In 101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass, eight months of supervised heavy lifting and impact work, built on five sets of five at over 85% of max, raised lumbar spine bone density by 2.9% while it fell 1.2% in a low-intensity home programme. McDonald advises people training for health to visit lower rep ranges from time to time for this reason, and to include lifts that load the spine and legs, such as squats or overhead presses. His caution is that constant heavy work wears on joints in older lifters, and that these lifts need competent teaching. Anyone with diagnosed low bone mass should be supervised.

- Include a few weeks of sets of 5 or 6 on a well-learned lift in each training block.
- With low bone density, start heavy loading only under professional supervision.

Health and general fitness · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Watson 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.3284), [McDonald 2019h](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/exercise-for-health-and-fitness)

### After the first year, vary reps in simple ways

**Three easy options: step the reps down over weeks, progress inside one wide rep range, or use two rep ranges in the same week.**

McDonald suggests more structure only after 6 to 12 months of steady basic training. One option is to start at sets of 12 and move to 10, 8 and then 6 every two or three weeks, adding weight at each step, then take an easy week or two and begin again. A second is double progression in a range of 6 to 12. A third is one session in a higher range such as 10 to 12 and another in a lower one such as 6 to 8. He does not rank them: the best choice is the one a person will keep doing. A meta-analysis of 18 studies found periodised training produced larger one-rep-max gains than unvaried training.

- Pick one scheme and run it for several months.
- Take an easy week when a cycle of heavier weeks ends.

Health and general fitness · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2019h](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/exercise-for-health-and-fitness), [Williams 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0734-y)

## Common mistakes

### Counting years in the gym as proof you are past the beginner stage

**Time served does not make a lifter intermediate. Stable technique, the ability to work hard and measurable gains do.**

McDonald's observation from two decades of coaching is that almost everyone rates themselves as more advanced than they are. A person can lift for two years, or twenty, and still have unstable technique, little capacity for hard effort, no added muscle and a disorganised routine. In his terms that person remains a beginner and progresses fastest on beginner training for a while. He adds that experienced lifters, like athletes in other sports, go back to basic work for short spells on purpose. A return to a simple plan is a normal part of training.

- Judge your level by technique and progress, then choose the routine that fits it.

Starting out · Beginner · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2015e](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/beginning-weight-training)

### Starting on a one-muscle-a-day bodybuilding split

**A split trains each exercise about once a week, which starves a beginner of practice and invites far more sets than needed.**

When weekly sets are matched, split and full-body routines produce similar strength and size in trials, so a split is not harmful in itself. McDonald's objection is specific to beginners. Each exercise comes round about once in seven days, so the movements are learned slowly. And because each session covers only one or two muscles, new lifters fill the time with exercises and sets they do not need. The only split he accepts for a beginner is upper body and lower body on separate days, used because it allows training two days in a row, and only with the sets for each muscle held down.

- Use full-body sessions for the first months.
- If your week forces a split, use upper and lower days and keep sets per muscle low.

Building a first programme · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Ramos-Campo 2024](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000004774), [McDonald 2015g](https://bodyrecomposition.com/muscle-gain/beginning-weight-training-3)

### Setting first weights from a body-weight formula

**Rules that tell a beginner to squat or press a fixed share of body weight fit some people and badly overload others.**

Some programmes give starting loads as a fraction of body weight. McDonald rejects the idea for beginners and for everyone else. Two people of the same weight can differ in age, sex, build, training history and how much of that weight is muscle, so one ratio cannot suit both. A heavy, untrained 55-year-old and a light 20-year-old athlete would each get the wrong answer, in opposite directions. Even where a ratio matches the average new lifter, an individual has no way of knowing whether they are that average. Trial from the light end takes a session or two longer and carries none of that risk.

- Ignore starting-weight ratios and work up from the lightest load.

Building a first programme · Beginner · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2015a](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/beginning-weight-training-5)

### Adding weight every session whether or not the last one was clean

**Plans that ask for more weight each time work when a coach makes the call. Alone, lifters keep adding after technique has begun to slip.**

Low-rep beginner plans usually ramp to one top set and ask for a small increase next time. McDonald accepts that beginners can ride this for a long while. His concern is the uncoached lifter, often a young man, who treats the increase as automatic. His own mentor's rule was stricter: add nothing until every set is completed under control, even if that means the same weights for the first month, and after that expect to add about once a week. McDonald sits between the two. He adds small amounts until the lifter can feel the lift, holds there until technique is stable, then progresses gradually.

- Add weight only when every set at the current load was done under control.
- Film a set or ask an experienced lifter to watch before raising the load.

Building a first programme · Beginner · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2015b](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/beginning-weight-training-6)

### Programming five heavy sets of five on every exercise

**Heavy low-rep sets need rests of several minutes, so more than one or two such lifts makes a session unworkably long.**

Five sets of five with a three-minute rest take at least 15 minutes for one exercise before warm-up sets are counted. Two or three lifts done that way fill 45 to 60 minutes. McDonald's rule for strength phases is to train one or two main lifts heavy in a session and everything after them with lighter loads and higher reps. He also keeps very low reps for multi-joint lifts, because singles to triples on a single-joint exercise load one joint heavily for little return. A review of rest intervals found that 3 to 5 minutes between sets allowed more reps across sets at loads of 50 to 90% of max.

- Pick one or two lifts to train heavy in a session.
- Do the rest of the session at 8 or more reps with shorter rests.

Strength and power training · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2014o](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training-9), [de Salles 2009](https://doi.org/10.2165/11315230-000000000-00000)

### Carrying on with jumps, throws or fast lifts after the snap has gone

**Power work only trains power while each rep is near your best. Slower reps add fatigue and nothing else.**

In growth and endurance training, fatigue is part of the stimulus. McDonald stresses that in power training it is not. Once bar speed, jump height or throw distance drops, the set has stopped doing its job, and when a new set opens with a flat rep the drill is over for the day. That keeps sets short and volume low, and it is why doing cleans or box jumps for high reps against the clock is conditioning, not power training. The 2026 ACSM stand found power was enhanced by low to moderate volumes with a fast lifting phase.

- End a power set at the first clearly slower rep.
- Keep jump, throw and fast-lift work early in the session, before fatiguing work.

Strength and power training · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Currier 2026](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000003897), [McDonald 2014g](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training-14)

### Holding off all jumping until you can squat twice your body weight

**The old rule was written for depth jumps, the hardest plyometric drill. Easy hops, jumps and throws need no strength threshold.**

McDonald traces the rule to a time when plyometrics meant depth jumps, in which the athlete drops from a box and rebounds. Applied to all jumping, it would keep many athletes from any explosive work for two or three years. His approach is to bring in low-level work early and in small amounts: ankle hops, standing jumps, medicine-ball throws and kettlebell swings, often as part of the warm-up before lifting. That teaches the movements and lets tissue adapt while strength is built. Intense drills come later. The cautions are to teach landing, watch for shin and ankle trouble, and do less when unsure.

- Add a few easy jumps or throws to your warm-up from early in training.
- Save depth jumps and loaded jumps until you are strong and well practised.

Strength and power training · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2014h](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training-15)

### Switching to light weights and high reps to get lean

**High-rep circuits burn energy but hold muscle and strength poorly, and holding muscle is the main job of lifting during a diet.**

The habit of swapping heavy training for light, fast, high-rep work before a contest comes from bodybuilding. McDonald's argument is that it took hold in an era when anabolic steroids protected muscle whatever the training looked like, and that drug-free lifters who copy it give up the stimulus that tells the body to keep muscle. He grants that it uses more energy than heavy sets and empties muscle glycogen. His position is that it can be added in small amounts during a diet but must not replace heavier work, with complete beginners as the one exception. The diet area covers how heavy training protects lean mass.

- Keep your usual heavy sets when you diet, with fewer sets if recovery drops.
- Treat high-rep circuits as an optional extra, not the base of the plan.

Growth and endurance training · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2014b](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training)

### Building high-rep circuits around technical barbell lifts

**Under heavy fatigue, squat technique changes within the session. Simple exercises are the safer choice for high-rep, short-rest work.**

Twenty-five people with at least six months of barbell experience did a highly fatiguing workout that included 55 squat repetitions. Knee and hip angles shifted, with less knee bend and more forward lean, and the authors concluded that such changes may raise injury risk. McDonald makes the same point from coaching: squats or deadlifts for sets of 15 to 20 on short rests invite a loss of form even when the load is light, and high-rep Olympic lifts are worse for anyone without years of practice. He builds these sessions from machines and other low-skill exercises.

- Use machines, simple dumbbell moves or easy bodyweight exercises for circuits.
- Keep squats, deadlifts and Olympic lifts for sets where you are fresh.

Growth and endurance training · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Hooper 2014](https://doi.org/10.1097/jsc.0000000000000237), [McDonald 2014b](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training)

### Doing three or four near-identical exercises for one muscle

**Barbell, dumbbell and machine versions of the same press train a muscle the same way. The extra exercises only add sets.**

McDonald traces the habit to high-volume bodybuilding of the steroid era, and thinks it leads drug-free lifters to do too many sets. His guideline is one or two exercises for a muscle in a session. The back is the exception at three or four, because it is several muscles with different jobs. Where a second exercise is used he wants it to differ in kind: a heavy compound lift, then one that loads the muscle at long length or one that is hardest in the shortened position. He keeps the same exercises through a block of six to eight weeks so progress can be tracked, and swaps them between blocks.

- Limit most muscles to one or two exercises in a session.
- Make a second exercise different in kind, not the same movement on other equipment.

Growth and endurance training · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2014l](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training-6)

### Treating 8 to 12 reps as the only range that builds muscle

**Growth has been shown across a wide span of loads. The 8 to 12 range is a convenient middle, not a boundary.**

A network meta-analysis of 28 studies in 747 adults found muscle growth was independent of load when sets were taken to failure, while strength gains were larger with heavier loads. McDonald wrote years earlier that he could find no original research behind 8 to 12 as the growth range, and placed growth training anywhere from about 5 to 20 reps. He notes that Olympic weightlifters add size with sets of five. His practical limits are at the edges: very low reps need many sets and are hard on joints, and very high reps are painful and inefficient.

- Use any rep range from about 5 to 20 that lets you train the muscle hard.
- Choose the range by the exercise and by what your joints tolerate.

Growth and endurance training · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Lopez 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000002585), [McDonald 2014j](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training-3)

### Taking warm-up sets close to failure

**High-rep sets on the way up tire you before the sets that count. Keep warm-up sets short and cut the reps as the weight rises.**

The old ascending pyramid starts with 12 to 15 hard reps and adds weight each set, so the lifter reaches the heaviest set already tired. McDonald prefers a ramp of short sets: roughly four steps from about half the working weight to just under it, with reps falling from five or more to singles, 30 to 60 seconds between steps and about two minutes before the first work set. Exact percentages do not matter; sensible plate jumps do. A squat trial fits this: adding heavier or faster warm-up sets did not increase the reps lifters could do afterwards compared with their usual warm-up.

- Do fewer reps on each warm-up set as the bar gets heavier.
- Finish every warm-up set well short of fatigue.

Warming up · Beginner · Thin evidence · Sources: [McDonald 2009ah](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/warming-up-for-the-weight-room), [Souza 2024](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.17347)

### Applying the 20-rep grind to deadlifts or good mornings

**Technique in these lifts breaks down quickly as reps climb, and the part that fails is the back.**

Versions of the breathing-squat idea have been published for the deadlift and the good morning. McDonald advises against both. A squat that goes wrong late in a long set can usually be set down on the safety bars. A deadlift or good morning taken to that level of fatigue tends to finish with a rounded back under load, and he notes that one magazine author who recommended the 20-rep deadlift was injured for months by it. He tried it once and did not repeat it. Rest-pause methods on machines or upper-body lifts are a different matter, since a failed rep there carries little risk.

- Keep very long, all-out sets to exercises where a failed rep is safe.

Classic programmes and methods · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2016d](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/all-about-the-20-rep-squat)

### Comparing your lifts with numbers that carry no conditions

**A lift only compares with a standard done the same way: same depth, no bounce or help, same body weight and sex, and no drugs or supportive suits.**

McDonald's test is to ask how many strict lifts at the 300, 400 and 500 lb (136, 181 and 227 kg) level you have seen from a lifter of about 90 kg (200 lb) in an ordinary gym. His guess is between none and two, because most big numbers quoted in gyms come from half squats, bounced or assisted bench presses, or much heavier people. Records mislead in the other direction: they describe the rare outlier, as the top tenth of drug-tested competitors shows with squats near 2.8 times body weight for men. A fair comparison needs the lift's conditions and the lifter's body weight attached.

- Record your own lifts to a fixed standard of depth and control.
- Compare yourself with lifters of your sex, size and training history.

Standards and time frames · Beginner · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2011a](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/300-bench-400-squat-500-deadlift), [van den Hoek 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2024.07.005)

### Judging every session by whether you set a personal best

**Records cannot come every time. Treating each ordinary session as a failure builds tension that makes the next one worse.**

McDonald's picture of the outcome-driven lifter is someone who must lift more than last time whenever they train, and who reads one flat day as proof of weakness. The usual response is to try harder, which tightens technique, spoils the following session and starts a run of bad days. He adds, as his own impression from coaching, that men fall into this more often than women and are less willing to take weight off the bar to fix a lift. His remedy is to change what counts as success: aim to do the movement right and let the load follow. After a poor session he often backs off a little or shifts the focus.

- Count a session as successful if the planned work was done with good form.
- Respond to a bad day by easing off, not by forcing a heavier one.

Goals and self-coaching · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2008n](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/goal-vs-process-oriented-training)

### Adding sets, sessions and exercises to your own plan as you go

**Self-coached lifters rarely fail through laziness. They do too much, too often, and explain to themselves why they are the exception.**

McDonald reports that experienced coaches say the same of themselves: they would never let an athlete do what they do in their own training, adding sets and exercises until the volume has doubled. His skating coach's blunt fix was to write the programme and then cut it in half. Outside accountability helps too: he had friends ask whether he was sticking to his plan. Supervision has a measurable effect. A meta-analysis of 12 studies found supervised training produced moderately larger strength gains than unsupervised training (standardised effect 0.40) and little difference in body composition.

- Write the plan before the session and do not add to it on the day.
- Ask someone to check now and then that you are following it.

Goals and self-coaching · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [McDonald 2019f](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/coaching-yourself), [Fisher 2022](https://doi.org/10.47206/ijsc.v2i1.101)

### Picking one habit of champions as the reason for their success

**The lifters who got nowhere usually had the same habit. What top performers share is a decade or more of consistent work, plus talent.**

McDonald's objection to the phrase about success leaving clues is that people choose the clue that suits what they are selling. Champion bodybuilders take supplements and train each muscle once a week; so do the far larger number who never succeed, and the champions mostly trained differently on the way up. A habit only counts as evidence if it separates those who succeeded from those who did not. His own list of what the best have in common is short: ten or more years of hard, sensible, consistent training toward one goal, and the talent that elite sport selects for. The rest he calls details.

- Ask whether unsuccessful lifters do the same thing before you copy it.
- Put consistency over years ahead of any single method.

Goals and self-coaching · Beginner · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2011b](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/success-leaves-clues), [Norton 2001](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200131110-00001)

## Signs you have outgrown a beginner programme

Drawn from Lyle McDonald's criteria. Most of each group should be true before you change to an intermediate plan.

**Technique**

- Each main exercise looks the same from the first rep to the last
- You can feel the muscles an exercise is meant to train
- Weight has gone up for months without the movement changing

**Capacity**

- A full session of 60 to 90 minutes no longer leaves you drained
- Joints and tendons are free of lingering soreness at current loads
- You have trained two or three times a week for six months or more with few missed sessions

**Progress**

- You are visibly more muscular or measurably stronger than when you started
- Weights and reps have not moved for two full weeks
- A 10 to 20% step back and rebuild did not take you past your old best

Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2015e](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/beginning-weight-training), [McDonald 2015b](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/beginning-weight-training-6)

## How a beginner plan shifts with the long-term goal

The base is shared. These are the adjustments Lyle McDonald describes for each aim, arranged here as a comparison.

| Long-term goal | Sessions a week | Exercise choice | Sets and reps |
|---|---|---|---|
| General health and fitness | 2, and it can stay at 2 | Machines or any simple mix that can be learned in a session or two | 1 set of 8 to 12 can be enough if it is worked hard |
| Support for another sport | 2 short sessions around sport practice | General whole-body lifts; bring in early any lift needed later | 1 to 3 sets of moderate reps |
| Physique | 3 | Free weights, machines and single-joint work; test which exercises suit your build | Build to 2 or 3 sets of 8 to 12 |
| Powerlifting or strongman | 3 | Squat, bench press and deadlift from the start, plus general work for the whole body | Sets of 5 on the main lifts for clean practice; 8 to 12 elsewhere |

## Free weights and machines: the practical differences

Results are similar, so the choice turns on practical points. These are the ones McDonald weighs for a new lifter.

| Point | Free weights | Machines |
|---|---|---|
| Time to learn | A barbell squat, bench press or deadlift can take weeks or months to learn well | Most can be learned in one or two sessions |
| Path of the movement | You set the path, so it can fit your build | Fixed by the machine; a poor fit can strain a joint |
| Balance and control | Trained by the lift; a barrier for people with balance problems | Largely removed, so effort goes to the target muscle |
| Tracking progress across gyms | A 20 kg (45 lb) plate weighs the same everywhere | Stacks and leverage differ by brand, so numbers do not transfer |
| Equipment needed | A bar, plates, bench and rack cover dozens of exercises | Usually one machine per movement; no problem in a commercial gym |
| Set-up time | Plates to load and more warm-up sets | Set the seat and move the pin |

Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2015g](https://bodyrecomposition.com/muscle-gain/beginning-weight-training-3)

## Three ways to count training volume

Each count answers a different question and each can mislead. The comparison follows Lyle McDonald's discussion of volume.

| Method | How it is counted | Useful for | Where it misleads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard sets | Sets taken close to failure, for each muscle each week | Planning growth training; the unit in recent dose-response analyses | Treats a set of 3 and a set of 12 as equal |
| Total reps | Reps for a muscle in a session, at about 60 to 85% of max | Comparing schemes that use different reps per set | Ignores how hard each set was |
| Volume load (tonnage) | Sets x reps x weight | Tracking workload on the same lift over time | Inflated by short-range heavy lifts and by large amounts of light work |

Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2014a](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/categories-of-weight-training-4), [Pelland 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)

## How many warm-up sets, by situation

Lyle McDonald's guidance gathered into one comparison. Heavier loads, more technical lifts and stronger lifters need more sets.

| Situation | Warm-up sets | How to do them |
|---|---|---|
| Beginner in the first weeks | None | The work sets are light enough to serve as the warm-up |
| Beginner once the weights feel heavy | 1 | About three quarters of the day's weight for 8 reps, then rest 30 to 60 seconds |
| Sets of about 12 at moderate loads | 1 to 2 | Light sets of a few reps |
| Heavy sets of 3 at 85 to 90% of max | 4 to 7 | Reps fall as the weight rises, ending in singles |
| Very strong lifter | Up to 8 or so | Bigger jumps early, smaller ones near the working weight |
| Second exercise for the same muscles | 1 to 2 | A short set of 3 to 5, then 1 to 2 reps, to find the groove |
| Sore or previously injured joint | Add 1 to 2 first | 10 to 15 easy reps with the empty bar or a very light load |

## Terms

- **20-rep squat (breathing squat)**: An old rest-pause method: one set of 20 squats with a weight you could normally lift about 10 times, made possible by standing with the bar and taking several deep breaths between reps.
- **Back-cycle (reset)**: A planned step back after a stall: cut the load by 10 to 20%, then build up again over three to four weeks and aim to pass the old best.
- **Beginner (novice)**: A lifter who still progresses on a simple, repeated routine. The 2009 ACSM position stand uses novice for people with no lifting experience, or none for several years.
- **Compound and isolation exercises**: A compound (multi-joint) exercise moves two or more joints and works several muscles, as in a squat or row. An isolation (single-joint) exercise moves one joint, as in a leg extension or curl. Both exist as free-weight and machine versions.
- **Double progression**: A way of progressing inside a rep range such as 6 to 12: add reps from session to session until you reach the top of the range, then add weight, which drops you back toward the bottom, and build up again.
- **Five sets of five (5x5)**: A strength routine of five sets of five reps on a few compound lifts, popularised by Bill Starr in a book for football players and revived by later barbell programmes. The label covers several quite different set plans.
- **Force-velocity curve**: The trade-off between the force a muscle can produce and its shortening speed: the faster the movement, the lower the force. Power, the product of the two, peaks in the middle of the curve.
- **General and specific warm-up**: A general warm-up is light whole-body activity that raises body temperature. A specific warm-up is progressively heavier sets of the exercise you are about to train, which also rehearse technique and ready the nervous system.
- **Hypertrophy and hyperplasia**: Hypertrophy is the enlargement of existing muscle fibres. Hyperplasia would be a rise in the number of fibres. Growth from training in humans is explained by hypertrophy; hyperplasia is unproven and, in McDonald's view, minor at most.
- **Load intensity and effort intensity**: Two different meanings of intensity. Intensity of load is the weight as a share of your one-rep max. Intensity of effort is how close the set comes to failure, often given as RPE or reps in reserve.
- **Maximal strength**: The greatest force you can produce in one effort with no time limit. In the gym it is measured as a one-rep max. Coaches outside the lifting sports often track a 3- or 5-rep max to avoid true maximum attempts.
- **Metabolic (depletion) training**: Weight training with light loads, sets of 15 to 20 or more and rests of a minute or less. It empties muscle glycogen and builds tolerance for fatigue more than it builds size or strength.
- **Myofibrillar and sarcoplasmic hypertrophy**: Myofibrillar growth adds contractile protein, the part of a fibre that produces force. Sarcoplasmic growth is a disproportionate expansion of the fluid and stored material around it.
- **Outcome, performance and process goals**: An outcome goal is a result against others, such as winning. A performance goal is a standard of your own, such as a 100 kg (220 lb) squat. A process goal is an action you carry out, such as hitting depth on every rep.
- **Post-activation potentiation and complex training**: A short rise in explosive performance after a few heavy efforts. Complex training alternates heavy sets with jumps to use it. A meta-analysis of 47 studies found small to moderate effects, larger in stronger, more experienced lifters.
- **Power (muscular power)**: Work divided by the time taken to do it, or force multiplied by speed. Lifting the same weight through the same distance in half the time doubles the power.
- **Prilepin's chart**: A table from Soviet weightlifting, attributed to A. S. Prilepin, that gives reps per set and total reps in a session for each band of intensity. It records what elite weightlifters did and was never tested in an experiment.
- **Ramped sets and sets across**: Ramped sets rise in weight from set to set, so only the last one or two are hard. Sets across use the same working weight for every set, after separate warm-up sets.
- **Rate of force development (RFD)**: How quickly force rises at the start of an effort. Sprint steps, jumps and throws allow only about 0.1 to 0.2 seconds, so the force you can reach in that window matters more than your slow maximum.
- **Relative strength**: Strength divided by body weight. It matters most where you move your own body or compete in a weight class. Gaining weight faster than strength lowers it.
- **Repetition continuum**: The idea that low, moderate and high reps lean toward strength, size and endurance in turn. The zones overlap: the heavy end of size training is the light end of strength training.
- **Repetition maximum (RM) load**: The top weight a lifter can manage for a stated number of reps in good form. A 10RM is a load you can lift ten times and not eleven. A zone such as 8 to 12RM sets the load without a percentage.
- **Static, dynamic and ballistic stretching**: Static stretching holds a muscle at length without moving. Dynamic stretching moves a joint under control through a range that grows with each rep. Ballistic stretching flings the limb through the range and has fallen out of favour.
- **Technical failure**: The point in a set where good form can no longer be kept, even if a rougher rep is still possible. On skilled lifts it comes before momentary muscular failure and is the safer place to stop.
- **Training variables (loading parameters)**: The settings that define a programme: intensity (how heavy), volume (how many sets and reps), frequency (how often) and exercise choice. Rest between sets and speed of movement are often added.
- **Volume load (tonnage)**: Sets multiplied by reps multiplied by the weight lifted, added up over a session or a week. Three sets of 5 with 100 kg (220 lb) is 1,500 kg (3,300 lb).
- **Weider principles**: Names such as the superset, split routine and drop set principles, attached by the publisher Joe Weider to methods lifters already used. They are labels from his magazines, not discoveries; the hypertrophy area covers what the methods do.
- **Work capacity**: The amount of training you can do and recover from. Beginners build it slowly. Leaving every session exhausted means the workload is ahead of it.
