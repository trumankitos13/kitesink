# Health: KiteSink fitness field guide

Training for a long working life. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, how well supported it is, and its sources.

## Guidance

### WHO asks adults for 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week

**Aim for 150 to 300 minutes of moderate or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity a week, plus strength work on 2 or more days.**

The 2020 WHO guidelines give these ranges for all adults, with an equivalent mix of moderate and vigorous activity allowed. They also advise muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days a week. The US 2018 and UK 2019 guidelines set the same weekly minimums. Moderate means you can talk but not sing. Vigorous means you can say only a few words before a breath.

- Add up walks, rides and sessions across the week to reach the range.
- Place two strength sessions on separate days.

Activity guidelines · Beginner · Rulebook · Sources: [Bull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955), [Piercy 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.14854), [UCMO 2019](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/physical-activity-guidelines-uk-chief-medical-officers-report)

### Mortality benefit levelled off at about two to five times the minimum

**In large cohorts, the lowest death rates sat near 2 to 5 times the guideline minimum, with no clear harm at higher amounts.**

Arem 2015 pooled 661,137 adults and found a 31% lower mortality risk at 1 to 2 times the minimum and 37% at 2 to 3 times, with the best result near 3 to 5 times and no excess risk at 10 or more times. Lee 2022 followed 116,221 US health professionals and found the near-maximum association at about 150 to 300 minutes a week of vigorous or 300 to 600 of moderate activity. Both studies are observational and rely on self-reported activity.

- Treat 300 minutes a week of moderate activity as a ceiling for health benefit, not a requirement.

Activity guidelines · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Arem 2015](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.0533), [Lee 2022](https://doi.org/10.1161/circulationaha.121.058162)

### Death risk in cohorts fell with daily steps and flattened around 5,000 to 8,000

**Cohort data show the steepest gains in the first thousands of steps, with 7,000 steps linked to about half the risk of 2,000.**

Paluch 2022 pooled 15 cohorts (47,471 adults): risk fell until about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day at age 60 and over and 8,000 to 10,000 under 60. Ding 2025 pooled 31 studies and found 7,000 steps a day linked to 47% lower all-cause mortality than 2,000 (HR 0.53). Evidence certainty was rated moderate. These are associations from device-measured cohorts.

- Use 7,000 steps as a realistic target and add more if it is easy.

Activity guidelines · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Paluch 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2468-2667(21)00302-9), [Ding 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2468-2667(25)00164-1)

### About an hour of daily moderate activity matched the extra risk from long sitting

**People who sat over 8 hours but did about 60 to 75 minutes of moderate activity a day showed no raised mortality risk.**

This pooled analysis covered 16 studies, and the sitting analysis used 13 of them with 1,005,791 people. Those sitting more than 8 hours a day with the highest activity level had a hazard ratio of 1.04 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.10) against the most active, least sedentary group. High TV time stayed linked to higher risk, though less so in the most active. Self-reported sitting is imprecise.

- Break up long sitting spells and fit in a daily walk or ride.

Activity guidelines · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Ekelund 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736(16)30370-1)

### The talk test finds moderate effort without a lab

**If you can talk but not sing, you are in the moderate range, and if you can only say a few words, you are at vigorous effort.**

The talk test links speech to the ventilatory and lactate thresholds. A 2014 review describes it as valid, reliable and practical for healthy adults and heart patients, and comfortable speech is likely when you are below the threshold. The US guidelines give the same rule of thumb. The review notes it may not suit high-intensity intervals.

- Hold a steady pace where full sentences are easy.
- Slow down if you need to catch your breath mid-sentence.

Aerobic base and zone 2 · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Reed 2014](https://doi.org/10.1097/hco.0000000000000097), [Piercy 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.14854)

### Zone 2 means effort just below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold

**Experts define zone 2 as easy aerobic work just under the first threshold, where breathing rises but speech stays comfortable.**

A 14-person panel of sport scientists and coaches agreed that zone 2 is best placed immediately below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold. The thresholds differ from person to person and shift with training. The panel said the adaptations are likely not unique to zone 2 and can occur a little above or below. This is expert opinion, not trial evidence.

- Start with the talk test to find a steady, conversational pace.

Aerobic base and zone 2 · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Sitko 2025](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2024-0303), [MacIntosh 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2021.682233)

### Evidence does not show zone 2 is the best intensity for health

**A 2025 review found no proof that zone 2 is optimal and argued harder work matters for health, especially at low weekly volumes.**

The review traced the zone 2 claims largely to observational data from elite endurance athletes who train a lot. It concluded the evidence does not support zone 2 as the optimal intensity for building mitochondrial or fat-burning capacity. It also said that higher intensities are important for cardiometabolic health when time is short. It is a narrative review, so it reflects the authors' reading.

- If time is short, keep some harder work in the week alongside easy sessions.

Aerobic base and zone 2 · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Storoschuk 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02261-y), [Sitko 2025](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2024-0303)

### Interval training raised VO2 max slightly more than steady training in short trials

**Pooled trials show interval training lifts VO2 max a little more than continuous training, by roughly 1 to 3 mL/kg/min.**

In healthy young to middle-aged adults, the gain was a possibly small 1.2 mL/kg/min more than endurance training (Milanovic 2015). In 273 patients with cardiometabolic disease across 10 studies, interval training beat moderate continuous training by 3.03 mL/kg/min, or 9.1% (Weston 2014). Both reviews pool short trials.

- Add one interval session a week if your clinician agrees you can train hard.

Aerobic base and zone 2 · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Milanović 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0365-0), [Weston 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092576)

### Higher aerobic fitness goes with lower mortality, with no upper limit seen

**Across large cohorts, each 1 MET higher fitness was linked to 11% to 17% lower all-cause mortality, and the top fitness group did best.**

An umbrella review of 26 reviews found high versus low fitness linked to a hazard ratio of 0.47 for all-cause mortality, with evidence certainty from very low to moderate. Kodama 2009 found low fitness carried a relative risk of 1.70 against high fitness. In 122,007 treadmill-tested patients and 750,302 veterans, the lowest risk was in the fittest group, with no rise in risk at the top. These are observational findings, and fitter people may differ in other ways.

- Test your fitness over time with a repeatable walk, run or ride.

VO2 max and fitness · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Lang 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-107849), [Mandsager 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2018.3605), [Kokkinos 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2022.05.031), [Kodama 2009](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2009.681)

### Weaker grip strength tracks with higher risk of death in large cohorts

**Each 5 kg (11 lb) lower grip strength went with about 16% to 20% higher all-cause mortality risk, a marker and not a proven cause.**

In 139,691 adults in the PURE study, the hazard ratio per 5 kg lower grip was 1.16 for all-cause death. In half a million UK Biobank participants it was 1.20 in women and 1.16 in men. A meta-analysis of 38 studies found higher grip linked to lower mortality (HR 0.69). The authors say trials are still needed to show that raising strength lowers risk.

- Track grip or another strength test over time and ask a clinician about low results.

Strength and muscle · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Leong 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736(14)62000-6), [Celis-Morales 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.k1651), [García-Hermoso 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2018.01.008)

### About 30 to 60 minutes a week of strength training was linked to the lowest mortality

**Pooled cohorts link any strength training to 10% to 17% lower mortality, with the best result near 30 to 60 minutes a week.**

Momma 2022 pooled 16 studies and found a J-shaped link, with the lowest risk at about 30 to 60 minutes a week. Shailendra 2022 pooled 10 studies and found 15% lower all-cause mortality for any resistance training, with a maximum reduction of 27% at about 60 minutes a week and smaller benefit at higher volumes. These are observational and training was self-reported.

- Start with 2 short strength sessions a week.

Strength and muscle · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Momma 2022](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061), [Shailendra 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2022.03.020)

### Resistance training builds strength, muscle and function in older adults

**Progressive resistance training reliably improves strength and daily function in people aged 60 and over, and harder training works better.**

A Cochrane review found a large effect on strength (73 trials, 3,059 people, SMD 0.84) and improved physical function. A meta-analysis of 47 studies found harder training gave bigger strength gains. Another of 49 studies found an average lean mass gain of 1.1 kg, with higher volume helping. The NSCA position statement backs resistance training for older adults. Serious adverse events were rare.

- Begin with a supervised program if you are new or have health conditions.
- Raise the load gradually over weeks.

Strength and muscle · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Liu Cj 2009](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd002759.pub2), [Peterson 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arr.2010.03.004), [PETERSON 2011](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3181eb6265), [Fragala 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003230)

### Strength falls two to five times faster than muscle size in later life

**At about age 75, strength drops about 2.5% to 4% a year while muscle mass drops under 1% a year.**

A review of longitudinal studies found muscle mass loss of 0.64% to 0.70% a year in women and 0.80% to 0.98% in men at age 75, with strength loss of 3% to 4% a year in men and 2.5% to 3% in women. Loss of strength carried a more consistent risk for disability and death than loss of muscle. The European consensus for sarcopenia now puts low strength first.

- Train strength, not only walking, to protect the quality that falls fastest.

Strength and muscle · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Mitchell 2012](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2012.00260), [Cruz-Jentoft 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/afy169)

### Raising activity in midlife was linked to similar mortality benefit to lifelong activity

**Adults who stepped up activity between ages 40 and 61 had a mortality risk similar to those who were always active.**

In a US cohort, those who kept high leisure activity through adulthood had a hazard ratio of 0.64 for all-cause death compared with the consistently inactive. Those who were less active until they raised it at ages 40 to 61 had 0.65. This is observational. Trials in older adults show strength and function improve with resistance training.

- Begin now with a modest plan and build over months.

Strength and muscle · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Saint-Maurice 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.0355), [Liu Cj 2009](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd002759.pub2)

### Exercise that challenges balance cuts falls by about a quarter in older adults at home

**Falls fall about 23% with exercise in community-dwelling older adults, and balance-challenging programs of over 3 hours a week did best.**

A Cochrane review of 108 trials (23,407 people, average age 76) found exercise reduced the rate of falls by 23% (rate ratio 0.77), with high-certainty evidence. An earlier meta-analysis found programs that challenged balance and ran over 3 hours a week gave a 39% cut. No effect was seen in aged care homes or after stroke. Most participants were women.

- Choose programs that include standing balance work, not only walking.
- Ask a physiotherapist or clinician to pick safe options if you have fallen.

Bones and balance · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Sherrington 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd012424.pub2), [Sherrington 2016](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096547)

### LIFTMOR: supervised heavy lifting and impact work raised spine bone density

**In 101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass, eight months of supervised heavy lifting and impact work improved spine bone density.**

LIFTMOR gave 49 women twice-weekly 30-minute supervised sessions of 5 sets of 5 repetitions at over 85% of one-rep max, with impact loading, while 52 did a home low-intensity program. Lumbar spine density changed by +2.9% versus -1.2% and femoral neck by +0.3% versus -1.9%. One minor adverse event occurred. Women were screened and supervised, so this does not suit unsupervised heavy training. The UK consensus advises resistance and impact exercise for bone and avoiding deep spinal flexion. After a vertebral fracture, impact should usually stay near brisk walking.

- Discuss any heavy or impact work with a clinician and use qualified supervision.

Bones and balance · Advanced · Thin evidence · Sources: [Watson 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.3284), [Brooke-Wavell 2022](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-104634)

### Adults are advised to sleep 7 or more hours a night

**The AASM and SRS consensus is 7 or more hours a night for adults, and the National Sleep Foundation gives 7 to 9 for most adults.**

The joint statement says regularly sleeping under 7 hours is linked to weight gain, diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, depression and higher mortality risk. The National Sleep Foundation panel put 7 to 9 hours for young adults and adults and 7 to 8 hours for older adults. Needs vary, and long-lasting trouble sleeping is a reason to speak to a clinician.

- Set a regular bedtime that leaves a 7 to 9 hour window.

Sleep and protein · Beginner · Rulebook · Sources: [Watson 2015](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4716), [Hirshkowitz 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2014.12.010)

### Regular exercise gave small to moderate gains in sleep quality

**Across 66 studies, regular exercise improved total sleep time and efficiency a little and sleep quality moderately.**

A 2015 meta-analysis found acute exercise had small benefits for sleep onset and efficiency, and regular exercise had small benefits for total sleep time and efficiency, small to medium for time to fall asleep and moderate for sleep quality. Intensity did not clearly change the effects. Time of day, type and adherence did matter in places.

- Keep to regular training and notice how your sleep responds.

Sleep and protein · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Kredlow 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10865-015-9617-6)

### Older adults are advised at least 1.0 to 1.2 g of protein per kg a day

**Expert groups advise at least 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg (0.45 to 0.54 g per lb) a day over age 65, and more with exercise or illness.**

The PROT-AGE and ESPEN groups both advise 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older people. PROT-AGE advises 1.2 g/kg or more for those exercising and 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg for many with illness. ESPEN gives 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg for those at risk of malnutrition. People with severe kidney disease may need to limit protein, so check with a clinician.

- Spread protein through the day and ask a clinician if you have kidney disease.

Sleep and protein · Intermediate · Well supported · Sources: [Bauer 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jamda.2013.05.021), [Deutz 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2014.04.007)

### Creatine plus resistance training added lean mass and some strength in older adults

**Across 22 trials in adults averaging 57 to 70 years, creatine added 1.37 kg (3.0 lb) of lean mass and small strength gains.**

The meta-analysis (721 people, 7 to 52 weeks of training 2 to 3 days a week) found greater lean tissue gain (1.37 kg), chest press (SMD 0.35) and leg press strength (SMD 0.24) with creatine than placebo. The ISSN position states supplementation was safe and well tolerated in healthy people, up to 30 g a day for 5 years. People with kidney or other conditions should ask a clinician first.

- Talk to a clinician before use if you have kidney disease or take medication.

Supplements · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Chilibeck 2017](https://doi.org/10.2147/oajsm.s123529), [Kreider 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z)

### Vitamin D pills did not prevent fractures, falls or major disease in trials

**Large trials and meta-analyses of vitamin D supplements found no effect on fractures, falls or major disease in general adult groups.**

A meta-analysis of 81 trials (53,537 people) found no effect on fractures (RR 1.00), hip fractures or falls. The VITAL trial (25,871 adults, 2,000 IU a day, median 5.3 years) found no lower cancer or cardiovascular event rates. The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests against routine blood testing and suggests supplements only for specific groups such as adults over 75. Results do not cover people who are deficient.

- Ask a clinician before supplementing, especially if you have a bone or kidney condition.

Supplements · Intermediate · Well supported · Sources: [Bolland 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2213-8587(18)30265-1), [Manson 2019b](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa1809944), [Demay 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgae290)

### Omega-3 had little effect on death or heart events but lowered triglycerides

**A Cochrane review of 86 trials found little or no effect on mortality or cardiovascular events, with about a 15% drop in triglycerides.**

Across 86 trials (162,796 people), long-chain omega-3 had little or no effect on all-cause mortality (RR 0.97) or cardiovascular events (RR 0.96). It slightly reduced coronary heart disease events and triglycerides by about 15%. The VITAL trial of 1 g a day found no fewer major cardiovascular events or cancers. Trials were in adults at varying cardiovascular risk.

Supplements · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Abdelhamid 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd003177.pub5), [Manson 2019a](https://doi.org/10.1056/nejmoa1811403)

### Fasting glucose and HbA1c have published ranges for prediabetes and diabetes

**The American Diabetes Association lists prediabetes at fasting glucose 100 to 125 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7% to 6.4%.**

The ADA Standards of Care list prediabetes as fasting plasma glucose 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L) or HbA1c 5.7% to 6.4% (39 to 47 mmol/mol). Diabetes is fasting glucose of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or more or HbA1c of 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or more, with confirmation. Fasting means no calories for at least 8 hours. Results are for discussion with a clinician, who makes any diagnosis.

- Take your results to a clinician and ask what they mean for you.

Health markers · Beginner · Rulebook · Sources: [ADAPPCD 2025](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-s002)

### Waist-to-height ratio of 0.5 or more marks increased health risk in NICE guidance

**NICE says a waist-to-height ratio of 0.5 to 0.59 means increased health risk and 0.6 or more means further increased risk.**

Divide waist by height in the same units, for example 85 cm (33.5 in) by 170 cm (67 in) is 0.5. A meta-analysis of 31 papers found the ratio discriminated cardiometabolic risk factors better than BMI or waist circumference alone. It is a screening measure, so discuss any result with a clinician.

- Measure your waist at the same point each time and compare with your height.

Health markers · Beginner · Rulebook · Sources: [NIHCE 2025](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng246), [Ashwell 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-789x.2011.00952.x)

### A higher resting heart rate was linked to higher mortality risk in a meta-analysis

**Each 10 beats a minute higher resting rate went with 9% higher all-cause mortality risk, and readings over 80 with 45% higher.**

The 2016 meta-analysis found relative risk 1.09 per 10 beats/min and 1.45 for over 80 compared with the lowest category, with substantial heterogeneity and publication bias. MedlinePlus lists 60 to 100 beats a minute as the adult resting range and 40 to 60 for well-trained athletes. Measure at rest after 10 minutes, and discuss persistently high or low readings with a clinician.

- Record your pulse at rest on several mornings to see your average.

Health markers · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Zhang 2015](https://doi.org/10.1503/cmaj.150535), [MPNLM 2025](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm)

### ApoB counts the particles that carry cholesterol and can disagree with LDL cholesterol

**A National Lipid Association consensus says that when apoB and LDL-C disagree, risk tends to follow apoB.**

LDL-C measures cholesterol mass, while apoB reflects the number of atherogenic particles. The NLA consensus says apoB is a useful addition to a standard lipid panel and risk tends to track apoB when the two disagree. It lists 160 mg/dL LDL-C and 130 mg/dL apoB as about the 90th percentile of untreated adults, both risk-enhancing. Results are for discussion with a clinician.

Health markers · Advanced · Some support · Sources: [Soffer 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacl.2024.08.013), [Sniderman 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2019.3780)

## Common mistakes

### Using an age formula as your zone 2 heart rate

**Age formulas give a population average, so use them as a rough guide and check against how the effort feels.**

The common estimate of maximum heart rate is 208 minus 0.7 times age, drawn from 351 studies and 18,712 adults. It describes the average, not you. A review of exercise intensity methods found that fixed percentages of maximum heart rate have little merit for hitting a given physiological domain. Thresholds differ between people and move with training.

- Use the talk test as the main check.
- Treat any heart rate number as a starting range.

Aerobic base and zone 2 · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Tanaka 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0735-1097(00)01054-8), [Jamnick 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01322-8)

### Treating 10,000 steps as a pass mark

**In cohorts, benefit rose steeply to about 7,000 steps, and 10,000 is a fine target without being a threshold.**

Ding 2025 found 7,000 steps a day linked to 47% lower all-cause mortality than 2,000. The authors say 10,000 can still suit more active people and 7,000 may be a more achievable target. Paluch 2022 found the benefit plateau at 6,000 to 8,000 steps for those 60 and over and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. Both are observational.

- Compare today with your own usual number and add a few thousand steps.

Activity guidelines · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Ding 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2468-2667(25)00164-1), [Paluch 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2468-2667(21)00302-9)

### Treating a mortality predictor as a score to chase

**Grip strength and fitness predict risk in cohorts, and that does not show that pushing the number up lowers risk by a set amount.**

The grip strength authors say further research is needed to test whether improving strength reduces mortality. An umbrella review of fitness found certainty of evidence from very low to moderate. Fitter and stronger people tend to differ in many other ways. Training is worth doing for the other benefits it brings, and heavy or extreme efforts need medical clearance when risk is present.

- Use test scores to track progress, not to promise outcomes.

VO2 max and fitness · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Leong 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736(14)62000-6), [Lang 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-107849)

### Doing only walking or cardio and skipping strength and balance work

**WHO advises strength work on 2 or more days for all adults and balance plus strength on 3 or more days for older adults.**

Cohort data on 479,856 adults linked meeting both aerobic and strength guidelines to a hazard ratio of 0.60 for all-cause death, against 0.71 for aerobic alone. Strength falls faster than muscle size with age, and resistance training is the main exercise approach shown to counter it. The NSCA position statement backs resistance training for older adults.

- Add two strength sessions a week.
- Add balance practice if you are over 65.

Strength and muscle · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Bull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955), [Zhao 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.m2031), [Fragala 2019](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003230)

## A simple heart-rate zone guide

A five-zone effort guide with how each feels. Zones are a coaching convention and the boundaries differ between people and systems.

| Zone | How it feels | Talk test | Heart rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Recovery | Very easy, little effort | Chat freely, can sing | Just above resting |
| 2 Easy aerobic | Steady, comfortable, can last long | Full sentences, cannot sing | Just below the first threshold |
| 3 Moderate | Working, pace feels brisk | Short sentences | Above the first threshold |
| 4 Hard | Uncomfortable, hard to hold | A few words at a time | Near the second threshold |
| 5 Maximal | All-out, only for short bursts | Cannot speak | Near your maximum |

## Terms

- **ApoB (apolipoprotein B)**: A protein carried on each cholesterol-bearing particle that can clog arteries. The blood level reflects the number of these particles and can differ from LDL cholesterol.
- **Dynapenia**: The age-related loss of muscle strength. In later life it runs faster than the loss of muscle size, so strength and mass are tracked separately.
- **Hazard ratio (HR)**: A figure comparing risk between groups over time. 1.0 means no difference, 0.80 means 20% lower risk and 1.20 means 20% higher. It shows association, not cause.
- **HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin)**: A blood test that shows average blood sugar over about the past 2 to 3 months. It is given as a percentage, and published ranges separate normal, prediabetes and diabetes.
- **Lactate threshold**: The effort above which lactate starts to build up in the blood faster than it clears. The first threshold marks the end of easy effort and varies between people.
- **MET (metabolic equivalent)**: A unit of effort. 1 MET is resting energy use, and brisk walking is about 3 to 5 METs. Fitness is often reported as the peak METs reached on a treadmill test.
- **Sarcopenia**: A condition of low muscle strength with low muscle quantity or quality, found mostly in older adults. A clinician diagnoses it using strength tests and a body measure.
- **VO2 max**: The highest rate at which your body can use oxygen during hard exercise, in mL of oxygen per kg of body weight per minute. It is the standard lab measure of aerobic fitness.
- **Waist-to-height ratio**: Waist circumference divided by height, both in the same units. A ratio of 0.5 means the waist is half your height.
- **Zone 2**: Easy aerobic effort just below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold. You breathe harder but can still speak in sentences. Zone numbers vary between systems.
