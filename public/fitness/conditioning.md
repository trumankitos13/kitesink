# Conditioning: KiteSink fitness field guide

Cardio and intervals, and how they fit with lifting. Each entry gives the point, the reasoning, what to do, how well supported it is, and its sources.

## Guidance

### Endurance performance rests on VO2 max, threshold and efficiency

**Three things set endurance performance: peak oxygen uptake, the share of it you can sustain, and how much speed each unit of oxygen buys.**

A review of champion athletes names three main factors: VO2 max, the lactate threshold and efficiency, meaning the oxygen cost of a given speed or power. VO2 max and threshold together set the oxygen uptake you can hold for the length of a race. Efficiency then decides how fast that uptake moves you. Two people with the same VO2 max can therefore race very differently. A high VO2 max is needed at the top level and does not win by itself. The authors add that motivation and social factors also shape who becomes a champion.

- Track the pace or power you can hold for a set time. A VO2 max estimate alone says little.

### Aerobic fitness supports repeated hard efforts in stop-and-go sports

**Recovery between sprints leans on the aerobic system, so basic aerobic work earns a place in team and combat sports.**

Repeated-sprint ability means holding your average over a series of sprints of 10 seconds or less with recoveries of 60 seconds or less. A review found it depends on oxidative capacity, phosphocreatine recovery and buffering as well as neural factors. It recommends some training for single-sprint speed plus some interval work at 80 to 90 percent of VO2 max to speed recovery between sprints. Lyle McDonald's view is narrower: these athletes need basic aerobic sessions and short sprints, and practice of the sport supplies most of the rest. In track races, energy supply turns mainly aerobic within 15 to 30 seconds.

- Keep a few easy aerobic sessions a week if your sport is stop-and-go.
- Train sprint speed with short efforts and full rests.

### No single intensity trains every part of endurance

**Easy volume, threshold work and intervals stress different parts of the system, so McDonald argues none can be the one best intensity.**

Endurance training raises the heart's stroke volume, blood volume, capillary supply, mitochondria, aerobic enzymes and the handling of acid. Lyle McDonald's point is that these change at different speeds and answer to different mixes of intensity and duration. Easy work mainly trains slow-twitch fibres, and harder work is needed to reach fast-twitch ones. Trial evidence fits his argument. Across 13 studies of trained endurance athletes, a polarized split did not clearly beat other ways of dividing training for VO2 max or time-trial results.

- Choose your mix from your goal and weekly hours. Be wary of claims that one zone is best.

Methods and intensity zones · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Rosenblat 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02149-3), [McDonald 2009r](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/guide-to-endurance-training-methods)

### Easy aerobic training pays off through volume

**Elite endurance athletes do about 80% of sessions easy, but they train 10 or more times a week. A few easy hours do far less.**

Studies of national and international endurance athletes who train 10 to 13 times a week converge on about 80 percent of sessions at low intensity, near 2 mmol/L of blood lactate, with about 20 percent dominated by hard work. The German team that set the 4000 m team pursuit record in 2000 built its aerobic fitness on 29,000 to 35,000 km (18,000 to 21,700 miles) of riding a year, for a race of about four minutes. Lyle McDonald's caution is that this approach depends on the sheer amount done. Once you are past the beginner stage, he argues, three to six easy hours a week bring little further gain.

- If you train only a few hours a week, do not copy an elite split by default.

Methods and intensity zones · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Seiler 2010](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.5.3.276), [Schumacher 2002](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-200206000-00020), [McDonald 2009s](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-1)

### Polarized training has not clearly beaten other intensity splits

**Pooled trials show similar time-trial results for polarized and other splits. Recreational athletes may do as well or better on pyramidal.**

A network meta-analysis of individual data from 13 studies and 348 trained endurance athletes found no difference between polarized and pyramidal training for VO2 max or time-trial performance,. Competitive athletes may gain more VO2 max from polarized training and recreational athletes from pyramidal. A meta-analysis of 17 studies found a small VO2 peak edge for polarized training, only in programmes under 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes. McDonald's 2009 series predates these trials. His advice that people short of time swap some easy work for middle-intensity work fits them.

- With limited weekly hours, a mostly easy week with some tempo work is a sound default.

### The more often you train, the easier most sessions must be

**McDonald's rule: plenty of training time means mostly easy work, and little time means harder steady work. High volume and high intensity do not mix.**

Lyle McDonald frames endurance planning as a trade. Harder steady work gives more aerobic stimulus per hour, and he estimates one hard hour is worth roughly two easy ones. It also takes longer to recover from, so it cannot be done every day. With three or four sessions a week, he would make most of them tempo-level work and add one longer easy session if time allows. With six sessions a week, two or three should be easy. Athletes who try to train both long and hard every day run past what they can recover from, and overuse injuries or overtraining follow.

- Count your weekly sessions first, then set how hard the typical one should be.
- When you add a training day, make it an easy one.

Methods and intensity zones · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2009q](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-2), [McDonald 2009w](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-methods-putting-it-together)

### Threshold sessions work in small doses

**Classic threshold work is 20 to 40 minutes at your one-hour pace, once a week. McDonald keeps it to about a tenth of training at most.**

The standard session is one to three blocks of 20 minutes at the hardest pace you could hold for about an hour, with about 10 minutes of easy movement between blocks. Shorter blocks with brief rests, which coach Jack Daniels calls cruise intervals, are easier to finish. Lyle McDonald's view is that this is the quickest way to raise the threshold when time is short, while a bigger aerobic base raises it more cheaply over years. He limits it to one session a week, two at most. Seiler and Tønnessen report that elite athletes give only one to three of 10 to 12 weekly sessions to work at or above this level.

- Start with shorter blocks, such as 4 x 5 minutes, before trying 20 minutes straight.

### VO2 max intervals last 2 to 5 minutes at just above threshold pace

**Work bouts of 2 to 5 minutes with equal or shorter rests keep you near peak oxygen uptake. Going harder only cuts the time you can spend there.**

The aim is time near maximal oxygen uptake. As McDonald describes common practice, the pace is only a little above threshold, close to a runner's 3 to 5 km race pace. Bouts last 2 to 5 minutes because uptake needs about a minute to climb, and rests are no longer than the work. A meta-analysis of 53 trials supports the longer format. Against steady training, only intervals of 2 minutes or more, with at least 15 minutes of hard work per session, over 4 to 12 weeks or more, showed an advantage for VO2 max. In 14 well-trained cyclists, varying the power inside each bout gave more time above 90 percent of VO2 max.

- Pace the first repeat so that the last one can match it.

Intervals and HIIT · Advanced · Some support · Sources: [Wen 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2019.01.013), [Bossi 2020](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2019-0260), [McDonald 2009v](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-4-part-2)

### Plan hard intervals in short blocks on top of an aerobic base

**McDonald uses hard intervals for three to six weeks at a time, then returns to base work. Untrained people keep improving for longer.**

Lyle McDonald's reading of research and coaching practice is that the systems trained by hard intervals respond fast and then level off, within about three weeks in trained athletes. He programmes them twice a week for three weeks or once a week for six, before an event. The German pursuit team added track-specific work only before major races. In untrained people the response lasts longer. Eight subjects who trained six days a week, three with six 5-minute intervals at VO2 max and three with 40-minute runs, raised VO2 max in a straight line for all 10 weeks, by 44 percent in total.

- Use a block of intervals before an event or a test, then go back to mostly steady work.

### Beginners improve on short, easy sessions

**Twenty to thirty easy minutes three times a week is enough to start. McDonald builds duration for 4 to 6 weeks before adding intervals.**

People new to training adapt to very little. Lyle McDonald's starting dose is 20 to 30 minutes of easy steady work three times a week, at an effort that feels like work but stays comfortable, building toward 45 to 60. Only after 4 to 6 weeks does he add harder work, in one or two sessions a week. Interval studies do show quick early gains, and a meta-analysis found that even short, low-volume interval programmes beat no training. He argues the efforts in those studies are more than most beginners will tolerate. The ACSM position stand adds that raising intensity and volume gradually may lower risk.

- Start with three easy sessions of 20 to 30 minutes a week.
- Add minutes before you add intensity.

### Very few weekly hours can be met with interval blocks a few times a year

**When weekly hours are too low for steady methods to work, an interval-heavy block of about 12 weeks can peak you for an event. It cannot run all year.**

McDonald passes on cycling coach Chris Carmichael's observation that the usual mix of easy, tempo and threshold riding works for amateurs who can ride 10 to 12 hours a week and stops working at about 8 hours or less. Below that, Carmichael builds a block around hard intervals plus one longer ride. It suits events of up to about three hours and must be followed by easier maintenance. McDonald's own caveats: interval-only training burns people out if it continues, and the approach does not transfer to running, where weekly volumes are about half those of cycling and joints limit hard sessions to two a week.

- Save interval-heavy blocks for the weeks before an event that matters.
- Between blocks, hold fitness with easier sessions.

Intervals and HIIT · Advanced · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2009w](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-methods-putting-it-together)

### The afterburn adds only a few dozen calories to a session

**EPOC is larger after intervals than after steady exercise, but the gap averages about 30 kcal or less. The session itself does the work.**

A review of the research found that even sessions long or hard enough to keep metabolism raised for 3 to 24 hours produce an EPOC of only 6 to 15 percent of the exercise's own oxygen cost. Its authors called early optimism about EPOC and weight loss generally unfounded. A 2021 systematic review of 22 studies compared interval and steady sessions directly. In studies that tracked more than 3 hours, EPOC averaged about 289 kJ (69 kcal) after intervals and 159 kJ (38 kcal) after steady exercise. Lyle McDonald reached the same conclusion in 2008: plan cardio around the energy burned during the session.

- Choose the session you can repeat and recover from, and ignore afterburn claims.

Calories, EPOC and efficiency · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [LaForgia 2006](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640410600552064), [Panissa 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13099), [McDonald 2008l](https://bodyrecomposition.com/research/epoc-after-exercise)

### One chamber study found a larger afterburn after 45 hard minutes

**Ten men burned 519 kcal in a hard 45-minute ride and about 190 kcal more over the next 14 hours, while fed enough to stay in energy balance.**

The study put 10 men aged 22 to 33 in a metabolic chamber for a rest day and an exercise day. The ride lasted 45 minutes at about 73 percent of VO2 max. Energy use stayed raised for 14 hours and added 190 kcal to the 519 kcal burned during the ride. Lyle McDonald's reading adds three cautions. The men were fed extra to stay in energy balance, so the result may not hold in a deficit. The effort was near the hardest pace most people can hold for that long, which few will repeat several times a week, least of all alongside lifting. And riding a little easier for 15 minutes longer burns about the same total.

- Read this as a finding for long, hard sessions at maintenance calories.

Calories, EPOC and efficiency · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Knab 2011](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3182118891), [McDonald 2011c](https://bodyrecomposition.com/research/epoc-following-45-minute-exercise-bout)

### Intervals and steady cardio give the same fat loss

**Pooled trials find trivial differences in fat mass and lean mass between interval and steady training. Choose by time, recovery and preference.**

A multilevel meta-analysis of randomised trials lasting at least four weeks found a trivial difference in fat mass between interval and moderate continuous training, and the same for fat-free mass, with high certainty. Its authors add that changes from either are likely to be small anyway. In 13 trials of overweight and obese adults, averaging 10 weeks at three sessions a week, both methods reduced fat mass and waist size with no significant difference, and intervals took about 40 percent less time. Lyle McDonald argued the same in 2008: neither method is superior, and each has costs and benefits.

- Use intervals when time is short and steady work when you want more sessions.
- Let diet drive fat loss. Either kind of cardio adds a little.

### A few all-out sprints matched long rides for early fitness gains

**In active young men, six sessions of 30-second all-out sprints improved time trials as much as six rides of 90 to 120 minutes.**

Sixteen active men trained six times in 14 days. One group did four to six all-out 30-second cycling sprints with 4 minutes of recovery. The other rode for 90 to 120 minutes at about 65 percent of VO2 peak. Training time was about 2.5 hours against 10.5, and the two groups improved their time trials by similar amounts. A later review from the same lab reached the same conclusion for low-volume interval work. McDonald's cautions: the subjects were not highly trained, the sprints demand a braked ergometer and very high motivation, and gym interval classes look nothing like this protocol.

- If you try sprint intervals, use a bike or rower and make each sprint a true all-out effort.

### Put intervals on leg-training days, in a separate session if you can

**McDonald groups hard leg work on the same days so the legs get two hard days a week instead of four. If both share a session, lift first.**

Two interval sessions can fit around two lower-body lifting days in two ways. Putting them on other days keeps every session fresh but loads the legs hard four days a week. Lyle McDonald prefers the same day, ideally hours apart, which leaves the other days for upper-body work, easy cardio or rest. When both must share one session, he puts the intervals after the lifting and on a bike or similar machine. Hard intervals before lifting make the lifting worse, and sprinting on legs tired from heavy lifting risks a knee or ankle injury. He also keeps at least one day a week fully off.

- Pair intervals with lower-body lifting days and keep the days between easy.
- After heavy leg work, choose a bike over running sprints.

Cardio with lifting · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2008y](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/steady-state-and-hiit)

### Very low carbohydrate intake and frequent hard intervals do not mix

**McDonald's rule: the diet sets the training or the training sets the diet. A lot of high-intensity work needs carbohydrate.**

Hard intervals and high-rep, short-rest lifting both run on muscle glycogen. Lyle McDonald's argument is that someone who removes nearly all carbohydrate and then adds several such sessions a week will run glycogen down, lose performance and drift into overtraining. He sees two workable choices. Keep carbohydrate very low and reduce high-intensity work to what can be sustained, with most cardio easy. Or keep the hard training and eat carbohydrate to support it, daily or in planned higher-carbohydrate periods. His wider point is that three tactics that each work alone can fail when stacked together.

- When carbohydrate is very low, keep cardio mostly easy.
- If you want frequent intervals, plan carbohydrate around them.

### On a diet, make most cardio steady and keep intervals to one or two sessions

**Steady cardio can be done most days without hurting recovery. Intervals cannot, least of all in a deficit with lifting.**

Dieters usually want some activity on most days. Lyle McDonald's advice is to make steady, low to moderate work the bulk of it, because it burns energy, can be repeated daily and does not cut into recovery from lifting. He notes that physique competitors reached contest condition for decades on low-intensity cardio alone. Intervals fit as one or two sessions a week, and people who tried them daily on reduced calories burned out. He also warns against a common contest-prep habit: raising lifting volume and adding hours of cardio when the body is least able to recover. Keep a few heavy sets and cut the rest.

- Fill most cardio days with steady work you could repeat tomorrow.
- Limit intervals to two sessions a week while dieting.

### Introduce intensity with short pick-ups inside an easy session

**After a month of regular cardio, add 30 to 60 seconds a little faster every 5 minutes. Lengthen the faster parts over several weeks.**

Lyle McDonald does not move beginners straight from easy cardio to hard intervals. First comes at least four weeks of consistent training, meaning three or more sessions a week of 20 minutes or longer at a moderate effort, with duration rising as fitness allows. Then he adds brief pick-ups: 30 to 60 seconds slightly above the usual pace, followed by about 4 minutes back at easy pace, repeated through the session. Over the following weeks the faster parts grow and the easy parts shrink. His reasoning is that an unfit person gains little from true interval intensity and carries a higher risk of injury.

- Hold the pick-ups at a pace only slightly above your normal one at first.

### Early trials that favoured intervals for fat loss did not control diet

**The well-known ninefold figure is a skinfold change divided by energy cost. Later pooled trials found no fat-loss advantage for intervals.**

A 1994 trial in young adults compared 20 weeks of endurance training with 15 weeks of intervals. The interval programme cost 57.9 MJ against 120.4 MJ and produced a larger fall in skinfolds, ninefold greater once divided by energy cost. Lyle McDonald notes the absolute change was small and diet was not controlled. He lists reasons such trials could favour intervals: hard exercise can blunt appetite, untrained people may gain a little muscle, and intervals make people work harder than the easy pace most use for steady cardio. Later meta-analyses of randomised trials found a trivial difference in fat mass.

- Be wary of fat-loss claims built on a single small trial without diet control.

### Stored fat is lost in three steps, and the slow step depends on how lean you are

**Fat has to be released from the fat cell, carried away in the blood, then burned. McDonald argues the limiting step differs between lean and obese people.**

Lyle McDonald explains fat loss as mobilization, transport and oxidation. Insulin holds fat in the cell and adrenaline-type hormones release it, blood carries it away, and muscle burns it. With a lot of body fat, he argues, the blood already carries plenty of fatty acids and the weak point is burning them. In lean dieters heading for very low body fat the reverse holds: fat cells respond more strongly to insulin, the last fat sits where blood flow is poor, and release becomes the limit. This is his model and has not been tested in a trial. Lasting fat loss still requires an energy deficit.

- Treat timing tricks as relevant, at most, to lean people getting leaner.

Fasted cardio · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2024](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/is-fasted-cardio-useless-for-fat-loss), [McDonald 2010e](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/fasted-cardio-fat-loss)

### Fasted cardio is unproven for very lean dieters, which differs from disproven

**The one diet-controlled trial ran 4 weeks in 20 women. McDonald argues it cannot rule out a small benefit in people who are already lean.**

Twenty young women in a calorie deficit did an hour of steady cardio three days a week for four weeks, half of them fasted and half after a shake. Both groups lost weight and fat mass, with no significant difference between them. Lyle McDonald accepts that result for people at average or high body fat, where he sees no reason to expect a benefit. He objects to stretching it to lean dieters going lower, which he puts at men near 12 to 15 percent body fat and women near 20 to 24 percent. The trial was short and was not limited to lean subjects. He calls a benefit there possible and unproven.

- At average or high body fat, do cardio at whatever time you will keep to.
- If you are lean and prefer fasted cardio, trials give no sign that it harms fat loss.

### Cardio burns only a few grams of protein, even with low glycogen

**In one small study, protein supplied about 10% of the energy for an hour of cycling when glycogen was depleted, close to 14 g.**

Six subjects cycled for an hour at 61 percent of VO2 max, after loading carbohydrate and after depleting it. In the depleted state, urea lost in sweat pointed to a protein breakdown of 13.7 g an hour, or 10.4 percent of the energy cost. Lyle McDonald uses about 5 percent as the usual share and reads both figures as small: a few grams a session, drawn largely from the body's pool of free amino acids. He does not see fasted cardio as a threat to muscle. His practical step is a little protein after the session, or sipped once it is under way. A meal beforehand raises insulin and defeats the purpose.

- Have a protein-containing meal or drink soon after a fasted session.

### Realistic amounts of exercise create a small deficit

**At 5 to 10 kcal a minute, burning 500 kcal takes 50 to 100 minutes of exercise. Food intake is usually the easier lever.**

Lyle McDonald's working figures for untrained people are 5 to 10 kcal a minute at efforts they can sustain, and 300 to 400 kcal for an hour of typical weight training including rests. Three 30-minute sessions a week at the low end come to about 450 kcal, around one eighth of the energy in a pound (0.45 kg) of fat. Trained athletes can burn two or three times as much per minute. Pooled trials agree on the scale: exercise programmes produced 1.5 to 3.5 kg (3 to 8 lb) of weight loss in an overview of 12 reviews. He adds that a real programme, unlike most trials, progresses the exercise as fitness grows.

- Set the deficit mainly through food, and use exercise to add to it.
- Progress duration or intensity as you get fitter.

### Keeping weight off is linked with about an hour of activity a day

**Successful maintainers report roughly an hour of daily activity, but controlled trials have not shown that exercise prevents regain.**

Lyle McDonald's view in 2010 was that exercise matters more for keeping weight off than for losing it, and that the dose is large: 2,500 to 2,800 kcal of exercise a week, which he works out as 40 to 70 minutes a day at 5 to 10 kcal a minute. Registry data fit that picture. Members who had kept off large losses for more than five years reported about an hour of physical activity a day. Controlled trials are less clear. A 2021 overview of systematic reviews found no significant effect of exercise training on weight maintenance and called for more research.

- Plan to keep or raise your activity after a diet ends.

### Exercise can help you hold a diet, and hunger responses vary

**McDonald rates better adherence among the most useful effects of exercise while dieting. Hunger after training differs between people.**

In Lyle McDonald's experience many people keep to their diet better on days they train, because they do not want to waste the effort. He considers this possibly more useful than the energy burned. The link runs both ways: when exercise stops, the diet often lapses with it. On appetite, he reads the research as showing that exercise tends to lower hunger for a while, and that extra eating afterwards usually falls short of the energy burned, with wide differences between people. He has also seen beginners get hungrier after sessions in their first weeks, an effect that fades after about a month.

- If you are new to training and hungrier after sessions, give it about four weeks before judging.

### Real sprint training is a few seconds of work with minutes of rest

**Coaches rest sprinters 1 to 2 minutes for every second of maximal sprinting. A proper session takes hours and burns little energy.**

Gym intervals of 30 to 60 seconds with short rests resemble middle-distance running more than sprinting. Lyle McDonald describes true speed work as efforts of about 6 to 8 seconds at top speed, each followed by long passive rest, after a long warm-up. A review of elite sprint practice gives the working rule: 1 to 2 minutes of recovery per second of maximal sprinting, so that two or three 100 m runs with 10 minutes between them can be a full session for an untrained junior. McDonald concludes that sprint training is the least time-efficient option for fitness or fat loss.

- If top speed is the goal, keep efforts short and rest until fully recovered.
- For fitness or fat loss, choose intervals or steady work over true sprints.

### Split long indoor sessions into blocks and change something at each one

**Thinking only about the current block makes an hour on a machine easier to finish, and the changes can add useful training.**

Lyle McDonald's method for indoor cardio is to stop treating a session as one long stretch. Divide it into a handful of blocks, such as three or four parts of an hour, and alter something each time a block ends: pace, incline, resistance or the machine itself. Attention stays on the block in progress. He cautions against going too far, since dozens of tiny blocks are as tedious as one long one, and treats the warm-up and cool-down as blocks of their own. The same approach lets you add small amounts of faster work or move up a level gradually. He puts the practical limit indoors near 90 minutes.

- Plan the blocks before you start and note what changes at each boundary.

### A little easy cardio fits a gaining phase

**McDonald's range while gaining is 20 to 45 minutes, three to five times a week, at or below 70% of maximum heart rate, on low-impact machines.**

Lyle McDonald rejects both no cardio and an hour every day. His range for a gaining phase starts at 20 to 30 minutes three times a week, tops out near 40 to 45 minutes, and rarely needs more than five sessions. Effort stays at or below about 70 percent of maximum heart rate, around 4 out of 10. He prefers bikes, rowers, ellipticals and brisk walking, and rules out running and intervals. A meta-analysis of 43 studies supports the low risk: adding aerobic training did not reduce muscle growth or maximal strength. His exception is the naturally thin lifter with a small appetite, who may be better off without it.

- Start with three easy sessions of 20 to 30 minutes a week.
- Eat back the energy if your weight gain stalls.

Cardio with lifting · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Schumann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7), [McDonald 2009g](https://bodyrecomposition.com/muscle-gain/cardio-for-mass-gains)

### Why keep some cardio when the goal is muscle

**McDonald's reasons: easier recovery, appetite for poor eaters, work capacity for higher-volume lifting, and a smoother start to the next diet.**

Lyle McDonald lists several benefits of easy cardio in a gaining phase, none of them large. Light movement works as active recovery, mainly for the legs. In people with a poor appetite it can help them eat more. It preserves work capacity, the ability to get through high-volume sessions, which heavy low-rep training with long rests lets slip; holding it takes far less than building it. He also observes that lifters who do no cardio while gaining are slow to start losing fat when they diet, and that keeping some in avoids this. He does not expect it to keep you lean; a controlled surplus does that.

- Treat off-season cardio as upkeep. It will not offset a large surplus.

### Keep cardio and lifting a few hours apart when power matters

**Gains in explosive strength were blunted when both were done in one session, less so with 3 hours or more between them.**

Across 43 studies, adding aerobic training left muscle growth and maximal strength unchanged but reduced gains in explosive strength, more so when both were done in one session than when at least 3 hours separated them. Lyle McDonald's advice goes the same way: do cardio on other days or about 12 hours from lifting when you can. He also tells lifters not to run. In the 2022 analysis running versus cycling made no significant difference, while a fibre-level analysis found a negative effect on type I fibres with running only.

- Put cardio on non-lifting days, or leave 3 or more hours between sessions.
- After heavy leg training, limit same-session cardio to a short, easy spin or walk.

### Intervals alongside lifting slightly reduce lower-body strength gains

**Pooled trials found similar muscle growth and upper-body strength with added intervals, but a smaller gain in lower-body strength.**

A meta-analysis compared resistance training alone with resistance training plus high-intensity intervals. Muscle growth and upper-body strength changed by similar amounts. Lower-body strength rose less with the intervals added (effect size -0.25). The authors suggest that longer rest between the two kinds of training may reduce the effect. This fits Lyle McDonald's position that intervals count as hard leg work. He does not recommend them in a gaining phase unless a sport requires them, and he accepts that some people combine the two without trouble.

- If squat or deadlift strength is the priority, limit intervals or place them well away from leg sessions.

Cardio with lifting · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Sabag 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2018.1464636), [McDonald 2009g](https://bodyrecomposition.com/muscle-gain/cardio-for-mass-gains)

### Training for a marathon or century: cut lifting to brief maintenance

**Two short full-body sessions a week with the usual loads will hold most strength and size while endurance training takes priority.**

When a lifter sets out to finish a marathon or a 100-mile ride, Lyle McDonald puts the endurance plan first. His template is two full-body sessions a week of 30 to 45 minutes: a few compound exercises and a couple of heavy sets each. He holds that strength and size survive a cut of up to two-thirds in volume and frequency if load is kept. A narrative review agrees: in younger adults, strength and muscle size were maintained for up to 32 weeks on as little as one session a week and one set per exercise, provided relative load stayed the same.

- Hold your working loads and cut sets and days.
- Return to normal lifting volume after the event.

Cardio with lifting · Intermediate · Some support · Sources: [Spiering 2021](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003964), [McDonald 2014p](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/combining-weight-marathon-training)

### Never lift the day before your long run or ride

**McDonald places lifting after the key endurance sessions on the same day, so hard days are grouped and the long session is done fresh.**

In a typical plan for finishing a marathon or century there are several short midweek sessions, one of them harder, and one long weekend session that grows each week. Lyle McDonald treats the long session as the one to protect, because running long on fatigued legs invites injury. His first choice is to lift later on the same day as the long session and the harder midweek session, which leaves the other days easy or off. If lifting has to fall on the day before a key session, he makes it a lighter, faster workout at about 75 to 85 percent of the usual loads. Leg lifting can drop to once a week.

- Mark the long session first, then fit lifting after it or two days before it.

Cardio with lifting · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2014p](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/combining-weight-marathon-training)

### For a very unfit beginner, the first goal is coming back

**McDonald builds the habit before the fitness: short, easy sessions that each end as a small success for the first 3 to 6 weeks.**

Lyle McDonald's rule for a heavy, untrained beginner is to break the person in without breaking them. Many arrive not wanting to be in a gym and expecting to fail, so the early aim is attendance. His first session held only 20 to 30 minutes of activity, the amount he found most beginners tolerate, and each later session added a little. He wanted every workout to end with the person feeling capable and free of crippling soreness. In his experience people begin to feel or see a benefit after 3 to 6 weeks, and those who get that far tend to stay. Volume and intensity can wait until then.

- Keep the first sessions to 20 to 30 minutes of easy work.
- Add a small amount each session so progress is visible.

### Let beginners set their own pace, just below where breathing gets hard

**Exercise below the ventilatory threshold feels pleasant to most people and exercise above it feels bad, which affects whether they return.**

A review of 33 studies found that most people report pleasant feelings during exercise below the ventilatory or lactate threshold, mixed responses close to it, and uniformly negative ones above it. Self-selected intensity was tolerated better than imposed intensity. A model built on this evidence argues that how exercise feels shapes whether people repeat it, and makes the case for self-paced exercise in overweight, sedentary adults. Lyle McDonald arrived at the same practice as a trainer. He asked for an effort of 3 to 4 out of 10, enough to notice while still able to hold a broken conversation.

- Ask for an effort of about 3 to 4 out of 10 in the first weeks.
- Raise the pace later, once the habit is in place.

### Very unfit beginners can start with five minutes of walking

**Start from what is manageable, add a minute or two each session, and reach 20 minutes three times a week before raising effort.**

Some of Lyle McDonald's heaviest clients could not walk briskly for more than five minutes at first, so that is where he started. He prefers treadmill walking early on: the movement is familiar, speed and incline adjust in small steps, and it tires the legs less than a bike or stepper. Each session adds one or two minutes. His first target is 20 continuous minutes three times a week at any pace, because duration and frequency come before intensity. After about four weeks of steady attendance he adds short, slightly harder stretches. Formal intervals wait until around week eight or later.

- Stop the first session when fatigue sets in, even at five minutes.
- Add one to two minutes per session until you reach 20.

### Several short bouts work as well as one long one

**Pooled trials found no fitness or blood pressure difference between exercise done in one bout and the same amount split across the day.**

A meta-analysis of 19 studies and 1,080 adults compared a single daily bout of exercise with the same mode, intensity and total time split into several bouts. Cardiorespiratory fitness and blood pressure changed equally. Body mass fell slightly more with the split approach, by 0.92 kg (2 lb) across five studies. For someone who tires after a few minutes, this removes the need to get through a long session. Lyle McDonald uses the idea within a gym visit as well, switching between short cardio blocks and a few strength exercises so that 15 to 30 minutes of aerobic work adds up.

- If 30 minutes at once is too much, do three 10-minute walks.
- In the gym, alternate short cardio blocks with strength exercises.

Starting out · Beginner · Well supported · Sources: [Murphy 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01145-2), [McDonald 2014w](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/training-the-obese-beginner-5)

### Start strength work and cardio together

**McDonald uses a few simple machine exercises from the first session because strength gains show within days, while aerobic gains take weeks to feel.**

Some advise very overweight beginners to do only cardio at first. Lyle McDonald does both from day one. Aerobic fitness takes three to four weeks to feel different, and he found many beginners quit near week three. Strength training fills the gap. He starts each exercise light, with one set of 8 to 12 repetitions on a handful of machines covering legs, pushing and pulling, so the weight can rise at the second visit. Part of that early gain is practice, which he accepts because it shows people their effort counts. Basic strength work also eases everyday tasks, which often wins people over before the scale moves.

- Begin with one easy set on four or five simple exercises.
- Record loads so the next session can show an increase.

## Common mistakes

### Blaming lactic acid for the burn and for fatigue

**Lactate marks hard effort. A biochemistry review argues that producing it slows acid build-up instead of causing it.**

The old account says lactic acid releases the protons that turn muscle acidic. A 2004 review of the biochemistry found no support for that. In its account, protons are released when ATP is broken down, and they pile up once effort passes the steady state and more ATP comes from glycolysis and the phosphagen system. Making lactate takes up protons, so it slows the fall in pH. Lactate stays a good indirect marker of those conditions. Other researchers published objections, so the details are debated. McDonald's training lesson: the more ATP your mitochondria can supply, the less acid you produce at a given pace.

- Read a high lactate value as a sign of intensity. It is not a toxin to flush out.

Methods and intensity zones · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Robergs 2004](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00114.2004), [McDonald 2009t](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-3)

### Doing every session at the same moderately hard effort

**Easy days drift too fast and hard days fall short, so everything lands near threshold effort.**

Seiler and Tønnessen describe this as a typical error of recreational athletes. In data they report from a study of recreational runners, a group meant to do 3 percent of its training in the middle zone logged 21 percent there, and its share of hard work fell from a planned 20 percent to 14. Sessions meant to be long and slow became faster and shorter, and intervals did not reach their target. McDonald adds the cost: middle-intensity work is fine a few times a week for someone with little time, but done daily it is hard to recover from.

- Keep easy days slow enough to talk in full sentences.
- Make hard days clearly hard, and keep them to one or two a week.

Methods and intensity zones · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Seiler 2009](https://www.sportsci.org/2009/ss.htm), [McDonald 2009q](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-2)

### Treating 4 mmol/L of lactate as everyone's threshold

**Blood lactate at the maximal steady state ranges from about 2 to 8 mmol/L between people, so a fixed 4 mmol/L line misplaces many of them.**

The maximal lactate steady state is the highest workload at which blood lactate stays level instead of climbing. A review reports that the lactate concentration at that point varies widely between individuals, from 2 to 8 mmol/L in capillary blood, and is unrelated to the workload reached. A zone table built on a fixed 4 mmol/L value will therefore be too easy for some people and too hard for others. The same review notes that average marathon speed sits just below this workload. McDonald's practical answer is to find threshold from what you can sustain, such as a well-paced effort of 20 to 60 minutes.

- Set threshold from a timed effort or from a test that tracks your own lactate curve.

Methods and intensity zones · Advanced · Some support · Sources: [Billat 2003](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200333060-00003), [McDonald 2009t](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-3)

### Pacing short intervals by heart rate

**Heart rate needs minutes to catch up with effort, so on a 30-second repeat it reads low. Set short intervals by pace, power or feel.**

Heart rate climbs slowly after effort changes and needs a few minutes to settle. During a 30-second repeat it rises only a little, and during a 10-second sprint it says nothing useful. Blood lactate lags in the same way. Lyle McDonald's advice is to set interval intensity from something that responds at once: a pace, a power reading, or the hardest even effort you can hold for the whole repeat and then repeat. New trainees usually start too fast and fade, so expect a few sessions of practice. Heart rate stays useful for steady sessions.

- Use pace, power or an even hard effort for work bouts under about two minutes.
- End the session when you can no longer hold your target pace or power.

Intervals and HIIT · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2009u](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-4-part-1)

### Running too much, too soon

**Novice runners are injured about twice as often per hour as recreational runners. Start with walk-run sessions and build slowly.**

A meta-analysis of prospective studies put injuries at 17.8 per 1,000 hours of running in novices against 7.7 in recreational runners. Lyle McDonald's explanation is that fitness and muscle adapt faster than tendons and joints, and that running adds impact that cycling, rowing and swimming lack. He also notes that a true beginner can rarely jog at an easy heart rate, because even the slowest jog is hard work at first. His advice is to alternate walking and jogging, to expect two months or more before running continuously, and to progress more slowly than with any other kind of cardio.

- Alternate walking and jogging for the first weeks.
- Use a bike, rower or brisk walk for extra sessions while your legs adapt.

Starting out · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Videbæk 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0333-8), [McDonald 2009q](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-2), [McDonald 2009w](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-methods-putting-it-together)

### Staying in the 'fat-burning zone' to lose more fat

**Easy effort burns a higher share of fat but fewer calories per minute, and fat burned over 24 hours was the same at 40% and 70% of VO2 max.**

The idea rests on fuel mix: at low effort a larger fraction of energy comes from fat. A fraction is not an amount. In a room-calorimeter study, 16 adults burned 400 kcal through exercise at 40 percent of VO2 max on one day and at 70 percent on another. Energy use over 24 hours rose equally on both days, and 24-hour fat oxidation did not differ between them or from a rest day. Lyle McDonald's advice for fat loss follows from this. Choose the effort that burns the most energy in the time you have while still letting you recover, which for steady sessions usually means the upper end of comfortable.

- Judge a cardio session by total energy burned and by how well you recover from it.

Cardio for fat loss · Beginner · Thin evidence · Sources: [Melanson 2002](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00706.2001), [McDonald 2009w](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-methods-putting-it-together)

### Picking the workout with the bigger afterburn percentage

**A larger percentage of a smaller session can be fewer calories. Longer steady sessions usually burn more in total than short interval sessions.**

The error is the same as the fat-burning zone: it compares shares and ignores totals. Take figures from the two ends of the range in the EPOC research. A 15-minute interval session averaging 12 kcal a minute burns 180 kcal, and a 15 percent afterburn adds 27. A 45-minute steady session at 9 kcal a minute burns 405 kcal, and a 6 percent afterburn adds 24. The totals are about 207 and 429 kcal. Lyle McDonald adds that an interval session averages its hard and easy minutes, so over the same length of time it burns about what a brisk steady session does. He puts typical gym cardio at only 5 to 10 kcal a minute.

- Compare sessions by total energy for the time you really have.

Calories, EPOC and efficiency · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [LaForgia 2006](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640410600552064), [McDonald 2008z](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/ss-vs-hiit-epoc-application), [McDonald 2008y](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/steady-state-and-hiit)

### Calling any 20-seconds-on, 10-seconds-off circuit a Tabata

**The original protocol was 7 to 8 exhaustive bouts at about 170% of VO2 max on a braked bike. Bodyweight circuits do not reach that work rate.**

In the 1996 study, seven subjects did seven to eight 20-second bouts at about 170 percent of VO2 max, each followed by 10 seconds of rest, five days a week for six weeks, on a mechanically braked cycle ergometer. VO2 max rose by 7 mL/kg/min and anaerobic capacity by 28 percent. A group that rode 60 minutes at 70 percent of VO2 max raised VO2 max from 53 to 58 mL/kg/min with no change in anaerobic capacity. Lyle McDonald's point is that the result belongs to that work rate. Squats or swings done to the same timer fall far below it. A bike also lets you reach exhaustion safely, which skilled movements do not.

- Use the 20/10 timer if you enjoy it, without expecting the study's results from light circuits.
- Do all-out intervals on a bike, rower or similar low-skill machine.

### Adding intervals on top of two or three hard leg sessions

**Intervals are another hard day for the legs. McDonald's fix is to cut leg lifting to maintenance or to stay with steady cardio.**

Lyle McDonald points out that interval studies test people who are not lifting and not dieting, usually at two or three sessions a week. Stacking that on heavy leg training during a calorie deficit is a different load. He notes that athletes in most sports give their legs only about two truly hard sessions a week. His options for a lifter who wants intervals: drop leg training to a maintenance dose by holding bar weight and cutting the sets to roughly a third, or keep the leg training and do steady cardio. With leg lifting cut back, two interval days plus one or two steady days is his usual ceiling.

- Count each interval session as a leg session when you plan the week.
- Keep bar weight and cut sets if you make room for intervals.

Cardio with lifting · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2008y](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/steady-state-and-hiit), [McDonald 2008aa](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/steady-state-vs-intervals-summary)

### Thinking a night's sleep empties muscle glycogen

**An overnight fast lowers liver glycogen and insulin. Muscle glycogen barely moves, so it does not explain the higher fat use in fasted cardio.**

A common explanation for fasted cardio is that muscles wake up short of glycogen. Lyle McDonald calls this wrong. The overnight fall is small, and it takes days of carbohydrate restriction to lower muscle glycogen by much. What does change overnight is the liver's store and the hormonal picture. Insulin is low, fat cells release more fatty acids, and with more of them in the blood, muscle burns a higher share of fat during easy and moderate exercise. A meta-analysis of 27 studies confirms higher fat oxidation when aerobic exercise is done fasted. Whether that changes fat loss over weeks is a separate question.

- Do not skip carbohydrate the evening before in the hope of a bigger effect.

Fasted cardio · Intermediate · Thin evidence · Sources: [Vieira 2016](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114516003160), [McDonald 2024](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/is-fasted-cardio-useless-for-fat-loss)

### Dropping steady cardio because you 'get efficient' at it

**Efficiency changes little and slowly. When a session feels easier you are fitter, so raise the speed, incline or resistance.**

The claim is that the body adapts to steady cardio and soon burns far fewer calories. Lyle McDonald gives two replies. Lifters add load when a lift gets easier, and cardio can be progressed the same way. And efficiency sits near 20 to 25 percent in cycling, so even the full span would change the energy cost of a set workload by about a fifth. A review concluded that training does improve gross efficiency in well-controlled studies. McDonald's point is the scale: such changes take years of heavy training. A lower heart rate at the same speed after a few weeks shows fitness, with the energy cost hardly changed.

- Raise speed, incline or resistance when your usual session starts to feel easy.

Calories, EPOC and efficiency · Beginner · Thin evidence · Sources: [Hopker 2009](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0029-1237712), [McDonald 2008m](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/exercise-efficiency)

### Rewarding a workout with more food than it burned

**People often credit a moderate hour with 1,000 kcal when 400 to 500 is nearer the mark, and one snack can cancel a session.**

Lyle McDonald describes a common trap. Someone guesses that an hour of moderate exercise burned about 1,000 kcal, decides a large meal has been earned, and eats more than the session used. His own estimate for that hour is 400 to 500 kcal. The figure on a machine also includes the 60 to 100 kcal you would have burned in that hour at rest, so the extra burn is lower still. Wearables do not settle it: a meta-analysis of 60 studies found that the accuracy of activity monitors for energy expenditure varies widely with the device and the activity.

- Assume a session burned less than the display says.
- Keep meals on plan on training days unless your weight trend says otherwise.

Cardio for fat loss · Beginner · Coaching practice · Sources: [O’Driscoll 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099643), [McDonald 2010d](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/exercise-weight-and-fat-loss)

### Running all-out sprints with no preparation

**Maximal sprinting is a skill with a real injury cost. Hamstring strains cluster where sprint intensity rises quickly.**

Lyle McDonald warns lifters against going to a track, jogging for a few minutes and running 100 m flat out. In his experience that ends within a few repeats in a pulled hamstring and weeks off, and he reports the same outcome in physique athletes who added sprints. Sprinters warm up at length, build speed over several runs and spend years learning to sprint well. Data from competitive sprinters point the same way: in one report cited by a review of sprint training, two-thirds of hamstring injuries came in the weeks when volume fell and sprint speed rose before the competition season.

- Build up over weeks with sub-maximal runs before any all-out sprint.
- Use a bike or a hill for hard efforts if you have not trained to sprint.

Sprint and speed work · Beginner · Thin evidence · Sources: [Haugen 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-019-0221-0), [McDonald 2016q](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/what-is-sprint-training)

### Choosing sprints over steady cardio to look like a sprinter

**Sport selects body types, and muscular athletes lift. The kind of cardio you do adds little muscle either way.**

The argument compares a muscular sprinter with a thin marathon runner and credits the difference to their running. Lyle McDonald replies that elite sport selects people already built for it, that sprinters lift heavy weights and most distance runners do not, and that distance athletes keep body weight low on purpose. The comparison also skips the middle: an hour of cardio a day is still several times less than an elite distance runner does. In a meta-analysis of 21 studies, the effect size for muscle growth was 1.23 with strength training and 0.27 with endurance training alone.

- Lift to build muscle, and pick cardio for fitness, energy use and recovery.

Sprint and speed work · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e31823a3e2d), [McDonald 2016m](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/sprinters-endurance-athletes-again)

### Going from no cardio to a lot of hard cardio when a diet starts

**McDonald blames this jump, with a large calorie cut on top, for much of the muscle loss people attribute to cardio.**

Many lifters do no cardio at all in the off-season and then begin a diet with long or hard sessions from the first week, alongside a steep cut in calories. Lyle McDonald sees three problems arriving at once: a body that is out of practice at using fat for fuel, a sudden training load it has not adapted to, and less food to recover with. Muscle loss under those conditions gets blamed on cardio as such. His fix is to keep a small amount of easy cardio through the gaining phase and to add volume gradually once the diet begins, in step with how the body responds.

- Keep a base of easy cardio year-round.
- Add cardio in small steps across the first weeks of a diet.

Cardio with lifting · Intermediate · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2009g](https://bodyrecomposition.com/muscle-gain/cardio-for-mass-gains)

### Making a beginner's first session as hard as possible

**Leaving exhausted and waking up too sore to move teaches a reluctant beginner that exercise is miserable. Many do not return.**

Lyle McDonald blames two habits. Trainers who always liked training forget what starting was like and want to show what they know, and some clients ask to be pushed because they assume harder is better. For an unfit person the result is exhaustion, a sense of failure and severe soreness the next day. Research on how exercise feels agrees: responses above the ventilatory threshold are uniformly negative. He adds that highly motivated people will put up with a brutal start and most heavy beginners will not. Any amount above nothing improves fitness at this stage, so the risk buys nothing.

- End early sessions while the person still feels good.
- Avoid new soreness in the first weeks.

### Giving a heavy beginner bodyweight circuits

**A push-up, burpee or lunge asks someone to move most of a large body weight. Machines and light dumbbells let the load start small.**

Bodyweight exercises scale with the person's weight, so they are hardest for the people most often given them for fat loss. Lyle McDonald estimates that even a push-up from the knees means pressing roughly two-thirds of body weight, more than most untrained heavy people can lift for repetitions. Burpees, walking lunges and single-leg balance drills go the same way: done badly, in public, by someone already self-conscious. He begins with simple machine or dumbbell movements, where the load can be a small fraction of body weight and rise gradually, and he avoids making people get down to the floor and up again.

- Start with machines or light dumbbells and raise the load in small steps.
- Bring in bodyweight and free-weight movements once strength and confidence have grown.

Starting out · Beginner · Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2014x](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/training-the-obese-beginner-6)

### Filling a heavy beginner's sessions with lifting to 'raise metabolism'

**Resting muscle uses about 13 kcal per kg (6 per lb) a day, so added muscle changes daily energy use very little.**

The usual reasoning is that more muscle means a faster metabolism. The effect is small. In 131 healthy, non-obese adults, the standard figure of about 13 kcal per kg of skeletal muscle a day held up in those aged 21 to 50, against 4.5 for fat tissue. Five kilograms (11 lb) of new muscle, months of work for a beginner, would add roughly 65 kcal a day. Lyle McDonald also notes that people gain lean mass along with fat as they become obese, some of it leg muscle from carrying the weight. He still wants some strength training from day one, and gives most of the remaining time to activity that burns more energy.

- Keep strength work brief and give most session time to cardio and walking.

Calories, EPOC and efficiency · Beginner · Some support · Sources: [Wang 2010](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.2010.29885), [McDonald 2014t](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/training-the-obese-beginner-2)

### Opening with calipers and maximum tests

**Skinfolds are unreliable and often embarrassing at high body fat, and a maximal test contradicts an easy start. A scale and a tape are enough.**

Lyle McDonald dropped most first-day testing as he gained experience. At high body fat a clean skinfold is hard to take, the reading adds little, and the process can humiliate someone who is already uneasy. Maximal strength tests make no sense when the plan is to start well below maximum, and beginners improve by the second session from practice alone. He kept the intake questions about injuries, health and eating, a scale weight, and sometimes tape measurements to show change after six to eight weeks. How clothes fit tells much the same story.

- Record weight and, if the person is comfortable, a few tape measurements.
- Leave fitness and strength testing until after the first training block.

## Four ways to structure a steady indoor session

Structures McDonald describes for making indoor cardio pass faster while adding a training effect. The example numbers are illustrations.

| Structure | What changes | Example | Best used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timed pick-ups | A short faster stretch at fixed times | 20 seconds quicker every 6 minutes | Breaking up easy sessions |
| Alternating levels | Minutes at the next machine level, then back down | 8 minutes at the current level, 4 at the next | Moving up when one level is too big a jump |
| Three steps | Effort rises once per third of the session | Easy, moderate, then upper-aerobic thirds | Mixing volume with some harder work |

## Before you add hard intervals

Checks drawn from McDonald's articles on interval training. If several fail, stay with steady work for now.

**Base**

- At least 4 to 6 weeks of regular steady cardio behind you
- You can keep going for 30 minutes or more without stopping
- Short, slightly faster pick-ups already feel manageable

**Week plan**

- No more than two interval sessions a week
- Each one counted as a hard leg day alongside lifting
- Leg lifting reduced to make room
- Fewer or none during a hard diet or on very low carbohydrate
- Planned as a block of a few weeks, then back to steady work

**The session**

- A low-impact machine such as a bike or rower, above all if you are heavy or new
- A thorough warm-up first
- Short bouts set by pace, power or effort, not heart rate
- First repeat paced so the last can match it
- Session ends when pace or power falls by about a tenth

Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2009u](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-4-part-1), [McDonald 2009v](https://bodyrecomposition.com/training/endurance-training-method-4-part-2), [McDonald 2008y](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/steady-state-and-hiit)

## First month for a very unfit beginner

A sequence based on McDonald's approach for heavy, untrained beginners. Each step waits until the one before feels routine.

**Before the first session**

- See a clinician first if you have a heart condition, chest pain, dizziness or joint problems
- Choose an activity you already know, such as walking
- Fix days and times you can keep

**Weeks 1 and 2**

- Walk at an effort of 3 to 4 out of 10 for as long as is comfortable
- Add one or two minutes each session
- Do one light set on a few simple strength machines
- Finish each session feeling you could have done more

**Weeks 3 and 4**

- Reach 20 continuous minutes, three times a week
- Split sessions into shorter bouts if you need to
- Raise strength loads a little when 12 repetitions feel easy
- Note one everyday task that has become easier

**After week 4**

- Add a minute of slightly harder effort every five minutes
- Lengthen the harder parts over the following weeks
- Leave formal intervals until about week 8 or later

Coaching practice · Sources: [McDonald 2014v](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/training-the-obese-beginner-4), [McDonald 2014w](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/training-the-obese-beginner-5), [McDonald 2014x](https://bodyrecomposition.com/fat-loss/training-the-obese-beginner-6)

## Terms

- **Aerobic intervals**: Easy exercise broken into short bouts with rests, for people who cannot yet keep going for 20 minutes. For example, 5 easy minutes, a short break, then repeat. Over the weeks the bouts get longer and the rests shorter.
- **EPOC (the afterburn)**: Excess post-exercise oxygen consumption: the extra oxygen, and so energy, your body uses after a session while it recovers. It rises with intensity and duration, yet stays a small fraction of what the session itself cost.
- **Exercise efficiency**: The share of the energy you burn that becomes external work, with the rest lost as heat. McDonald puts it at about 20 to 25 percent in cycling. Higher efficiency means more speed or power for the same oxygen cost.
- **Fartlek**: Swedish for speed play: an easy session with short, unplanned faster stretches. McDonald suggests a timed version indoors, such as a brief pick-up every few minutes that is quicker than cruising pace without being a sprint.
- **Functional threshold**: The hardest pace or power you can hold steadily for about an hour. Lab names for nearby points include maximal lactate steady state and the second lactate threshold. Above it, fatigue builds within minutes.
- **Metabolic flexibility**: The ability to switch between burning carbohydrate and burning fat as supply and demand change. McDonald notes it is often reduced with obesity and inactivity, and that regular training helps restore it.
- **Polarized and pyramidal training**: Polarized training puts most sessions at easy effort and a small share at hard interval effort, with little in between. Pyramidal training also starts from a large easy base but keeps more work in the middle zone than at the top.
- **Sports continuum**: Lyle McDonald's way of placing sports on a line from pure strength and power, through mixed sports such as team and combat sports, to pure endurance. Where a sport sits sets its main training, the adaptations it seeks and its nutrition.
- **Strides**: Short, relaxed accelerations of about 50 to 100 m, or around 20 seconds, at faster than race pace, each followed by a minute or more of rest. Runners do 5 to 10 to practise speed and good form without building fatigue.
- **Sweet spot training**: Andrew Coggan's name for steady work between tempo and threshold effort, usually done in blocks of 10 to 20 minutes with rests half as long. It gives a large aerobic stimulus for the time and is too demanding for beginners.
- **Tempo (intensive endurance)**: Steady work in the upper aerobic range: clearly harder than easy pace, yet still a steady state that you could hold for an hour or two. Cyclists call it tempo. Sprint coaches use the same word for sub-maximal repeat runs.
