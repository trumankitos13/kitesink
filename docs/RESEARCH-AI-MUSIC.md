# AI and music rights: verified research digest

Deep-research run of 2026-11-20 (113 agents, 25 claims verified by 3-vote review, 22 kept). Feeds the ai-rights tracker and the batch 7 reading list. Every finding below rests on the sources listed with it; anything marked by the reviewers as secondary or self-reported says so in its evidence note.

## Summary

As of 20 November 2026, the verified evidence covers four areas: US Copyright Office AI policy, the federal NO FAKES Act, two lawsuits (Concord v Anthropic and GEMA v OpenAI), and EU and UK policy. Almost all of it rests on primary sources. The Copyright Office has published final versions of Part 1 (digital replicas, 31 July 2024) and Part 2 (copyrightability, 29 January 2025). Part 3 (generative AI training) is still only the 9 May 2025 pre-publication version. That version treats training an audio model to generate new sound recordings as at best modestly transformative, and it recommends against licensing intervention for now. The registration guidance from March 2023 still applies: only human authorship is protected, and AI material beyond a trivial amount must be disclosed and excluded. The federal NO FAKES Act is S. 4591, introduced 20 May 2026 and approved unanimously by Senate Judiciary on 18 June 2026. It was reported on 24 June 2026 as Calendar No. 446, with House companion H.R. 8915. No floor passage was found. On litigation, the Munich Regional Court ruled against OpenAI on 11 November 2025 (42 O 14139/24), finding that lyrics memorised in the model infringe and that the text and data mining exception does not cover this. In Concord v Anthropic, the publishers dismissed their contributory and vicarious infringement claims with prejudice in April 2026. In the EU, Article 50 transparency duties apply from 2 August 2026, supported by a final Code of Practice on marking and labelling published 10 June 2026. The UK's March 2026 report dropped the broad text and data mining exception with opt-out as its preferred option, set no legislative timetable, and proposed removing s.9(3) CDPA. None of the verified claims addresses DSP policies, PRO/MLC registration policies, Suno/Udio, state voice laws, licensing deals or AI tools, so the ai-tools page and large parts of the ai-rights page still need research.

## Verified findings

### 1. (high; vote 3-0 (x4 merged))

US Copyright Office AI report status: Part 1 (digital replicas) was published 31 July 2024, and its announcement recommended a federal digital replica law. Part 2 (copyrightability of generative AI outputs) was published 29 January 2025. Part 3 (generative AI training) exists only as a pre-publication version released 9 May 2025. The Office says a final version will follow 'without any substantive changes expected in the analysis or conclusions', but as of 20 Nov 2026 no final Part 3 has been published.

Evidence: Merges claims 0, 1, 2 and 3, all voted 3-0. The verifier fetched the copyright.gov/ai hub on 2026-11-20. It still says 'A final version of Part 3 will be published in the future', labels the only Part 3 link 'Pre-publication', and its latest dated item is 9 May 2025. The Part 1 and Part 2 dates are quoted word for word from the hub and match the Office's NewsNet releases. Context: Part 3 came out the day after Librarian Hayden was fired and just before Register Perlmutter was removed. A July 2026 law-firm alert (secondary source) links formal publication of Part 3 to Blanche v. Perlmutter (S. Ct. No. 25A478, order of 30 June 2026). The verifiers did not check that docket.

Sources: https://copyright.gov/ai/, https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf

### 2. (high; vote 3-0 (x3 merged))

What Part 3 (pre-publication) says on music and training: several stages of AI development are prima facie infringement, and fair use is the key question. Commercial use of copyrighted works to produce expressive content that competes with them, especially through illegal access, goes beyond established fair use boundaries. The report names music directly: training an audio model on sound recordings to generate new sound recordings 'aims to occupy the same space in the market for music' and is at best modestly transformative. Removing distortion from recordings is given as a more transformative use. On licensing, the Office recommends letting voluntary licensing develop without government intervention, says ECL should be considered only where market failures are shown, and notes the significant disadvantages of compulsory licensing.

Evidence: Merges claims 4, 5 and 6, all voted 3-0. The verifiers checked the quotes against PDF text extracted with pdftotext: the Conclusion section, the factor-one discussion and Section V at p.106. The page should also give the Office's balancing language: no prejudging of individual cases, research and analysis uses are likely fair, and guardrails can affect the assessment. 'Does not recommend compulsory licensing' is an inference from p.104, not the report's own wording. The report is the agency's view, not binding law, and it is still a pre-publication version.

Sources: https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf

### 3. (high; vote 3-0 (x3 merged))

US registration guidance for works with AI material (88 FR 16190, 16 March 2023) still applies. Copyright protects only human-authored material, and AI-determined expressive elements are not protected. A work combining human and AI material, or a human selection and arrangement of AI material, can be registered, but protection covers only the human contribution (example: Zarya of the Dawn). Applicants have a duty to disclose AI content. AI material that is more than de minimis 'should be' excluded, which 'may be done' under Limitation of the Claim > Material Excluded > Other, with optional details in the Note to CO field. Works with AI material must use the Standard Application, not the Single Application. Existing registrations can be corrected through a supplementary registration.

Evidence: Merges claims 11, 12 and 13, all voted 3-0. Federal Register pages redirected, so the verifiers checked the copyright.gov and govinfo PDF copies. The rule has been reaffirmed since: by the Part 2 report (January 2025, prompts alone are insufficient), by Thaler v. Perlmutter (D.C. Cir., March 2025), and by the Supreme Court's denial of cert on 2 March 2026 (No. 25-449, per supremecourt.gov docket and law-firm reports). No 2026 revision of the guidance was found. Allen v. Perlmutter (D. Colo.) is still pending. Wording note: the guidance says 'should' and 'may', not 'must'. The guidance applies to all classes of work; it does not name music specifically.

Sources: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/03/16/2023-05321/copyright-registration-guidance-works-containing-material-generated-by-artificial-intelligence, https://copyright.gov/ai/ai_policy_guidance.pdf

### 4. (high; vote 3-0 (x3 merged))

NO FAKES Act: the current bill is S. 4591 in the 119th Congress, 'NO FAKES Act of 2026', introduced by Sen. Coons on 20 May 2026 with 15 cosponsors including Blackburn and Klobuchar. The House companion is H.R. 8915. The Senate Judiciary Committee approved it unanimously by voice vote on 18 June 2026. Sen. Grassley formally reported it with an amendment on 24 June 2026, and it was placed on the calendar as Calendar No. 446. No evidence was found that either chamber has passed it as of November 2026. The May 2026 revision adds a DMCA-style counter-notice procedure (at least 14 days before content is restored, per Manatt), an exemption for libraries, archives and research institutions, and technical fixes for streaming music platforms.

Evidence: Merges claims 7, 9 and 10, all voted 3-0. The govinfo reported print confirms the bill number, sponsor, dates and Calendar No. 446. Blackburn's 18 June 2026 release confirms the unanimous committee vote and the revisions; it is primary but promotional in tone. H.R. 8915, the voice vote and the counter-notice detail come from secondary sources (Salazar release, Holland & Knight, IPWatchdog, Manatt, Grammy.com). congress.gov returned HTTP 403, so floor actions after June could not be checked. One weak source reports a unanimous-consent request on 30 Sept 2026 with no known outcome.

Sources: https://govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s4591rs/xhtml/BILLS-119s4591rs.html, https://www.blackburn.senate.gov/2026/6/blackburn-coons-bipartisan-bill-to-protect-individuals-and-creators-from-deepfakes-passes-senate-judiciary-committee

### 5. (high; vote 3-0)

Under S. 4591, a 'digital replica' is a newly created, computer-generated, highly realistic electronic representation readily identifiable as a person's voice or visual likeness. The bill creates a federal property right in voice and visual likeness. The definition excludes reproduction, sampling, remixing, mastering and remastering of existing sound recordings or audiovisual works when the copyright holder has authorised it.

Evidence: Claim 8, voted 3-0, checked against §2(a)(2)(A)-(B) and §2(b)(2)(A)(i)(I) of the reported text. A related claim that the bill preempts future state laws such as the ELVIS Act and the California and New York laws was refuted 0-3, so the page should not describe preemption from the sponsor's release.

Sources: https://govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s4591rs/xhtml/BILLS-119s4591rs.html

### 6. (medium; vote 3-0)

In Concord Music Group v. Anthropic PBC (N.D. Cal., No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK, Judge Eumi K. Lee), the publishers on 20 April 2026 filed a Notice of Voluntary Dismissal of Counts II and III (contributory and vicarious infringement) with prejudice (Dkt. 685). In its summary judgment brief filed the same day (Dkt. 686), Anthropic asked for entry of judgment on those claims, saying the publishers had refused to agree to judgment or to bar relitigation. Cross-motions were set for hearing on 15 July 2026. No ruling was found.

Evidence: Claim 14 was voted 3-0, and the verifier also read Dkt. 685. The account of what happened comes from Anthropic's advocacy brief, which should be cited as 'according to Anthropic'. A separate claim presenting Dkt. 686 as the docket's current status was refused 1-2. This is an April 2026 step, and outcomes after the July 2026 hearing are unknown, so it should not be shown as the latest step without checking the docket. Confidence is medium for that reason.

Sources: https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.431519/gov.uscourts.cand.431519.686.0.pdf

### 7. (high; vote 3-0 (x2 merged))

In GEMA v OpenAI, the Munich Regional Court I (42nd civil chamber, docket 42 O 14139/24) ruled on 11 November 2025 that ChatGPT infringes copyright. The models memorised lyrics from GEMA's repertoire (reproduction under §16 UrhG) and output them in response to simple prompts (making available to the public). The text and data mining exception (§44b UrhG) does not justify storing or outputting the lyrics. OpenAI is not a privileged research organisation and should have licensed the lyrics for training and operating ChatGPT, paying appropriate remuneration. Claims for an injunction, information and damages were largely granted. A personality-rights claim was dismissed. The case covered nine song texts, and there was no referral to the CJEU.

Evidence: Merges claims 15 and 16, both voted 3-0. GEMA's press release is a party's account, so the holding should be attributed to GEMA. Independent sources give the docket number and match the substance: CMS, Taylor Wessing, Osborne Clarke, Bird & Bird, Heuking, LTO and IRIS Merlin. One outlet gives 3 Nov 2025 as the date. The judgment is not final. OpenAI said it was considering an appeal, but no confirmed OLG München appeal docket was found. Cite the court's own judgment when it is available.

Sources: https://www.gema.de/en/w/landmark-ruling-gema-against-openai

### 8. (medium; vote 3-0 (x2 merged))

EU AI Act transparency: the Commission published the final Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content on 10 June 2026 (page updated 12 June). The Code is voluntary, and the Commission and AI Board were still assessing whether it is adequate. The Article 50 transparency obligations it supports apply from 2 August 2026. Law-firm reports say the AI Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, OJ 24 July 2026, in force 27 July 2026) gives systems already on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 to meet the Article 50(2) machine-readable marking duty for synthetic audio and other content. This would cover existing music generators.

Evidence: Merges claims 17 (3-0, high) and 18 (3-0, medium). The Code date and the 2 August 2026 application date come from the primary Commission page, which is corroborated by press release IP/26/1328 and law firms. The 2 December 2026 grace period, the Regulation number and the reported finalisation of the Art. 50 Guidelines on 20 July 2026 come only from law-firm summaries (Orrick, Cuatrecasas, Lewis Silkin, Ropes & Gray). Before publishing, cite the Official Journal text of 2026/1744. The GPAI copyright policy and training-data summary duties under Article 53 were not verified.

Sources: https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-code-practice-marking-and-labelling-ai-generated-content

### 9. (high; vote 3-0 (x3 merged))

UK: the government's Report on Copyright and AI (18 March 2026, laid under s.136 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025) drops the broad text and data mining exception with opt-out as its preferred option and names no replacement. It commits to gathering more evidence and says it will not reform copyright law 'until we are confident'. It sets no legislative timetable, proposes no intervention in the licensing market, and will monitor technology, litigation, international approaches and licensing. It proposes removing protection for wholly computer-generated works (s.9(3) CDPA) unless evidence of ongoing value emerges, while keeping protection for AI-assisted works. It notes that AI-generated music may still be protected as sound recordings.

Evidence: Merges claims 19, 20 and 21, all voted 3-0 against the gov.uk page. Corroborated by Lewis Silkin, Reed Smith, Osborne Clarke, Hogan Lovells, DLA Piper, Simmons & Simmons, Pinsent Masons and RPC. No AI bill appeared in the 13 May 2026 King's Speech (Press Gazette), and FT-sourced reports say the bill slipped to 2027. Nothing found from April to November 2026 supersedes the report. Nuance: the opt-out approach was dropped as the preferred option but not formally ruled out. The report also plans transparency best practice and to explore a personality or digital replica right.

Sources: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/report-and-impact-assessment-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence/report-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence

## Refuted (do not use)

- As described by the sponsor, the bill would preempt future state laws on digital replicas. That would affect how state laws such as Tennessee's ELVIS Act and the California and New York laws interact with any federal law. (0-3; https://www.blackburn.senate.gov/2026/6/blackburn-coons-bipartisan-bill-to-protect-individuals-and-creators-from-deepfakes-passes-senate-judiciary-committee)
- Docket entry 686 in Concord Music Group, Inc. v. Anthropic PBC (N.D. Cal., San Jose Division, Case No. 5:24-cv-03811-EKL-SVK, Judge Eumi K. Lee) is Anthropic's motion for summary judgment and opposition to the publishers' motion for partial summary judgment. It was filed on April 20, 2026 (public redacted version), with a hearing set for July 15, 2026. This filing is the defendant's advocacy, not a ruling. As of late November 2026 it is seven months old, so the docket should be checked for a later decision or hearing outcome. (1-2; https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.431519/gov.uscourts.cand.431519.686.0.pdf)
- A separate GEMA lawsuit against Suno Inc. is pending at the Munich Regional Court. GEMA alleges that Suno trained on original recordings of GEMA-repertoire works and outputs misleadingly similar versions, and the page says a hearing is expected on 26 January 2026. (0-3; https://www.gema.de/en/w/landmark-ruling-gema-against-openai)

## Not covered by this run

Coverage is uneven. The 22 surviving claims cover only scope items 1, 2 (federal only), 3 (partly) and 5 (partly). Nothing verified covers state voice and likeness laws (ELVIS Act, California, New York), labels v Suno/Udio and their settlements or deals, GEMA v Suno, licensing deals (GEMA, SACEM and STIM models; label and publisher deals with AI firms), EU GPAI copyright and training-summary duties or the GPAI code, DSP and distributor policies, PRO/MLC/society registration policies, DDEX AI fields, or any AI tools. The 'ai-tools' page has no verified content at all. Two claims were refuted and must not be used: (1) that NO FAKES preempts future state laws (0-3), and (2) that GEMA v Suno had a hearing expected on 26 Jan 2026 (0-3). An unverified search summary suggests a first-instance GEMA v Suno ruling on 31 Jul 2026 and an OLG appeal numbered 6 U 2314/26 e. Treat that as unconfirmed. Time-sensitive points: congress.gov could not be reached (HTTP 403), so floor action on S. 4591 after June 2026 is unconfirmed. The latest verified step in Concord v Anthropic is from April 2026, and the outcome of the July 2026 hearing is unknown. Whether the GEMA v OpenAI appeal went ahead is unknown. The publication of Part 3 may depend on the Perlmutter removal litigation. Source quality: the Blackburn release and GEMA's page are party or sponsor accounts. Dkt. 686 is Anthropic's advocacy brief. The details of the EU AI Omnibus and Art. 50 grace period rest only on law-firm summaries. The three uploaded PDFs (a CMO report, the ISWC Net user guide and the Music Business Association metadata style guide) were not reflected in any verified claim.
